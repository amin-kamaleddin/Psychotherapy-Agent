# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

---

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 5
**Comments:** The psychiatrist consistently communicates unconditional positive regard, validating both doubt and resolve as legitimate ('Both your doubt and your resolve are welcome here'). Even confessions about wanting to lose weight or peeking at the scale are met without judgment, reframed as diet-culture conditioning rather than personal failure.

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 5
**Comments:** Reflective listening is highly accurate and emotionally attuned, with complex reflections capturing both content and feeling (e.g., 'worn out by structured diets... still here, wondering'). The clinician also mirrors meaning beneath the surface, reflecting 'what peace feels like' and 'not at war with me' back to the patient.

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 5
**Comments:** The session maintains clear, consistent momentum from identifying what to change, to concrete first steps, success markers, a chosen focus (energy), maintenance strategies, and deeper values-based motivation. Each response advances the arc without rushing while keeping the client in the driver's seat.

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 5
**Comments:** The interview operates as a genuine exchange between equals, with 'we' language and co-creation at every step. Yasmin's own ideas (gym walk-home check-in, phone notes, 'no streaks, no scores') visibly shaped the entire plan.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 5
**Comments:** Autonomy is actively reinforced throughout — 'signs you'd choose to look for,' 'which feels most alive for you right now?' — with no advice-giving, persuasion, or pressure. The clinician explicitly contrasts the collaborative approach with imposed diets, and the patient's ownership of the change process remains entirely her own.

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 5
**Comments:** The clinician consistently draws motivation out from the client rather than instilling it, culminating in powerful self-generated change talk ('I want my life back'). The question 'What do you think your body might show you first, if the war actually paused?' is an exemplary evocative question.

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 5
**Comments:** The psychiatrist explicitly and repeatedly positions Yasmin as the expert on her own life, with nearly every question framed as 'what do you think' or 'in your eyes.' The session's plan originated entirely from her contributions, demonstrating textbook power-sharing.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 5
**Comments:** This is the session's greatest strength — skillful open, forward-oriented questions elicit rich client-generated motivation ('Small proof first. That feels doable,' 'I want my life back'). Change talk is then reinforced with affirmations and deepened through strategic follow-up questions.

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Sustain talk (scale attachment, desire to lose weight, 'my old brain will scream it's pointless') is acknowledged without reinforcement, using effective double-sided reflections and gentle pivots to change. Growth opportunity: the patient's attachment to weight loss remains intact at session's end, and more deliberate discrepancy work could help actively resolve rather than park that ambivalence.
