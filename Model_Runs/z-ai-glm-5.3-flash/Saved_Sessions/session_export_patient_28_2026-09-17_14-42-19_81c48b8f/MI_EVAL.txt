# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

---

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist maintained a warm, nonjudgmental stance with affirmations like 'That sounds honest, doable, and kind.' However, there was little explicit normalization of the patient's repeated history of quitting, which would characterize a higher score.

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Strong reflective listening early on accurately captured both the patient's history and frustration. Empathy narrowed later into planning questions, with emotional nuances like shame about skipping acknowledged only briefly rather than deeply explored.

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 5
**Comments:** The session maintained an exceptionally clear focus on re-establishing exercise, with every turn advancing toward a concrete target. No topic drift occurred, and the conversation steadily progressed toward an actionable, specific plan.

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** While the tone was collaborative, the counselor frequently fired multiple questions in a single turn, making the exchange feel more like an assessment than dialogue. The patient explicitly flagged this imbalance ('That's a lot of questions') and it was not adjusted.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The counselor repeatedly emphasized the patient's choice and the plan was entirely self-generated. Some turns were subtly directive, offering menus of options rather than purely evoking, which prevented a higher score.

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Motivation was largely drawn from the patient himself, including family reasons, confidence ratings, and his own solutions. A significant lapse occurred when the patient asked for advice and was given a counselor-generated list instead of his own ideas being evoked first.

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The counselor invited the patient's input but several turns bundled 4-6 questions at once, shifting the expert role to the counselor. The most striking missed opportunity was answering the patient's direct request for advice prescriptively rather than evoking his own ideas.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Change talk was effectively evoked through questions about values ('I want to be around for my family'), confidence scaling (moving from 5 to 7), and having the patient articulate his own plan and commitment. Evocation was heavily question-stacked and some change talk moments were not deepened.

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The counselor did not reinforce sustain talk but provided little active softening of the patient's doubt ('I'm always the guy who quits'). These statements were skipped past rather than met with double-sided reflections to strategically resolve ambivalence.
