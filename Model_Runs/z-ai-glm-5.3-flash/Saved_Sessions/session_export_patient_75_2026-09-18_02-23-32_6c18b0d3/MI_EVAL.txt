# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

---

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The clinician maintains a warm, nonjudgmental stance with consistent validation of Linda's fears and ambivalence. However, affirmations occasionally feel formulaic (e.g., 'Both make real sense' repeated as a template), and her history of self-managing without medical guidance was not explicitly normalized.

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Reflective listening is strong and accurate, including the opening double-sided reflection capturing her core ambivalence. Reflections occasionally summarize content rather than deepen into her emotional core, such as the fear of ending up like her sister-in-law or feeling like 'asthma just happens to me.'

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The session maintains a clear focus on asthma self-management and builds coherently toward a concrete, achievable plan. A significant lapse occurred when Linda proposed reducing her daily controller inhaler without the clinician proactively flagging that medication changes warrant her physician's involvement—the client discovered this on her own.

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 5
**Comments:** The interaction functions as a genuine partnership between equals, with the clinician framing everything as joint inquiry and building each step on Linda's prior answers. The resulting plan, including how to approach her doctor, is entirely co-constructed and owned by the client.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 5
**Comments:** Autonomy support is exemplary—the clinician explicitly decouples tracking from decision-making ('no decisions yet') and reinforces Linda's ownership of the pace and direction of change. No pressure, persuasion, or directives appear anywhere; the clinician only asks what Linda wants to do.

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 5
**Comments:** Every substantive idea—tracking, phone logging, evening reminders, husband involvement—came from Linda via strategic open questions, then was connected to her intrinsic motivations of self-control and having 'some say.' The result is authentic, client-generated commitment language rather than clinician-persuaded agreement.

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 5
**Comments:** The clinician consistently treats Linda as the expert in her own life, with all key plan elements originating from her and affirmations explicitly attributing insight and agency to her. The expert role is never asserted, even around medication decisions.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 5
**Comments:** This is the session's greatest strength—skilled open questions consistently elicit sustained commitment language ('I could probably start today,' 'I think I'm actually going to do this'), which the clinician then reflects and amplifies. The evocation of Linda's intrinsic reasons is textbook-quality MI.

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Linda's substantial sustain talk (fear of attacks, fear the numbers will be worse, her sister-in-law's story) is acknowledged accurately without amplification, then redirected toward capability and agency, including the reframe that 'missing a day isn't failing.' However, the clinician mostly reflects sustain talk neutrally rather than deliberately reframing it into change-oriented language.
