# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

---

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 5
**Comments:** Communicated unconditional positive regard throughout, explicitly stating 'I'll listen without judgment' and never moralizing despite the client's minimization. The client directly confirmed feeling accepted: 'thanks for not lecturing me... everyone else treats it like some moral thing.'

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Used accurate double-sided reflections capturing both the enjoyable, controlled side of use and the infection's downside. Missed opportunities to reflect deeper emotional material, such as Adrian's shame about not telling anyone close or fear of his medical record.

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Kept the session anchored to meth use and its risks, following the client's preference for harm reduction and closing with concrete next steps. Deferred when Adrian opened the door to discussing his use itself ('maybe down the track') rather than anchoring it as a future focus with a plan.

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Functioned largely between equals through menu offerings, asking what would help, and adapting to privacy concerns with discreet options. Collaboration was uneven, with the counselor often shifting into directive expert mode (bulleted lists, scripted language) and the client shaping logistics more than substance.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Consistently supported autonomy ('support whatever you choose'), accepting the client's decision not to change use itself without pressure. Advice-heavy responses and a persuasive rather than decision-framed correction about naloxone slightly undermined this stance.

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Some motivation drawn from within via the confidence rating and the client's own plan ideas, but the default was providing information and scripts rather than eliciting the client's reasons. Rich seeds like 'not knowing what's in it has been sitting with me' were never explored.

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Offered collaborative menus and repeatedly asked 'which feels most doable?', but frequently slipped into an expert stance, e.g., delivering a six-bullet harm-reduction list. Did not draw on the client's existing knowledge or invite his ideas before providing information.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Some change talk was evoked and reflected (ordering the kit, 6/6.5 confidence, 'sort the kit out before Saturday'), and the confidence scale was well used. The most promising change talk—concern about not knowing what's in the drug—was never deepened, and no desire/need/reason language was elicited beyond harm-reduction steps.

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Never argued against or reinforced sustain talk, and acknowledged ambivalence with double-sided reflections. However, statements like 'I'm still gonna use, it's fun, it's our thing' passed without strategic reflection or pairing with his expressed concerns, leaving ambivalence unresolved.
