# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

---

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The counselor maintained a respectful, nonjudgmental tone throughout, notably telling Nabil 'No need to apologize' and opening with a safe, nonjudgmental framing. However, warmth was largely procedural — options were offered quickly rather than affirming Nabil's individual strengths, such as his 15-year work record, which was never acknowledged.

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Early reflections were accurate ('You sound angry and unfairly treated'), but reflections later became formulaic preambles immediately followed by option lists. The counselor never explored the emotional core Nabil raised — his job fears, worry about hypervigilance, and discomfort with being sent — and his vulnerable moment ('Maybe that's daft') received no emotional reflection.

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The session stayed connected to the referral concern of anger at appointments and job consequences. However, focus drifted into document production (email wording, CC decisions, printable versions) rather than the behavioral target of anger management, and the session ended as a logistics exercise without commitment or a change-relevant summary.

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The tone was cooperative and Nabil's preferences did shape the deliverables (he edited the email wording, which the counselor accepted). Yet collaboration was largely transactional — the counselor proposed, Nabil picked — with little genuine two-way idea generation, a dynamic Nabil himself called out as menu-like.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Autonomy was consistently supported through 'You're in charge of how we use this time,' repeated option menus, and acceptance when Nabil rejected choices ('Both. I'm not choosing one'). However, framing every step as multiple-choice became a formula, with Nabil noting 'You keep giving me options like this is a menu,' suggesting ownership transfer had become mechanical.

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Motivation and solutions came almost entirely from the counselor, who drafted the email, invented the de-escalation line, and designed the log and threshold plan. Nabil's own motivations (job security, professional identity) were never elicited, and his single moment of insight about hypervigilance went undeveloped.

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Power-sharing language was used and Nabil chose among options, but the expertise dynamic was reversed from MI ideals: the counselor positioned themselves as the producer of scripts, templates, and plans, reducing Nabil to answering intake-style logistics questions. Nabil's own wisdom about hypervigilance surfaced spontaneously but was not developed.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Nabil offered spontaneous change talk ('whether I actually use that line on the day... that's another matter,' 'Maybe if I know the rule is there, I don't have to keep scanning for trouble'), which the counselor did not reflect, amplify, or deepen. No questions were asked to evoke desire, ability, reasons, or commitment for behavioral change.

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The counselor did not reinforce sustain talk, as Nabil's blame of reception was not amplified. However, there were no double-sided reflections linking his desire to keep his job with his anger pattern, and the counselor implicitly accepted the framing that the system was the problem by drafting complaint-focused documents.
