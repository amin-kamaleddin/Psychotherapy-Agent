# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

---

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Maintained a respectful, nonjudgmental stance throughout, validating the client's convictions and frustrations. However, warmth felt somewhat instrumental, pivoting quickly to checklists and plans rather than lingering with unconditional positive regard.

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Some accurate validations were present, but reflections were sparse and deeper emotions—fear of cancer like her mother-in-law, feeling labeled 'hysterical'—were only superficially acknowledged. Little use of complex reflections to deepen understanding of her meaning.

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 5
**Comments:** Maintained clear, consistent focus on the client's GI complaints and her goal of being properly investigated. Every response tied back to concrete next steps with no drift.

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Genuine collaborative elements existed (offering options, drafting letters together, role-play), but the pattern was often question → bulleted expert plan. Solutions were predominantly supplied rather than developed jointly.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Repeatedly offered menus and asked permission, letting the client choose tools and pace at her own volition. Some recommendations were delivered as directives ('Try this short plan at 3am') with limited framing of her ownership of the change process.

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Did not argue for change and allowed motivation to emerge, which is a strength. However, evocation was largely passive—few open questions were asked to elicit her own reasons, hopes, or confidence, with the session mostly supplying content instead.

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Asked for preferences and positioned the client's notebook as central evidence, respecting her expertise. But the session was largely a stream of expert content—test lists, scripts, protocols—rather than co-created plans.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Skillfully created conditions for the client's own change talk—she voluntarily connected symptoms to stress and proposed self-management herself. Missed chances to deepen this change talk explicitly by exploring what coping differently might mean for her life.

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Avoided arguing against biomedical convictions, instead affirming her agenda while gently introducing coping tools through a side-door approach that avoided head-on collision. Deducted for never explicitly honoring the sustain talk with a double-sided reflection to resolve ambivalence.
