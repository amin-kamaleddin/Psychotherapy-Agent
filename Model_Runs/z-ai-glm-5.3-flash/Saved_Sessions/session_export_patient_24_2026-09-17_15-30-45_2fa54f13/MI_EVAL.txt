# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

---

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Warm, nonjudgmental stance throughout with consistent validation of the patient's skepticism and no disapproval even during minimization. However, explicit affirmations of the patient's strengths (marathon discipline, willingness to experiment) are largely absent.

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Reflective listening accurately captures the patient's ambivalence and surprise, e.g., mirroring his reaction to '18 a week.' Reflections sometimes stay at summary level without digging into the deeper identity conflict between health-focused athlete and flagged drinker.

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Clear, consistent focus on alcohol use tied to the patient's stated values (training, recovery, sleep), progressing logically to a concrete experiment. Falls short of 5 because action planning began early, before fully consolidating the patient's own motivation.

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Choices were offered at nearly every juncture, and the patient's input shaped the delay strategy, weeknights, patio swap, and even the weekend review questions. The plan structure itself (metrics, scales, if-then format) was counselor-imposed rather than co-created.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Repeated affirmations of choice and framing of the plan as an experiment the patient controls, with self-compassion after slips reinforced. Autonomy eroded later when the counselor supplied most plan details, and the patient's 'I'm doing this for the training' caveat was not explicitly honored.

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Some motivation was elicited (curiosity, commitment to the experiment, confidence ruler), but the counselor relied heavily on psychoeducation and suggesting the experiment. Key evocative questions connecting drinking to his athlete identity were absent, and the discrepancy was surfaced by the counselor rather than evoked from the client.

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Permission-seeking and option-offering support power sharing early on, but the action-planning phase became noticeably counselor-driven. The patient's expertise (his own routines and running knowledge) was never explicitly acknowledged as central to designing the plan.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Some change talk was elicited ('a 6, maybe,' 'maybe it's worth a shot,' 'I admit I'm a little curious now') and commitment to start was secured. Opportunities to deepen these statements were missed — never asking why it's a 6 rather than a 3, or what improvement would mean to him — and planning proceeded before evoking desire/need language.

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Frequent sustain talk was acknowledged with neutral reframes rather than reinforced, which is adequate. However, no double-sided reflections paired sustain talk with change talk, no strategic pivoting toward the patient's own concerns (sleep, training), and ambivalence was often left unaddressed in favor of information or planning.
