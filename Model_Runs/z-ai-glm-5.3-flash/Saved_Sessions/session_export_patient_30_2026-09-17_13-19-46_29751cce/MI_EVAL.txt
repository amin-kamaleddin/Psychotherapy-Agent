# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

---

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Maintains a respectful, nonjudgmental tone but positive regard is shallow and formulaic, relying on brief acknowledgments rather than consistent warmth. Affirmations of David's honesty and effort are absent until the very end, and his ambivalence is never explicitly normalized.

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Reflective listening is minimal; the counselor responds to emotional disclosures (distrust, his father's heart attack, his wife's frustration, admission of avoidance) with LDL thresholds and checklists instead of reflections. Little effort is made to understand the meaning behind David's distrust, which he ultimately surfaces himself.

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Keeps a clear, consistent focus on cholesterol management and the medication decision with no aimless drift. However, this direction is maintained through structured advice rather than gently guiding toward the client's own goals.

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** The session resembles an expert consultation where the counselor dispenses ready-made plans, checklists, and a pre-drafted form that David merely accepts or tweaks. David's ideas shape only minor details and there is no joint agenda-setting or genuine problem-solving.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The counselor repeatedly emphasizes choice and frames the plan as optional, honoring David's right to decide. However, autonomy is undermined by heavy scripting—ready-made scripts and a pre-drafted form tell David what to say and do, limiting his authorship of his own plan.

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Motivation and ideas are predominantly supplied by the counselor rather than drawn from the client. Rich motivational material David volunteers himself (his father's heart attack at 60, his wife's frustration) is passed over without exploring its significance, and questions remain logistics-oriented rather than values-focused.

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Despite collaborative-sounding offers, the interaction is expert-driven with the counselor delivering pre-made plans while David is left to react. His own ideas (diet first, dog walks, water instead of beer) surface only because he volunteers them, not because the counselor draws them out.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Change talk emerges spontaneously rather than being deliberately evoked through questions about what a realistic first step might look like or why diet-first matters to him. When commitment does occur ('I'll make the appointment'), it is never reflected, deepened, or affirmed.

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Sustain talk (side-effect fears, 'my numbers aren't that bad') is met with factual rebuttal rather than strategic softening, and no double-sided reflections are used. The counter-arguments function like persuasion attempts that risk entrenching resistance.
