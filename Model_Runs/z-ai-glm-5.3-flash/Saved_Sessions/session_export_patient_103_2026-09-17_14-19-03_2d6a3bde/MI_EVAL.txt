# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

---

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Maintained a respectful, nonjudgmental stance throughout, validating the patient's anger about the wait without defensiveness or irritation. However, warmth felt somewhat formulaic and lacked individualized affirmations of the patient's strengths.

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Early reflections accurately captured the patient's dual concerns about disrespect and job consequences, visibly softening him. Empathy dropped mid-session when reflections were replaced by option lists, and vulnerable disclosures ('feeling like the problem,' 'wearing me down') went unreflected.

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Focus remained tightly on the target concerns (delays, anger, job consequences) with logical progression from immediate crisis to future prevention. Could have more explicitly connected strategies back to the core anger concern, but focus never drifted.

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Worked as a service partner, co-editing drafts at the patient's direction, with his ideas directly shaping the final products. Capped because the menu-based approach sometimes positioned the counselor as a solution vendor rather than a thinking partner.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Repeatedly offered choice menus, and the patient exercised real control—rejecting breathing exercises, editing the message, and setting the card threshold. Would be stronger with explicit statements reinforcing the patient's ownership of the change process.

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** The approach was predominantly prescriptive, supplying ready-made solutions rather than drawing motivation from within the client. The counselor never asked about the patient's own reasons for managing his anger, his values, or what success would look like.

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Respected the patient's practical expertise, which materially shaped session outputs. However, the counselor frequently slipped into an expert/deliverer role, answering invitations for joint exploration with counselor-generated lists.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** The patient generated change talk largely on his own ('maybe I've proven their point a few times too often by losing my head'), but the counselor rarely recognized or deepened these moments. No evocative questions or commitment-strengthening reflections were used to build on these openings.

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The counselor avoided arguing with or reinforcing sustain talk (futility, external blame, predicted failure), which is a strength. However, no active softening occurred—dismissals and pessimism were met with new strategy lists rather than double-sided reflections or ambivalence exploration.
