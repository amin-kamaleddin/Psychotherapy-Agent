# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

---

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 5
**Comments:** The psychiatrist affirmed the patient's worth and autonomy throughout, explicitly honoring that 'no talk of quitting' and that labeling is off the table. Even ambivalence was accepted without judgment, keeping the patient engaged and respected.

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist consistently reflected the patient's core values (social connection, feeling healthy, control) and noticed subtle shifts like the sleep concern. A slightly deeper reflection of the emotional weight of his father's health decline could have added emotional depth.

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 5
**Comments:** Every response served a clear purpose: moving from defensiveness toward curiosity, information exchange, and a self-generated behavior plan. The conversation maintained focus on health and drinking without losing the patient's agenda.

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 5
**Comments:** The exchange was fully collaborative — the psychiatrist repeatedly positioned the patient as the expert ('you're the expert on your life') and asked permission-style questions. The plan was entirely patient-generated with the clinician guiding rather than prescribing.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 5
**Comments:** The psychiatrist consistently reinforced patient control and choice ('that's completely your call,' 'your experiment, your choice'). Each step, including the weekend experiment, was framed as the patient's own decision with no pressure or coercion.

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 5
**Comments:** Open questions skillfully drew out the patient's own motivations, self-generated solutions (dinner first, water between beers, stopping at four), and reasons for change. The patient moved from 'I don't have a problem' to designing his own experiment — a hallmark of effective evocation.

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 5
**Comments:** The clinician maintained a genuine side-by-side stance, crediting all ideas to the patient ('that's your own observation, not mine'). Questions invited shared exploration rather than advice-giving, strengthening trust and buy-in.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 5
**Comments:** The psychiatrist expertly elicited and amplified change talk, prompting the patient to verbalize 'I've got control over this thing, not the other way around' and to imagine the benefits of better sleep. Reflecting this language back strengthened the patient's own commitment.

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 5
**Comments:** Sustain talk ('doesn't mean I'm quitting,' 'not a lifestyle change') was consistently accepted and reframed rather than argued against, allowing the patient to hold ambivalence while still moving toward experimentation. This non-confrontational approach kept defenses low and diffused resistance.
