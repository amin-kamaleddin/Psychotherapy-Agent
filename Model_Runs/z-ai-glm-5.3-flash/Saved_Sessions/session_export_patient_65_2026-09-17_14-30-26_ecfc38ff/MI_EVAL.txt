# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

---

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Maintained a neutral, respectful stance and apologized appropriately when safety questions annoyed the client, but warmth and explicit positive regard were minimal. The client's 'less annoying than I thought' comment suggests low expectations were met rather than exceeded.

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Reflections of the client's feelings or perspective were largely absent; the counselor relied on questions and advice instead. When Julian expressed guardedness or fear of freezing up on calls, the counselor pushed forward with checklists or tips rather than reflecting.

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Session stayed focused on the concrete target of completing community service hours, but drifted with repeated broad screening questions the client hadn't asked for. These tangents frustrated the client ('Stop asking me that') and diluted the focus, though direction improved during the role-play.

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The counselor offered options at key junctures and Julian's preferences did shape the session's path, but the overall style was instructional and checklist-driven. The counselor supplied plans, scripts, and tips while Julian passively agreed, making it feel more like advice delivery than joint problem-solving.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Menu-style choices ('fill the script now or role-play?') supported client choice, but autonomy was undermined by repeated unprompted safety screening after the client denied risk. The counselor also tended to prescribe steps rather than invite Julian to generate his own solutions.

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Little effort was made to draw out Julian's own motivations, values, or reasons for change — no exploration of what completing the hours meant to him. The counselor provided answers rather than eliciting, and Julian's spontaneous commitment ('I think I can actually do that this week') arose from the role-play, not deliberate evocative questioning.

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** The counselor frequently assumed the expert role, delivering pre-formatted numbered lists and scripts that drove the agenda. Julian was occasionally consulted, but his contributions did not substantially shape the session and his expertise about his own situation went unacknowledged.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The role-play successfully led Julian to generate his own plan and commitment language ('I can actually do that this week'), and offered choices let him select his preferred path. However, this change talk emerged from practical problem-solving rather than deliberate MI technique, and the counselor never reflected back or amplified his commitments to strengthen them.

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The counselor didn't dwell on or reinforce Julian's avoidant statements, redirecting to the practical task instead of pressing. But there were no skilled double-sided reflections or deliberate pivots — sustain talk was sidestepped rather than strategically transformed.
