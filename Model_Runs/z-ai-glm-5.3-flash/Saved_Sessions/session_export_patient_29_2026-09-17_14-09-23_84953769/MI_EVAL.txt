# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

---

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** ☒ 3 ☐ 1 ☐ 2 ☐ 4 ☐ 5
**Comments:** Basic respect and warmth maintained (e.g., "I'm glad you reached out," "I believe in you"). However, affirmations were largely generic and formulaic rather than specific to the patient's strengths (e.g., insight into triggers, honesty about past failures). No explicit validation of the emotional difficulty of 19 years of smoking or prior failed quit attempts.

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** ☒ 2 ☐ 1 ☐ 3 ☐ 4 ☐ 5
**Comments:** Reflective listening was notably minimal—no statements were reflected back. Emotionally significant disclosures (irritability nearly ending his marriage, 19 years of talking about quitting, fear that skipping dinner "sounds like an excuse") were met with information or checklists rather than reflections. Understanding was implied by accurate advice but never demonstrated through reflection.

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** ☒ 4 ☐ 1 ☐ 2 ☐ 3 ☐ 5
**Comments:** Consistent focus on smoking cessation throughout; medication, quit date, triggers, and coping strategies all addressed without drift. However, direction was achieved primarily through directive advice-giving rather than guiding, moving efficiently to a plan without collaborative MI pacing.

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** ☒ 2 ☐ 1 ☐ 3 ☐ 4 ☐ 5
**Comments:** Interaction functioned largely as expert-to-recipient. Medication comparisons, refusal scripts, evening plans, and symptom timelines were supplied without first exploring the patient's own ideas. Ideas flowed almost exclusively one direction, with limited negotiation or joint problem-solving.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** ☒ 3 ☐ 1 ☐ 2 ☐ 4 ☐ 5
**Comments:** Some autonomy-supportive moments ("Which would you prefer?", presenting medication options), and the patient's decision to skip the birthday dinner was respected. However, the overall style was heavily prescriptive—repeated lists of instructions, scripts, and checklists—leaving little space for the patient to generate his own solutions.

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** ☒ 2 ☐ 1 ☐ 3 ☐ 4 ☐ 5
**Comments:** Motivation clearly resided in the patient, but almost no effort was made to elicit it. No open questions about reasons for quitting, values, confidence, or vision of success. Motivation was assumed and the session moved straight to advice and planning; change talk occurred despite, not because of, counselor technique.

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** ☒ 2 ☐ 1 ☐ 3 ☐ 4 ☐ 5
**Comments:** Counselor assumed the expert role as advice-giver ("I recommend monitoring BP," "Good call—here are quick scripts," repeated checklists). The patient's choices (quit date, skipping dinner) shaped the plan through his own initiative rather than deliberate counselor fostering of power sharing.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** ☒ 2 ☐ 1 ☐ 3 ☐ 4 ☐ 5
**Comments:** Patient spontaneously offered substantial change talk ("I need some medication," "it feels like the smarter play," "feels good to finally have an actual date"), but it was rarely evoked or deepened. No open questions about his own reasons for quitting, no elaboration questions, and no strategic reflections reinforcing motivation.

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** ☒ 2 ☐ 1 ☐ 3 ☐ 4 ☐ 5
**Comments:** Sustain talk (fear of the quit date, "willpower failed me before," dreading the dinner) was not reinforced—a positive—but was also not actively softened or reframed. No double-sided reflections, no affirmations acknowledging ambivalence, and no pivoting of doubts into confidence language; solutions were simply supplied, bypassing opportunities to resolve ambivalence.

---

**Overall Global Ratings Score:** **2.4/5**

**Summary:** The session was well-organized and strongly focused on the target behavior, producing a concrete, realistic quit plan (quit date, medication pathway, trigger management, social support). However, it was delivered in an expert-driven, advice-heavy style with very little reflective listening, evocation, or collaboration—core MI processes. The patient's spontaneous change talk and ambivalence were repeatedly met with information and checklists rather than reflections or strategic MI responses.

**Strengths:** Clear direction, practical safety guidance (BP monitoring, mood concerns), and respect for some client choices.

**Priority Improvement Areas:**
1. Use open questions and reflective listening to demonstrate empathy and evoke the patient's own motivations.
2. Actively reinforce and deepen change talk.
3. Soften sustain talk with double-sided reflections.
4. Shift from prescriptive advice to collaborative, power-sharing exploration.
