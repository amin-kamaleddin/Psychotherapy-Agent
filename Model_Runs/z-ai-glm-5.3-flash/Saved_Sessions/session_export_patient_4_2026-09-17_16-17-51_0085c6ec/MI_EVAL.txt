# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

---

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 5
**Comments:** The psychiatrist consistently maintains warm, respectful, nonjudgmental regard throughout the session, validating Aizada's experience without labeling or dismissing her somatic focus. This unconditional positive regard is maintained even during moments of mistrust and accusation, with non-defensive responses that respectfully reframe.

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist demonstrates strong reflective listening, accurately capturing Aizada's emotional and situational experience ('that uncertainty is brutal'). Some reflections are brief or bundled with immediate problem-solving, and deeper emotional undercurrents (isolation, loneliness) could have been reflected more fully before moving to problem-solving.

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 5
**Comments:** The session maintains clear, purposeful focus from the opening agenda question through concrete planning to a closing next step (Monday visit to Dr. Brown with the letter). Every exchange ties back to the GI symptoms with no topic drift.

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 5
**Comments:** The interaction is genuinely collaborative: the letter is co-constructed with Aizada's input, and her contributions substantially shape the session (adding letter details, requesting Russian versions, proposing the diary design). Power balance feels equal, with the client's preferences driving decisions.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 5
**Comments:** The psychiatrist consistently offers menus rather than prescriptions, asks permission before acting, and respects boundaries—immediately accommodating Aizada's 'not CBT yet' limit with information only. Her self-determined commitments ('not promises... small steps') are affirmed rather than pushed.

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist effectively draws out Aizada's own motivations via reflections on her spontaneous disclosures, leading her to self-initiate walking and request the diary. However, evocation is somewhat passive—no targeted evocative questions (importance/confidence scaling) were used, and a final summary consolidating her own arguments for change was missing.

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Aizada is consistently positioned as the expert on her own life, and her contributions visibly shape the session (letter wording, material choices, diary idea). At times the psychiatrist slips into an expert/administrative mode with less explicit exploration of Aizada's own knowledge and past coping strategies.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist skillfully creates conditions for Aizada's own change talk ('maybe I wouldn't mind learning more about that nerve thing,' 'Maybe I'll try to walk a little each day'). The change talk is not systematically deepened—no explicit confidence/importance scaling or elaboration questions.

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The psychiatrist avoids arguing and uses effective reframes ('the pain is real,' 'it can be biological even when tests are normal'), but sustain talk (cancer fear, distrust of Dr. Brown) is acknowledged rather than actively softened. Double-sided reflections to resolve ambivalence were largely absent.
