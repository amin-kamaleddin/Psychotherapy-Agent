# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

---

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Maintained a respectful, nonjudgmental stance with a guarded, externally motivated client, offering specific, genuine affirmations and normalizing awkwardness. Regard could be warmer and more explicitly unconditional—e.g., affirming Julian's courage in attempting the role-play rather than primarily praising task performance.

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Opening reflections on Julian's stressors were accurate, but empathy remained largely cognitive rather than emotional. Repeated vulnerabilities (fear of messing up, anxiety about calling, shame about not following through) were moved past quickly in favor of scripts and logistics, missing deeper reflections on the feelings beneath.

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 5
**Comments:** Direction is the strongest domain: the session maintained unwavering focus on completing service hours by the deadline, with every exchange advancing a concrete pathway from requirements to schedule. Pacing was efficient with no drift, well-suited to the urgent timeline.

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Inclusive language and responsiveness to stated preferences (e.g., switching to texting) show surface collaboration, but the plan, scripts, and problem-solving were largely clinician-generated with Julian mostly selecting from offered options. True co-creation—building on Julian's own ideas—was minimal.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Frequent choice points (plan vs. explore, text vs. call, role-play options) let Julian's selections demonstrably shape the session, though support was somewhat menu-driven. Missed chances to affirm his ownership of the outcome, notably returning the 'doable' conclusion to Julian rather than validating it for him.

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Motivation was treated as a logistics-and-skills problem rather than something to draw out from within—no exploration of Julian's values, reasons, or hopes. His emerging hope arose spontaneously from rehearsal, not deliberate evocation, which is a significant limitation given his externally motivated presentation.

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Verbal framing was shared ('we can map out'), but expertise and direction flowed heavily from clinician to client, who supplied the text, scripts, site list, and practice dialogue. The stance was more coach/consultant than partner, with no acknowledgment that Julian holds expertise on his own barriers and schedule.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Moderate evocation through open questions, structured choices, and behavioral rehearsal produced incremental commitments and, eventually, genuine self-initiated hope ('that's doable, right?'). However, these statements were never deepened—no confidence rulers, elaboration, or questions about Julian's own reasons for succeeding.

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Julian's sustain talk ('I always mess that part up,' 'I don't really like calling people') was acknowledged briefly without reinforcement, consistently pivoting to actionable next steps. The 'no' role-play effectively converted anticipated failure into a learned workaround, though double-sided reflections could have resolved ambivalence more skillfully.
