# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

**Patient:** Elena | **Session:** Contraception follow-up planning after pregnancy scare

---

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Consistent respect and nonjudgment throughout. Elena explicitly valued the absence of a lecture ("Thanks for not, like, lecturing me today"). No disapproval expressed regarding avoidance or current practices (withdrawal). Affirmations were somewhat formulaic ("You handled that scary moment," "you're doing the sensible, caring thing") with limited individualized positive regard for her honesty about ambivalence or fear of her mother's reaction.

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Some accurate early reflections ("I hear relief — and also how scary those waiting days were"). However, the counselor repeatedly missed opportunities to reflect Elena's richest emotional material — e.g., "I keep playing it over in my head" and "I don't want to roll those dice again" were bypassed in favor of logistics. Internal conflict ("part wake-up call, part I managed fine") was not reflected. Session dominated by closed logistical questions rather than reflective listening.

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Clinical focus lost almost immediately after the opening. Once Elena deferred the contraception conversation, the session shifted to anxiety management and appointment logistics (booking, reminders, parking, info sheets). No effort to keep the target behavior alive through exploration of the discrepancy between "I managed fine so far" and "I don't want to roll those dice again." Session concluded with scheduling details rather than substantive movement toward the referral concern.

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Menus of options offered (calming tools, appointment format, reminder method) reflect partial collaboration. However, agenda-setting and the plan were largely counselor-structured; Elena's role was mostly answering logistical questions. The counselor did not invite Elena's ideas about what would help her feel ready, nor co-create meaning from her scare. Collaboration real but superficial and unevenly distributed.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Relative strength. Explicit statements: "You don't need to decide anything that day — just gather info" and "It's okay to come undecided — you're in control"; agreed to add "just info, no pressure" to the reminder text at Elena's request. Elena confirmed it landed ("That made this easier"). Not higher because autonomy support was operationalized mainly through deferral and logistics rather than affirming self-direction in moments of expressed doubt.

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Little effort to draw motivation from within the client. Motivational content ("I owe it to myself," "I don't want to feel that again") emerged spontaneously from Elena, not through counselor technique. No open questions designed to elicit or expand motivation (e.g., "What would need to be different for you to feel safer?"). Reliance on menus and reassurance rather than evocation; final evocative prompt from Elena left unanswered in transcript.

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Repeatedly solicited Elena's preferences (phone vs. in-person, morning vs. afternoon, text vs. email), supporting some power-sharing. However, interaction often read as administrative checklisting; rapid multi-barreled questions positioned the counselor as case manager rather than recognizing Elena's expertise about her own motivations and fears. Elena shaped logistics but not the therapeutic direction.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Elena spontaneously offered substantial change talk — "That scare was a wake-up call," "I don't want to feel that again," "maybe I owe it to myself to at least hear the options" — but the counselor rarely evoked or deepened it. No open questions exploring motivations, hopes, or vision of success. Change statements were typically glossed over in favor of scheduling logistics; most change talk was client-generated despite, not because of, counselor technique.

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Appropriate non-probing of sustain talk, with brief acknowledgment of ambivalence ("It's totally okay to feel torn"). However, little active softening or strategic reorientation — statements like "I'm careful, mostly" and "why fix what isn't broken" were left unexplored rather than met with double-sided reflections pairing them with her stated fear. Neutral acceptance without skilled pivoting limited the score.

---

**Overall Global Rating:** 2.9/5

**Summary:** Strong autonomy support and a nonjudgmental, pressure-free stance Elena explicitly valued; a low-pressure follow-up was successfully secured and practical anxiety tools offered. However, the session was dominated by administrative logistics at the expense of MI substance: Elena's rich spontaneous change talk was repeatedly skipped over rather than reflected, deepened, or used to build discrepancy with her sustain talk. The target behavior — contraception decision-making — drifted out of focus after the first two exchanges.

**Priority Improvements:**
1. Reflect and selectively reinforce change talk instead of moving to scheduling questions.
2. Use double-sided reflections to hold the ambivalence ("part wake-up call, part I've managed fine") and gently keep the contraception focus alive.
3. Replace multi-barreled logistical questions with one open evocative question at a time.
4. Affirm Elena's specific strengths (her honesty, her self-awareness about pressure) rather than generic reassurance.
