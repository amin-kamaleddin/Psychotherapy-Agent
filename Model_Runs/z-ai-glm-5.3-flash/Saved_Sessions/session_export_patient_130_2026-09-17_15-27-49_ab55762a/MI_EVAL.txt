# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

---

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Maintained a respectful, nonjudgmental stance even with a dismissive, defensive client, never moralizing about his use. However, warmth felt formulaic ('I hear you' repeated) and affirmations of the client's strengths were underused.

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Reflections were consistently accurate, including capturing the key emotional moment about his wife's misery mattering more than he let on. Reflections tended to be mirror-like and repetitive, restating both sides without deepening the emotional meaning.

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Kept a clear focus on drug use and its consequences, progressively narrowing toward a concrete safety plan. Focus was occasionally muddied by unacknowledged corrections (drug use vs. drinking) and circling questions.

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Built the plan from the client's own words ('balanced,' 'staying over is easiest') and offered choices at nearly every turn. Occasionally moved faster than the client's readiness and suggested specific ideas before being asked.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Strongly affirmed the client's control ('You're in charge') and accepted refusals without pressure, not challenging his insistence on deciding for himself. Autonomy was sometimes expressed through option menus rather than open invitations, and ownership of chosen change wasn't explicitly reinforced.

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Open questions elicited the session's most important material — the client's admission that his wife's misery 'does get to me.' However, this crucial change talk was immediately pivoted to logistics rather than deepened, leaving motivation elicited only sporadically and shallow.

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Used choice-offering language but repeatedly relied on menus of pre-formatted options, with the counselor's agenda somewhat ahead of the client's. The client's own plan emerged more from his resistance to the offered ideas than from deliberate power-sharing.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Elicited meaningful change talk (admitting the driving risk, agreeing to text Dmitri, concern for his wife) and reinforced with follow-through questions. Evocation was inconsistent — prompts often offered options instead of inviting the client's own reasons, and strong moments weren't deepened.

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Repeatedly reflected sustain talk neutrally, at times amplifying it ('you're proud of keeping your responsibilities,' 'that makes sense' after minimizing). Arguing and confrontation were avoided, but strategic double-sided reflections weighted toward change were absent.
