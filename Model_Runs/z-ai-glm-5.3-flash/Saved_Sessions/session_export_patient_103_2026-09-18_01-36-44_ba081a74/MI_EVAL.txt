# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

---

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 5
**Comments:** The psychiatrist consistently affirms the patient's autonomy and efforts (e.g., 'you've taken something huge and made it small enough to actually do') while accepting his anger and ambivalence without judgment. Responses honor his perspective, including his sense of justification about being disrespected.

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 5
**Comments:** Reflections accurately capture both surface content and deeper meanings, such as the collision between feeling disrespected and the personal cost of anger, and the fear of failing in front of his wife. Complex reflections repeatedly land with the patient ('That's a fair way of putting it').

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 5
**Comments:** The clinician steers the conversation toward identifying early warning signs, in-the-moment strategies, and commitment to a specific first step. Each question builds a coherent change arc from problem awareness to a concrete, testable plan.

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 5
**Comments:** Questions are consistently invitational ('what do you think might work,' 'what would be your signal') rather than directive, positioning the patient as the expert on his own solutions. The plan emerges entirely from the patient's own ideas and language.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 5
**Comments:** The clinician explicitly supports self-direction ('you can only control me' is highlighted as the patient's own insight) and lets the patient set his own pace, ultimately committing only to the step he chose himself ('I'll try the jaw').

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 5
**Comments:** Change talk is actively elicited throughout: desired self-image ('the version of you you want to be'), success criteria ('how would you know it worked?'), and self-motivational reminders. The patient articulates reasons, abilities, and commitment largely in his own words.

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 5
**Comments:** The exchange functions as a genuine back-and-forth where the clinician's reflections and questions feed directly off the patient's last statement. Ideas like the phone note and jaw check come from the patient, with the clinician amplifying rather than adding.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 5
**Comments:** The clinician strategically evokes and reinforces desire ('I don't want to be that bloke'), ability ('I can only control me'), and commitment talk ('I'll commit to the jaw'), then deepens it by exploring signals, plans, and success imagery. Change talk progresses naturally across the session.

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 5
**Comments:** Sustain talk ('you're asking like I'm the problem here,' 'it just happens to me') is met with reflective validation rather than confrontation or arguing for change. Doubt about the plan is acknowledged and reframed into a small, manageable commitment.
