# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

---

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The counselor maintained a respectful, nonjudgmental stance with warm affirmations that validated Mai's frustration and skepticism. However, the affirmations became formulaic and repetitive (e.g., "You handled this really well" used nearly verbatim multiple times).

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The opening reflection accurately captured Mai's confusion, worries about muscle effects, and dose concerns, but later reflections became transactional. Emotional undertones—feeling dismissed by Dr. Ferris and giving up her favorite juice—went unreflected as the counselor drifted toward information delivery.

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 5
**Comments:** Excellent focus throughout on understanding and adhering to the new statin, with every turn advancing toward a concrete, actionable plan. The session concluded with Mai accurately restating the full plan with no topic drift.

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The counselor framed the session as joint work and the final plan genuinely reflected Mai's own routines and choices. However, the repeated menu-offering structure felt scripted and weighted toward the counselor's agenda rather than true power sharing.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Choices were consistently offered without any pressure, and Mai's decisions (nighttime dosing, orange juice, sending the portal message) were fully honored. Autonomy support relied mainly on option menus rather than explicit statements reinforcing her ownership of the change.

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The opener was a strong evocative question, but the session then shifted predominantly to psychoeducation rather than deliberate evocation. Questions about Mai's own reasons, importance, or confidence (e.g., a confidence ruler) were never used to develop her intrinsic motivation.

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Offering choices and asking "Which would help most right now?" invited client input, but the repeated numbered menus created a service-provider dynamic. The counselor's expertise dominated the exchange, and Mai's own knowledge had to be volunteered rather than actively elicited.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Mai generated strong commitment statements about nighttime dosing, switching juices, and calling Dr. Ferris about symptoms, which the counselor affirmed. Change talk arose indirectly through education rather than deliberate evocative questions, and opportunities to deepen commitment language were missed.

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Mai's skepticism ("Why am I taking this medication?") was never reinforced or amplified and was acknowledged as valid before pivoting to understanding and action. No double-sided reflections were used to actively resolve ambivalence, but sustain talk was minimal and effectively redirected.
