# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

---

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Consistently respectful, warm, and nonjudgmental tone; opening ("I'm here to listen, understand, and support your choices") set an accepting frame. Validated fear of failure without judgment about weight or past diet failures. Affirmations were somewhat formulaic and repetitively restated the plan rather than being individualized.

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Accurate reflective listening, e.g., "That sounds really hard — new job and move disrupted routines... understandably scared of failing." Reflections often bundled with multiple questions; deeper emotional exploration (repeated failures, loss of active life before relocating) was touched on but not developed further.

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 5
**Comments:** Clear, consistent focus on the target behavior (realistic, non-diet movement change). Every turn tied back to the plan: first step, cue (shoes by the door), time (7pm), fallback, alarm label, check-in structure — resulting in a fully formed implementation intention by session's end.

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Joint framing ("we can choose one tiny, specific step," "Shall we check in here tomorrow") and client shaped check-in content. However, the initial plan template (5-minute walk, shoes by the door, fallback) was offered by the counselor before fully eliciting her own ideas, making power-sharing slightly uneven in the plan-building phase.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 5
**Comments:** Consistently reinforced ownership and choice: "support your choices," "If anything changes tonight or you want to tweak the plan, tell me," and aligned with her non-diet preference. Fallback framing ("2 min also OK") removed coercive pressure; client's own words — "something I'm choosing for myself, not something being done to me" — confirm success.

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Strong elicitation of client motivation: past successes (walking memory), importance/confidence scales, "what one small thing would raise it," and self-compassion language ("one missed night doesn't erase anything" came from the client). At times supplied content (example plans, alarm labels) rather than eliciting; deeper values (energy, self-image) not explicitly linked.

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Permission-like questions ("Which feels doable?", "Would you like to...?") and incorporated the client's ideas (walk choice, alarm, check-in questions). However, solutions were frequently introduced rapidly (e.g., "5-minute walk after dinner, shoes by the door") rather than eliciting her own strategies first, shifting the expert role toward the counselor.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Effective use of importance/confidence scaling and evocative questions, successfully eliciting change talk ("maybe a 6 or 7" rising to 8; "I actually think I can do it") and reinforcing it ("That 8/10 confidence is a real strength"). Scaling questions were repeated without deepening follow-up (e.g., "Why a 7 and not a 4?"), and commitment language was never explicitly summarized to consolidate it.

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Sustain talk ("nights I come home drained," "scared of failing again") was acknowledged in accurate reflections and gently pivoted to solutions; the fallback plan reframed failure as acceptable ("it's not a test I can fail"). Avoided reinforcing "diets don't stick" talk by aligning with her anti-diet stance. Would benefit from more deliberate double-sided reflection; some reflections restated the worry without redirecting.

---

**Overall Global Ratings Score: 4.1/5**
