# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

---

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Maintains a respectful, nonjudgmental stance with warm validation of a stigmatized behavior ('I believe you,' 'I'm glad you're safe now'). However, warmth is brief and formulaic, repeatedly interrupted by safety checklists, limiting the depth of felt positive regard.

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Some accurate reflections ('it makes sense that cutting quiets the overwhelm'), but empathy is inconsistent and shallow. Key disclosures about isolation, parental fear, and chronic despair ('it's always there in the background') are bypassed in favor of safety logistics rather than reflected.

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Maintains consistent focus on self-harm and risk, with repeated risk checks and a concrete, scheduled follow-up plan. Focus is somewhat narrow, however, locked on immediate risk reduction while neglecting the underlying exhaustion and school stress the client identified as triggers.

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** The psychiatrist proposes nearly all ideas, and the client mostly selects from pre-made options or complies passively. The clinician never asks what Liora thinks might work or has already tried, and the final plan is essentially therapist-designed with minor client edits.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Genuine choice exists through menu-style options and accepting her refusal to involve a roommate without pushback. But much of the interaction is prescriptive with long directive lists, and her lukewarm consent ('I'm not promising anything more') is never met with explicit affirmation of her control over the change process.

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Motivation is treated as something to be installed via tools and plans rather than elicited from within. Questions are predominantly yes/no risk checks and menu selections, and her only self-generated motivation ('I don't want to keep doing this forever') goes undeveloped.

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** The psychiatrist largely assumes the expert role, responding to her crucial disclosure about the self-harm experience with directive safety steps rather than inviting her expertise. The plan and reminder text are largely clinician-designed, consistent with the client's resigned 'whatever, fine' compliance.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** One significant moment of client change talk ('I don't want to keep doing this forever') is missed rather than reflected, affirmed, or deepened. No open questions evoke desire, ability, reasons, or commitment, and the commitment obtained is therapist-proposed and grudgingly accepted.

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Substantial sustain talk ('the only thing that helps,' 'won't stop it anyway,' 'no promises') is neither reinforced nor actively softened. There are no double-sided reflections linking the relief cutting provides to her stated wish to stop, and ambivalence is never strategically resolved.
