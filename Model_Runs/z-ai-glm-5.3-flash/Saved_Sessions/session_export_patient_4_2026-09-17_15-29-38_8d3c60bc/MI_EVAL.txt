# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

---

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Maintained respectful, nonjudgmental validation of the patient's pain and biomedical concerns ('the pain is real'). Affirmations were somewhat formulaic and brief, lacking deeper individualized regard during moments of distrust or insistence.

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Accurate reflections captured both content and emotion, e.g., the exhausting four-year illness. Reflections were often compressed and quickly followed by option-listing, missing chances to explore the grief and loneliness beneath disclosures like her mother-in-law and 3am googling.

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Session stayed consistently focused on diagnostic clarity and symptom management, moving steadily toward concrete steps with minimal drift. Direction relied on option-offering rather than guiding, and missed chances to summarize the emerging stress-symptom pattern back to her.

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The interaction felt like joint problem-solving, with the patient's ideas directly shaping the plan (diary entries, email, rehearsal). The counselor's frequent ready-made templates and scripts tilted the exchange toward expert/advice-giver rather than true equal partner.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Nearly every turn ended with choice, and 'I'm with you either way' reinforced ownership; no pressure toward psychological interpretation. Some turns offered dense counselor-generated menus rather than inviting her to generate her own path.

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The weakest domain: the counselor offered options and tools rather than drawing out the patient's own motivations and values with open questions. Change talk emerged largely despite, not because of, counselor technique.

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Consistently positioned the patient as decision-maker, and her contributions demonstrably shaped the concrete plan. Defaulting to pre-drafted scripts and templates subtly retained an expert/helper role rather than fully centering her expertise.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The patient generated meaningful change talk, but it was self-initiated rather than skillfully evoked; no open evocative questions, confidence scaling, or elaboration of her own reasons for change. Change talk was affirmed but not actively cultivated.

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Avoided amplifying sustain talk, validating her test requests without arguing or reinforcing the biomedical fixation; the double-sided reflection on context vs. symptom reality was a highlight. Fell short of actively pivoting her cancer fears toward change or revisiting the stress-symptom link she raised.
