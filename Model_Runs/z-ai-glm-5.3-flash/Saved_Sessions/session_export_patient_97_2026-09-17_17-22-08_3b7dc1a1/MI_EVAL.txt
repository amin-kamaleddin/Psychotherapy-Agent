# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

---

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Maintained a respectful, nonjudgmental stance throughout, affirming Mateo's efforts ('You did it — said the words even though they felt awkward') despite his criminal history and ambivalence. Warmth was somewhat formulaic, lacking deeper affirmations of inherent worth or normalization of his struggle.

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Offered accurate summaries of Mateo's situation, but emotional content was lightly touched — phone anxiety, awkwardness about saying 'recently released,' and shame were acknowledged but not deeply reflected. Reflections were often bundled with rapid-fire questions, diluting emotional attunement.

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 5
**Comments:** Exceptionally clear focus on the target behavior with each turn moving progressively — from goal clarification to scaling, step selection, rehearsal, and a week-long commitment. Essentially no topic drift; direction was consistently maintained and purposeful.

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Included collaborative elements like joint role-play and asking which plan 'feels most doable,' but the interaction was noticeably asymmetric — the psychiatrist designed the options and supplied all materials while Mateo mostly responded. Co-construction occurred through limited-choice menus rather than genuine joint generation of the plan.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Consistently presented choices ('Would you rather A or B?'), and Mateo's preferences steered the plan, including his decision to decline an extra rehearsal. Minor deduction for the heavy prescribing of scripts and checklists, which at times felt structurally controlling.

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Skillfully drew motivation from Mateo through rulers and open questions, eliciting his own vision of a job, a place, and kids, plus intrinsic motivations like 'I'm trying to do this right.' Evocation was largely technique-driven rather than a deep exploration of his values and emotional drivers.

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Mateo's contributions clearly shaped the plan — he chose Tuesday after 1, option B, and his own script wording — but the overall style remained fairly directive. The psychiatrist supplied most content and structured nearly every turn with stacked questions, leaving Mateo in a mostly responsive role.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Effectively used importance and confidence rulers, prompting meaningful change talk: Mateo chose option B, raised his confidence from 5 to 8, and committed to 'not picking up when those guys call... that's the deal with myself this week.' Relied heavily on structured scaling questions rather than open-ended exploration of his own reasons.

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Acknowledged sustain talk ('it's easy money,' 'I've tried before') without reinforcement and redirected toward actionable steps, including gently holding the boundary when Mateo expected the psychiatrist to call for him. Missed opportunities for double-sided reflections, responding with redirection rather than reflective softening.
