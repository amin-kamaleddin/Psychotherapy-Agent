# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

---

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Maintained a respectful, nonjudgmental stance throughout, apologizing for the wait and validating the client's frustration without defensiveness. However, acceptance felt somewhat formulaic, focused on efficiency rather than deeper personal regard.

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Some good empathic moments (reflecting anger over lost billable time), but empathy was limited overall. The counselor quickly moved to logistics and rarely reflected the client's emotional undertones — fear about health, breathlessness, and past failed attempts.

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Maintained clear focus on target behaviors and efficiently narrowed to a concrete action (3 packs at 8:45) with a specific follow-up (Thursday 8:15) and fallback plan. Direction was achieved through directive structuring rather than collaborative goal evolution.

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Partial collaboration through offering choices and honoring the client's requests, but the counselor supplied virtually all substantive content without asking for the client's ideas. The client was treated more as an implementer of the counselor's plan than an equal partner.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Repeatedly offered choice ('Which would be most useful?') and scheduling options, negotiated the walking deal, and avoided pressure or coercion. The frame still steered toward the counselor's pre-designed plan, and the client had to resist ambition to protect his autonomy.

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Made minimal effort to elicit the client's own motivations — no evocative questions, importance/confidence rulers, or exploration of hopes and values. Relied on persuasion-through-practicality rather than drawing out and strengthening internal motivation.

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Offered choices (menu of options, scheduling slots), conveying some power-sharing, but the expertise clearly resided with the counselor throughout. The client explicitly noted his own knowledge ('I could write that lecture myself'), yet the counselor delivered didactic, expert-driven plans.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** The client generated strong change talk himself ('I do want to get hold of the weight'), but the counselor did little to evoke, reflect, or amplify it. No open questions designed to elicit motivations, and spontaneous commitment language went unexploited.

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Did not reinforce sustain talk or argue with the client's complaints, and the 'data, not failure' reframe was a good moment. However, sustain talk about time pressure and past failures was largely sidestepped through planning rather than resolved through strategic or double-sided reflections.
