# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

---

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Respectful, nonjudgmental tone throughout; normalized ambivalence appropriately. Affirmations were thin and generic, and crisis-line disclaimers felt formulaic at times.

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Some accurate reflections (shame, kids as motivation), but empathy was uneven—early pivot to risk assessment skipped exploring his shame, and emotional moments (daughter's question, pride in three months) went unexplored.

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Session stayed directed toward relapse prevention with a coherent, actionable plan and strong closing momentum. Early risk-assessment pivot and lockbox/price details briefly pulled focus from the target.

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Elias substantially shaped the plan (contacts, text edits, lockbox choice) and counselor solicited preferences. Initial plan template was counselor-generated, and repeated advice-dispensing tilted the balance toward the expert.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Decisions consistently framed as Elias's choice, with permission sought and objections honored via alternatives. Safety plan presented as a package with limited co-design, and no affirmation of his ownership of his three months.

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Weakest domain—no open questions eliciting Elias's own reasons for change, no scaling questions, no exploration of the daughter's 'better now' moment. Most change talk emerged spontaneously rather than through skillful evocation.

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Elias's input was invited and incorporated at several points, but the session had a noticeable expert-driven quality with detailed lists of strategies, products, and prices supplied rather than elicited.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Meaningful change talk emerged and was partly capitalized on, but the counselor never reflected his strongest commitment statements ('I want that to actually be true') or asked evocative confidence/importance questions. Change talk was passively received rather than actively cultivated.

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Validated his resistance to surrendering pills and offered autonomy-preserving alternatives (lockbox, limited fills), effectively softening it. But early response paraphrased sustain talk without a double-sided reflection pivoting to change reasons.
