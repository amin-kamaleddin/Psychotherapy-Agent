# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

---

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 5
**Comments:** The clinician consistently affirms the patient's autonomy and strengths, framing her insights ('I pour another glass without thinking') as genuine accomplishments and normalizing ambivalence as understandable.

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 5
**Comments:** Reflections accurately capture both the value the patient places on wine and its costs, demonstrating deep understanding of her mixed feelings and life context.

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 5
**Comments:** The conversation maintains a consistent, gentle direction toward change—exploring what the patient wants different—without drifting away from the topic.

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 5
**Comments:** The clinician repeatedly invites the patient's own ideas and choices ('What feels like a first step you could actually live with?'), letting the plan emerge from her rather than prescribing one.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 5
**Comments:** Change is framed as the patient's own decision ('a plan that's genuinely yours'), and she explicitly rejects an imposed guideline goal in favor of her own, which the clinician honors.

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 5
**Comments:** Open questions elicit the patient's own reasons for change and change talk—her morning fog, 'not liking who I am on bottle nights,' and readiness statement ('I think I'm ready to try that').

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 5
**Comments:** The exchange feels genuinely two-way, with the clinician building on the patient's ideas (bath with a book, limiting wine at home, calling her sister) rather than steering her.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 5
**Comments:** Questions skillfully evoke commitment, reasons, and ability ('what tells you you're ready?', 'what would be the first sign this is working?'), producing strong sustained change talk.

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Sustain talk (wine helping her unwind, the hard-to-give-up role on low evenings) is reflected and accepted rather than confronted; while well handled, the clinician does not explicitly use strategies to actively soften it.
