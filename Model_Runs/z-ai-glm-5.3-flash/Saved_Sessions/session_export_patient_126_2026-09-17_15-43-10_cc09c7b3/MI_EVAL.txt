# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

---

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist maintained a respectful, nonjudgmental stance throughout, explicitly stating 'No judgment—just trying to understand it clearly' and avoiding disapproval even when the patient minimized the score. However, affirmations were thin and warmth somewhat procedural, with little validation of the difficulty of balancing family, work, and social identity.

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Accurate simple reflections appeared early on (e.g., reflecting the patient's surprise and view that drinking is normal), but responses increasingly shifted to information-giving and menu-listing. The emotional and social meaning of drinking—pride in providing for family, fear of looking 'like a lemon' among mates—was never explored, and complex reflections about underlying ambivalence were largely absent.

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The session maintained clear focus on alcohol use, progressing logically from agenda-setting to psychoeducation, screening, and concrete reduction strategies, with consistent redirection when the patient drifted. Momentum occasionally stalled due to heavy reliance on tactic-listing, and the smoking topic at the end went unaddressed.

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The counselor behaved as a partner, asking permission, offering transparent score information, and jointly setting the agenda. However, change options were drawn largely from a pre-set tactic list rather than co-created from the patient's own ideas, making the menus feel somewhat scripted.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 5
**Comments:** Autonomy support was the strongest domain—the counselor consistently framed everything as choice ('I'm here to listen and support whatever you choose,' 'Which of those sounds doable?') and accepted the patient's rejection of suggestions without pressure. No coercive or persuasive language appeared, reinforcing the patient's ownership of the change process.

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** An excellent opening evocative question and skillful use of the confidence scale showed evocative ability, but most effort went to psychoeducation and tactic provision rather than drawing out the patient's own motivations. The patient's question 'What's it actually supposed to be doing to me?' was answered with information instead of reflection, and values like fatherhood and family were raised but never used to deepen motivation.

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Repeated permission-asking before screening and explanations, along with offering choices rather than directives, signaled respect for the patient's expertise. The clinician-led structure (screen, score, options) and absence of explicitly inviting the patient's own solutions kept it from a full score.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The counselor effectively elicited commitment-adjacent language, including 'I can try keeping it to four,' endorsement of the cash-limit tactic, and a confidence rating of 5–6. However, these moments were not deepened—e.g., not asking what would raise the confidence or why the cash limit appealed.

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The counselor avoided arguing and sometimes neutrally reflected sustain talk, preventing reinforcement. But there was little active pivoting—no double-sided reflections linking to the patient's quit-smoking success, no reframing of drinking as stress relief, and risk information even prompted further minimization ('that's just being normal').
