# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

**Client:** Derrick | **Context:** Court-mandated session (DUI-related)

---

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Strong unconditional positive regard in the opening—"you don't have to want to be here for this to be worth your time" normalizes mandated attendance without judgment. Validated the awkwardness of the encounter ("a hard place to start from"). No labeling or disapproval despite client minimization ("it was beer, bad luck"). Few visible counselor turns limit confirmation of sustained warmth throughout the session.

---

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Accurate perspective-taking demonstrated: "Sitting across from a stranger, talking about things you'd probably rather keep private... I get why this might feel like a box to check" reflects the client's guarded stance before he voiced it. Insufficient visible counselor turns to confirm consistently deep reflective listening across the full session.

---

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Clear focus maintained on the target behavior area (drinking/DUI consequences), anchored to what matters to the client (license, work). The opening question steers productively without wandering. Single visible turn limits assessment of whether focus persisted through later exploration of peer pressure and isolation.

---

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Session framed as a joint enterprise—"what we focus on is completely your call"—inviting the client to shape the agenda. No expert-driven lecturing despite the assessment context. Observable evidence of two-way co-construction across the full session is limited.

---

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 5
**Comments:** Exemplary. "What we focus on is completely your call" is an explicit, direct affirmation of client choice at the outset—especially important given the client's court-mandated status and resistance to being controlled. Framing avoids any pressure or persuasion; the client defines the session's value on his own terms.

---

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The closing question ("What would need to happen in here for this to feel less like a waste of yours?") is a strong evocative question that successfully engaged a defensive client, drawing out his own reasons (license, work) rather than imposing external ones. Repeated, skillful evocation across the session's full arc is not observable in the transcript.

---

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Explicitly positions the client as the expert on his own needs ("what we focus on is completely your call"). Particularly skillful with a mandated client who expects an authoritarian encounter. Limited observable turns prevent confirming sustained power-sharing throughout.

---

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The evocative open question successfully engaged a reluctant client. However, no visible counselor responses that reinforce or deepen the client's substantial change talk (e.g., his license/reasons for change). Evocation of change language present but not demonstrably consistent.

---

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Client produced significant sustain talk ("I don't really think I have a problem," "bad luck with the cop," "it was beer"). The counselor does not reinforce it and effectively sidesteps by framing the session around the client's own agenda. No evidence of active redirection, double-sided reflections, or strategic pivoting when sustain talk emerged mid-session.

---

**Overall Global Ratings Score:** **3.9/5**

---

*Summary: Strong opening—normalizing mandated status without judgment, explicitly handing control to the client, and using an evocative question that engaged a defensive client. Standout strengths: autonomy support and acceptance. Key limitations: few counselor responses visible, so reflective listening depth, change talk cultivation, sustain talk softening, and sustained direction could not be fully verified. Recommendations: use more visible complex reflections when the client minimizes ("bad luck," "just beer"), double-sided reflections to hold ambivalence poles (belonging vs. safety/freedom), and deliberate deepening of change talk as it emerges.*
