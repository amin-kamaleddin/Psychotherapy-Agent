# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

---

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The clinician conveyed strong acceptance of the patient's perspective, explicitly avoiding judgment about drinking ('not here to tell you those nights are wrong') and affirming his autonomy and self-worth. Affirmations were genuine and frequent.

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 5
**Comments:** The clinician demonstrated excellent reflective listening throughout, accurately capturing the patient's perspective, including key sentiments like being 'unlucky,' embarrassment about his roommate bailing him out, and disappointment in missing the group project meeting. Reflections were consistently validating without judgment.

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The clinician maintained a clear direction toward safer drinking behaviors while following the patient's lead, keeping the conversation anchored to the patient's stated concerns (blackouts, letting others down). Direction was present but at times followed rather than gently guided the patient's momentum.

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The interaction was highly collaborative, with the clinician positioning the patient as the expert on his own life ('That's you figuring this out, not me telling you'). The clinician offered suggestions sparingly and prioritized the patient's autonomy in generating solutions.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The clinician consistently reinforced the patient's autonomy, affirming that change was his choice and that his plan was self-generated. Autonomy support was strong throughout, though occasional suggestions (holding a drink, alternating with water) came from the clinician's prompts rather than fully from the patient.

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 5
**Comments:** The clinician skillfully drew out the patient's own motivations and values, including his desire to show up for teammates, keep his social life intact, and prove the incident wasn't a big deal. Most change talk originated from the patient himself, reflecting strong evocative technique.

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The clinician consistently framed decisions as the patient's own ('you get to decide how they fit together') and built plans collaboratively from the patient's own ideas. Some questions were slightly leading (e.g., offering 'eating first or skipping the shots' as options) rather than fully open.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The clinician used effective open-ended questions and affirmations to draw out change talk, such as asking what would make mornings go differently and highlighting the patient's own plan ('you just built your own plan right there'). Strong reflective emphasis on the patient's own desire and ability statements.

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The clinician accepted the patient's sustain talk ('not like I need to stop going out') without arguing, and continued to roll with it while gently keeping the focus on what the patient wanted to change. More direct exploration of the ambivalence could have deepened this further.
