# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

---

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Maintains a neutral, non-judgmental stance and validates the client's fear of being dismissed, but warmth is formulaic with few genuine affirmations of her strengths (persistence, self-knowledge, candid ambivalence).

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Offers occasional accurate reflections (e.g., 'that flare pattern doesn't mean it's all in your head'), but most turns are test lists and practical reminders. Powerful emotional disclosures — grief over her 'small life,' loneliness, fear of cancer — receive no reflective response.

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The session stays focused on concrete GI concerns and next steps without wandering aimlessly. However, direction serves an advice-giving agenda rather than an MI change process, with no collaborative agenda setting or exploration of her broader distress.

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** The interaction resembles a consultation with an expert rather than work between equals — the counselor generates nearly all content (diagnoses, tests, scripts, coping plans) while the client merely reacts, approves, or requests edits.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Occasionally offers choices ('Which would you prefer?') and honors the client's edits to the letters, but the dominant mode is prescriptive — supplying test lists, scripts, templates, and behavioral prescriptions — with no explicit framing that she owns the decisions.

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Motivation and ideas are supplied almost entirely from outside; the counselor never asks what the client believes is happening, what she hopes for, or what life less ruled by her stomach would look like. Her own insights (nerves 'worn out,' willingness to talk to someone) are left unexplored.

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** The counselor repeatedly assumes the expert role, drafting scripts verbatim, listing tests to demand, and assigning homework. There is little acknowledgment that the client holds expertise about her own body, and her contributions shape the session only cosmetically.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** The client spontaneously offers rich change talk (pain-free wedding period, flares with husband's travel, willingness to try breathing exercises and behavioral therapy, 'at least I'm moving'), but the counselor never reflects, amplifies, or deepens any of it — responding instead with logistics.

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** When the client pushes back ('A breathing exercise doesn't fix a damaged gut'), the counselor briefly reframes but never uses double-sided reflections to connect her skepticism with her own stated openness. Sustain talk about distrust and fear of being labeled 'crazy' is accommodated rather than explored, arguably reinforcing avoidance.
