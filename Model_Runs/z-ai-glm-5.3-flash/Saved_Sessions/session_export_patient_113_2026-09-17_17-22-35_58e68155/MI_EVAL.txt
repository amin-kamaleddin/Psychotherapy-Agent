# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

---

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The counselor maintained a respectful, nonjudgmental tone throughout, normalizing potential setbacks and accepting the patient's blunt refusals. However, affirmations were somewhat formulaic ('Nice —' appeared repeatedly) and lacked individualized depth.

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Accurate complex reflections captured the patient's core barriers, such as recognizing that the issue was 'not the device itself but the added routine and annoying follow-ups.' Reflections rarely touched the emotional layer beneath the resistance, and the counselor moved quickly from reflection to option-offering.

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 5
**Comments:** The counselor maintained unwavering focus on the target behavior from opening to close, moving systematically from refusal to a specific plan, confidence rating, backup plan, in-session action, and follow-up. Even when the patient deflected, the counselor gently steered back without being pushy.

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The plan was genuinely co-constructed, with the patient's edits (Monday backup, script #2) changing the final outcome. At times the counselor front-loaded multiple suggestions before asking the patient's opinion, making the flow more suggestion-and-pick than mutual problem-solving.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 5
**Comments:** The patient controlled every element of the plan—alarm time, label, script wording—and his rejections (e.g., 'no two alarms same night') were accommodated without pushback. Setbacks were framed as normal, and no coercive or pressuring language appeared anywhere in the session.

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** While the confidence ruler and condition-focused questions elicited commitment language, evocation of the patient's deeper personal reasons for change was thin. The initial question about what mattered to him about staying healthy was deflected and never revisited, leaving motivation channeled through practical steps rather than values.

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The counselor consistently positioned the patient as decision-maker ('you're in charge,' 'pick or tweak one'), and his preferences substantially shaped the final plan. The counselor still drove the agenda with pre-packaged option menus rather than eliciting the patient's own ideas first.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Skillful use of condition questions, a confidence ruler with well-timed follow-up, and option menus moved the patient from outright refusal to concrete commitment ('Okay, alarm Sunday night... that's what we're doing'). Change talk was generated largely through option lists rather than deeper open evocation of the patient's own reasons for health.

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Sustain talk was consistently acknowledged and briefly reframed without argument, with quick pivots to concrete backup plans that reduced skip risk. Occasionally sustain talk was repeated back verbatim without a double-sided reflection linking it to the patient's stated willingness.
