# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

---

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** ☒ 4
**Comments:** Consistent warmth and non-judgment throughout ("supportive, nonjudgmental way"; normalizing embarrassment; "be kind to yourself"). Affirmations were supportive but leaned toward coach-like cheerleading ("You've got this") rather than deep, individualized regard; discouragement was often steered toward fixing rather than fully accepted.

---

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** ☒ 3
**Comments:** Some accurate reflections ("frustrating but very common"; "normal to worry it might fall apart"). However, empathy was often shallow and quickly pivoted to problem-solving — shame, history of failed attempts, and "noise in my head" disclosures were not deeply reflected. Complex reflections were rare.

---

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** ☒ 4
**Comments:** Clear, consistent focus on returning to physical activity; logical progression from barriers → concrete first step → follow-up → habit extension. Focus never drifted. Fell short of 5 because direction was counselor-imposed, pushing rapidly into action during contemplation stage.

---

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** ☒ 3
**Comments:** Partial collaboration — Rafael's ideas (walking, 6:30 pm, hot shower reward) were incorporated. However, interaction was noticeably unequal: counselor supplied most content (if-then cues, alarms, timers, backups) with little exploration of Rafael's own knowledge; checklists gave a coaching rather than egalitarian feel.

---

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** ☒ 3
**Comments:** Some genuine choices offered ("Which feels most doable?"). Undermined by prescriptive language ("Promise yourself no more than 10 minutes," "Try saying the cue out loud now") and step-by-step instructions delivered unprompted. Rafael's "I'm not promising anything" was not explicitly honored.

---

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** ☒ 2
**Comments:** Motivation largely supplied by counselor rather than evoked. Solutions proposed from the first substantive turn; few open questions eliciting Rafael's own reasons, values, or confidence. Change emerged through doing the plan, not through drawing out intrinsic motivation — limited trust that answers reside within the client.

---

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** ☒ 3
**Comments:** Some power sharing ("Want to keep 6:30 again?"), but overall stance was expert-driven: nearly every turn delivered numbered plans, tips, and instructions. Rafael's own insights (e.g., "waits around hoping I'll feel like it") were never explored to co-create strategies.

---

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** ☒ 3
**Comments:** Meaningful change talk was elicited — agreement to a concrete step, "I do want to," two successes, noticed benefits ("lighter," "slept better"), spontaneous contemplation of day three. However, change talk was rarely deepened with evocative questions ("How did you get yourself out the door despite being tired?") and was largely generated through the counselor's agenda.

---

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** ☒ 3
**Comments:** Sustain talk never reinforced, and some reframing offered ("Two days would be real momentum"). But doubts ("I usually say stuff like this and then don't do it"; "half waiting for it to fall apart") were met with tips and reassurance rather than double-sided reflections linking doubts to demonstrated successes. No consistent ambivalence-resolution technique.

---

**Overall Global Ratings Score:** 3.1/5

**Summary:** Strong behavioral activation skills — solid direction, concrete planning, consistent follow-up, and successful engagement of a contemplation-stage client who completed two walks. Warmth and non-judgment throughout. Key growth areas: (1) slow down and use OARS before action planning; (2) reduce prescriptive, expert-driven checklists; (3) deepen empathy with complex reflections of emotional content; (4) deepen change talk and use double-sided reflections for sustain talk; (5) co-create plans rather than prescribing them.
