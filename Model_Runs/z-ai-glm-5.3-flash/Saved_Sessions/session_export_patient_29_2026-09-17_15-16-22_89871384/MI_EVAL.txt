# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

---

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist maintained a warm, nonjudgmental, respectful tone throughout ('I'm glad you reached out,' 'Totally understandable'), treating past failed attempts and stress without criticism. Affirmations were somewhat brief and formulaic, with little explicit unconditional positive regard for David as a person regardless of quitting success.

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Early reflective listening was strong, accurately capturing both David's motivation (readiness 8) and ambivalence (confidence 6 due to stress). Empathy became less consistent later as reflections gave way to recommendation lists, and emotionally rich moments like David's insight that 'maybe it's really me' were acknowledged but not explored or deepened.

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 5
**Comments:** The session maintained exceptionally clear focus on smoking cessation from start to finish, with every exchange tying back to quitting—readiness assessment, medication selection, quit-date setting, role-play rehearsal, and a concrete 48-hour coping plan. There was no topic drift, and each response moved progressively toward actionable next steps.

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** There were collaborative elements such as joint agenda-setting, seeking David's reactions, and incorporating his ideas (telling the guys, coffee-ritual tweaks). However, the power balance tilted heavily toward the clinician during the medication discussion and bulleted advice blocks, with the session's overall structure largely clinician-architected.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Autonomy was partially supported through offering choices (quit date vs. taper, medication options) and asking what to tweak or practice next. The stance frequently shifted to recommendation-and-instruction mode, and when David said 'just tell me what to do,' the psychiatrist simply directed rather than reframing ownership back to him.

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The psychiatrist did elicit some motivation through rulers, strategy preferences, self-selected quit day, and role-plays that produced self-motivating statements. However, much of the session's content (medication details, coping techniques, the 48-hour plan, the wife's message draft) was supplied by the clinician rather than drawn out of David's own reasons, strengths, and past successes.

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The psychiatrist asked permission at the outset, requested input, and offered choices, but the middle of the session became notably expert-driven with detailed titration schedules and bulleted recommendations. When David handed over expertise ('just tell me what to do'), the psychiatrist largely complied by directing rather than restoring collaborative balance.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Skillful use of readiness/confidence rulers, eliciting preferences, and inviting David to identify doable strategies and role-play refusal lines produced strong commitment language ('I'm done,' 'I think I can actually do this Thursday'). Missed opportunities included not exploring why Thursday felt right, not asking what success would look like, and not deepening confidence talk with questions about David's own capacity.

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** David's sustain talk (stress, the restaurant relapse, worry about cravings) was acknowledged but not strategically reframed—barriers were sometimes mirrored back without pivoting to David's capacity to manage them, and expert solutions were offered before eliciting David's own. Positively, the psychiatrist never amplified or dwelled on reasons against change, but double-sided reflections pairing barriers with strengths were absent.
