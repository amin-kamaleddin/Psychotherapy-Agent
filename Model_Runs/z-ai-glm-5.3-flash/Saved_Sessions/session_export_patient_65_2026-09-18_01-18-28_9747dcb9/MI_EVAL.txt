# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

---

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 5
**Comments:** The psychiatrist consistently respected the patient's autonomy and perspective, accepting reluctance around phone calls and other concerns without judgment or argument. Support for the patient's intrinsic worth and potential was evident throughout, and the psychiatrist explicitly honored the patient's right to defer (e.g., waiting on the math until hearing back from the case worker).

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 5
**Comments:** Multiple reflections demonstrated deep, active understanding, including reframing worry as 'practical thinking' and 'real protectiveness, not just stress.' The clinician repeatedly captured the emotional undercurrent (fear of wasted effort, anxiety about the judge) beneath the practical content.

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 5
**Comments:** The clinician maintained a clear sense of purpose, gently steering the conversation from vague worry toward a concrete, achievable first step. Each turn built on the patient's own momentum toward goal-setting (identifying the case worker call, scheduling, and verification of hours).

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 5
**Comments:** The conversation was highly collaborative, with the clinician offering options and inviting the patient's input at every stage ('what's your gut say?', 'would that fit, or is there another way?'). The plan emerged from the patient's own ideas rather than being imposed, and choices were consistently shared.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 5
**Comments:** Autonomy was strongly supported: the patient was repeatedly given choices about pace and approach, and the clinician explicitly attributed progress to the patient ('that came from you'). Decisions about what to do now versus later were left entirely to the patient.

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 5
**Comments:** The clinician skillfully evoked the patient's own solutions, values, and self-efficacy — drawing out the food bank idea, the case worker's role, and the 'four hours, twice a week' schedule from the patient. Change talk was elicited naturally ('I think I could actually get moving on this'), with the clinician reinforcing the patient's own agency.

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 5
**Comments:** The interaction felt like a genuine partnership, with the clinician walking alongside rather than directing. Questions were offered collaboratively and the patient's answers shaped the direction of each exchange, including co-developing the strategy of bundling questions into one case worker call.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 5
**Comments:** The clinician consistently recognized and reinforced the patient's change talk — reflecting statements like 'now I kinda do' and 'I think I could get moving' as evidence of the patient's own capacity. Strategic questions ('what's your sense — is this enough to get moving?') actively cultivated commitment language.

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 5
**Comments:** Sustain talk (avoidance, fear of phone calls, forgetting things) was met with acceptance and reframing as self-awareness rather than confrontation. The clinician never argued against reluctance, instead neutralizing it through problem-solving that kept the patient's ownership intact.
