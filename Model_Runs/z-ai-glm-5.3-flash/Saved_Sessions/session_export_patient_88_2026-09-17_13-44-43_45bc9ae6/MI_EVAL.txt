# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

---

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Maintained a respectful, nonjudgmental tone throughout with affirming responses; the client explicitly noted being un-lectured. Acceptance occasionally felt formulaic ('Totally fair,' 'Totally reasonable') rather than deeply validating.

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Some accurate early reflections ('I hear you feel it's overblown') and brief validation of peer pressure ('Pressure sucks.'), but reflective listening was sparse and the session leaned toward question-asking and advice. Key experiences—peer belonging, the coach's push, and emerging ambivalence—went unreflected.

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Kept consistent focus on alcohol use and risk reduction from assessment through a concrete weekend plan with no topic drift. Direction was sometimes pursued via premature advice-giving and rapid multi-question bursts rather than guiding the client to his own goals.

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Permission-seeking and incorporation of the client's ideas (Diego, casual texting style) were genuine collaborative moments, but the overall structure was counselor-driven with pre-packaged checklists, scripts, and a 5-step plan. The client was often reacting to counselor proposals rather than co-creating them.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Repeatedly emphasized choice ('Would you like that?', 'Pick one tweak') and accepted partial commitment without pressure. Sometimes crowded the space with multiple pre-packaged options and never explicitly affirmed the client's capacity to lead his own change.

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** The weakest domain: motivation was largely supplied via counselor-generated tips and scripts rather than drawn from the client. His volunteered intrinsic motivators (coach, conditioning, peer respect) and his ambivalence ('could get me in trouble, but it hasn't') were never explored.

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Permission-seeking supported power-sharing, but the interaction frequently shifted into expert mode with checklists and pre-drawn plans. The client's best contributions (Diego, the coach) were bolted onto a template rather than allowed to shape the session.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Meaningful change talk emerged (DJ role, texting Diego, 'I'll try it this weekend'), notable for a precontemplative teen. However, it came mainly from checklist-driven structure, not deliberate evocation, and the counselor never deepened or reflected the client's own change statements.

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Appropriately avoided arguing or confronting, and normalized the client's enjoyment of drinking without debate. But there was limited strategic softening—no double-sided reflections linking sustain to change talk, and the final sustain talk ('that's on the plan, not me') went unaddressed.
