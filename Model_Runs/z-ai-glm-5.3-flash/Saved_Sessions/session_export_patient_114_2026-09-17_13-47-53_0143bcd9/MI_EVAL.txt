# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

---

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Respectful and nonjudgmental throughout; never labeled the patient an "alcoholic" and responded neutrally to minimizing ("everyone drinks in college"). However, almost no affirmations of honesty, courage, or effort; warmth was minimal and largely transactional. No explicit normalization of ambivalence was offered.

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Reflective listening largely absent; responses were dense with advice and questions. Only one early reflection ("That sounds frustrating and uncomfortable"). Emotional disclosures — blackout fear ("that freaks me out more than the hearing does"), worry about being "the weird one sipping water" — were met with new checklists rather than reflections. The values-vs-behavior conflict was never mirrored or explored.

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Consistent focus on the target behavior — conduct hearing, statement, and concrete risk-reduction steps. Clear next steps (read referral, enroll in course, weekend checklist). Weakness: direction served logistical problem-solving more than exploratory movement toward change; feelings and motivation received less attention than checklists.

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Counselor dominated with expert content: multi-point advice lists in nearly every turn, offering to draft documents and rehearse responses. Collaboration limited to menu-style choices ("pick 2–3"). Patient's ideas (softening statement wording, choosing limit and alarm) shaped the plan somewhat, but the process was largely counselor-driven advice-giving.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Partial autonomy support — asked "what would you like to start with?", offered strategy menus, and "want to pick 2–3 of these?" However, tone was directive ("I suggest 2" for the limit; prescriptive checklists). Patient's statement "I'm not trying to be a monk" was never explicitly honored with an affirmation of his ownership of the decision; he asserted autonomy himself ("We'll see").

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Minimal effort to draw motivation from within. Rarely asked open questions about the patient's own reasons, hopes, or confidence. Motivation addressed through external tools (limits, alarms, RA check-ins). Patient's self-generated motivation appeared, but was not elicited or expanded — a core MI skill largely missing.

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Counselor repeatedly assumed the expert role: drafting the hearing statement unprompted, prescribing drink limits, delivering itemized checklists. Patient's contributions were incorporated only after being offered by the counselor. Little recognition that the patient holds expertise about his own social life and triggers; interaction resembled consulting rather than power sharing.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Patient offered several organic change-talk statements ("I've thought about cutting back a little," "that freaks me out more than the hearing does," "maybe I keep it going"), but none were reflected, reinforced, or deepened. Each was followed immediately by practical advice. Change talk emerged despite, not because of, counselor technique.

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Did not argue with or amplify resistance when the patient said "everyone drinks in college" or "I don't want to be a monk." However, no active rolling with or reframing: skipping pre-gaming was presented despite stated social concerns, and his worry about standing out socially was never addressed with a double-sided reflection.

---

**Overall Global Ratings Score:** **2.6/5**

*Note: The session functioned more as practical case management and advice-giving than motivational interviewing. Strengths: clear direction and structure, nonjudgmental stance, concrete actionable plans (hearing statement, weekend checklist), and respect for the patient's limits. Key improvement areas: replace advice lists with open questions and complex reflections (especially after change-talk moments), affirm patient strengths and honesty, explicitly affirm autonomy, and use the patient's stated value of education to develop discrepancy collaboratively.*
