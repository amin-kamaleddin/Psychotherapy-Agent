# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

---

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 5
**Comments:** The interviewer consistently affirmed the patient's inherent worth and unconditional positive regard. Affirmations such as "that took something, even if it wasn't your idea" and "that's a sharper read than most people ever reach" convey deep respect for the patient's perspective and capacities, even during early resistance.

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 5
**Comments:** Reflective listening was precise and emotionally attuned. The interviewer accurately mirrored both sides of the patient's ambivalence ("some part of me does want that" alongside "don't get any ideas") and validated the difficult emotions behind the patient's guardedness, including the shame around past failed quit attempts.

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 5
**Comments:** The interview moved with clear purpose — from external motivation (wife's pressure) toward the patient's own internally generated reasons for change (peace at home, being "a decent man in her eyes"). Each question built purposefully on the patient's last statement, guiding the conversation toward change talk without forcing it.

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 5
**Comments:** The interviewer repeatedly positioned the patient as the expert ("You're the expert on your own life," "I'd rather hear your thinking than offer mine"). Advice was never imposed; the patient arrived at his own conclusions about what mattered most (wanting it for himself) and where to start.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 5
**Comments:** Strong, consistent support of patient autonomy throughout. Statements like "You don't owe anyone that, least of all me," "no decisions, no promises," and "that's yours to hold, at your pace" protected the patient's sense of choice, which directly facilitated the shift from defensiveness to openness.

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 5
**Comments:** Excellent evocation of the patient's own motivations and values. Open questions ("What would 'better at home' actually look like?" "What is that tired part of you really asking for?") drew out rich change talk: wanting peace, the house smelling better, standing taller, and willingness to "figure out the how."

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 5
**Comments:** The relational tone was that of equal partners. The interviewer explicitly declined the role of referee between the patient's conflicting parts and framed the exploration as the patient's own inquiry ("with your thinking, not mine"). The patient's shift from "ready to chew you out" to collaborative engagement reflects a strong working alliance.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 5
**Comments:** Skilled sequencing of questions yielded progressively stronger change talk: initial "maybe" statements evolved into desire ("more than I let on"), need ("tired of... feeling like the screwup"), ability ("maybe I'd be willing to figure out the how"), and a vision of change (house smelling better, wife knowing he's trying). Change talk was reflected back and deepened effectively.

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 5
**Comments:** Sustain talk ("It's just cigarettes," "who would I even be without them," "I'm not promising anything") was met with reflection and acceptance rather than confrontation or reassurance. The interviewer double-sided reflected ambivalence, normalized the patient's guardedness, and allowed sustain talk to resolve naturally into change talk — a hallmark of MI spirit.
