# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Nonjudgmental tone with expressions of gratitude ('thank you for telling me', 'that's a really hard, brave step'). However, warmth felt formulaic and repeatedly paired with risk checklist questions; client experienced this as not being heard ('like I'm a chart'). Positive regard present but undercut by scripted quality.

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Reflective listening limited. Brief acknowledgments ('I hear how unbearable this is') were followed by batteries of assessment questions, repeatedly experienced as invalidating. Missed key reflections, e.g., the client's crucial distinction of 'not wanting to die, just wanting the pain to stop.' A stronger empathic reflection appeared only near the end, after explicit protest.

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Maintained appropriate focus on suicidal ideation, means restriction, support contact, and monitoring risk. However, direction was delivered repetitively rather than progressively — the same plan/means/timeframe questions were asked at least four times, creating stagnation instead of momentum. Grief material followed but was not tied back to reasons for living.

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Collaboration improved over the session: co-drafting the text to her sister, asking permission before contacting others, and 'What do you want right now?' showed real power-sharing. The first half, however, was largely one-directional question barrages, and the client had to resist repeatedly before being heard.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Strong area. Repeated choices offered (coping vs. exploration vs. problem-solving; two text options plus 'draft your own'; talk vs. quiet presence). Client's autonomy honored on her own text wording, pill placement without fanfare, and refusal of grounding. Transparent limits ('I won't contact her without your OK'), though the repeated 'I may have to contact them' framing was experienced as coercive pressure.

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** 'What do you want right now?' elicited meaningful grief disclosure and the client's observation that talking made things 'quieter.' Text task drew out her sister as a reason for living. Inconsistent overall: hopeful statements ('part of me', 'it's quieter') were not developed, and reliance was placed on external safety steps rather than the client's own motivations.

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Partnership gestures present: offering choices ('Which would you send, or want me to make briefer?'), respecting her own wording, honoring refusal of grounding, and not making 'a speech' about the pills. Early stance was expert-driven and interrogative, with the client protesting the power dynamic ('You're not listening to me') before a meaningful shift occurred.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Responded well to spontaneous change talk — following up the sister mention with concrete text options led to the client composing and sending her own message. 'What do you want right now?' elicited meaningful material. Missed opportunities: the 'tiny part of me' statement was never reflected or amplified, and reasons for living beyond the sister were not explored.

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Avoided arguing with sustain talk ('nothing works') and did not reinforce it. The reframe 'it won't instantly take away the pain... doesn't mean it failed' reasonably addressed discouragement. However, few deliberate pivots toward change language; hopelessness was often met with checklist questions rather than reflective reframing toward her own ambivalence.

**Overall Global Ratings Score: 3.0 / 5**
