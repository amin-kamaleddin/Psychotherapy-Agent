# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

---

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The psychiatrist maintained a polite, nonjudgmental tone with no disapproval despite the patient's minimizing. However, affirmations were generic ('Good choice,' 'Nice work') rather than individualized to Andrei's specific strengths like his past smoking cessation or existing activity level.

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Reflective listening was nearly absent — responses were almost exclusively questions, advice lists, and recaps with virtually no reflections of content or feeling. The psychiatrist missed implicit cues like the patient's reassurance-seeking about alcohol and his discomfort with the scripted ask to his wife.

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The session stayed focused on hypertension and lifestyle behaviors without wandering, though direction was maintained through instruction rather than collaborative exploration. Critically, the psychiatrist dropped alcohol after the patient's minimization, leaving the key risk behavior unaddressed.

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** The interaction was largely expert-driven, with prescriptive plans and specific targets delivered without exploring the patient's own ideas. When the patient said 'You're the expert' and 'whatever you suggest,' the psychiatrist accepted this stance rather than redistributing expertise, and offered only constrained menu choices.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** There were token autonomy gestures (asking which steps to try, offering menu options), but these occurred within a counselor-imposed agenda. The psychiatrist supplied a full prescriptive plan immediately after the patient deferred authority and never emphasized that change is the patient's own decision.

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** The psychiatrist supplied expertise and motivation rather than drawing out the patient's own values, wisdom, or perceptions of the problem. The single confidence ruler question was a good technique but was not explored — the patient's answer ('the wife part that's out of my hands') contained important material left undeveloped.

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** The psychiatrist assumed the expert role for most of the session, steering the patient through a prebuilt plan with little impact from the patient's contributions. Some power sharing (choice of focus area and steps) prevented a score of 1, but patient input did not substantively shape the session.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** There was minimal evocation of change talk — no open questions about the patient's own motivations, concerns, or desired future. The patient's mild commitment language ('I'll give it a go') was met with generic praise rather than reflections to amplify commitment, and the counselor largely supplied reasons for change.

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** The patient offered substantial sustain talk ('that's normal, isn't it?', 'my diet's not bad', 'I don't see much changing there'), which was never reflected, softened, or reframed. The alcohol minimization was bypassed by sidestepping the topic rather than using double-sided reflections, leaving the key risk behavior unaddressed.
