# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

---

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 5
**Comments:** Consistent unconditional positive regard: directly addressed self-blame ('if the walking doesn't quiet the pain, that is not your fault') and reframed self-advocacy as strength rather than being difficult. No judgment anywhere in the session.

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 5
**Comments:** Highly accurate reflective listening throughout, including dual-sided reflections and catching the pivotal spontaneous disclosure about symptoms worsening when her husband is away. Empathy extended to the hidden burden of four years of self-blame.

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Clear focus on symptom management progressing to a concrete behavior change, with deft steering toward client-controlled patterns. The extended scripting of the doctor conversation was slightly tangential, and a stronger closing summary tying themes together would have earned a 5.

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 5
**Comments:** Fully collaborative interaction between equals: the plan frames walking as 'alongside' medical care, and all elements emerged from the client's own ideas. The client even co-authored the 'two fronts' approach.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 5
**Comments:** Consistent reinforcement of client ownership through open questions about what would help and what would make the first morning doable. Every plan element (going with neighbor, twenty minutes, day choice) was selected by the client with no pressure or coercion.

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 5
**Comments:** Core method was evoking motivation from within — the client generated her own evidence and change plan without the clinician arguing for the stress-illness link. The commitment talk ('I'll tell my neighbor tonight') was entirely self-authored.

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 5
**Comments:** Genuine power sharing with the client positioned as the expert ('You know your body better than anyone'). Session turning points — husband-away pattern, wedding memory, neighbor's invitations — all came from the client's own words.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 5
**Comments:** Skillfully evoked and developed change talk, turning the husband-away observation into client-generated solutions and a concrete commitment. Trajectory from ambivalence to 'Twenty minutes. I'll tell my neighbor tonight' is excellent MI practice.

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Generally avoided arguing with sustain talk, validating the reality of her pain while redirecting toward change. However, extended engagement with testing and cancer fears kept some focus on the status quo; explicit double-sided reflections pairing sustain with change talk would have strengthened ambivalence resolution.
