# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

---

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Consistently respectful and nonjudgmental toward the patient's resistance, accepting his harm-reduction goal and framing slips as feedback rather than failure. Falls short of 5 due to somewhat procedural warmth and few explicit affirmations of the patient's strengths.

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Early reflections accurately captured the patient's ambivalence, but reflective listening faded as the session became dominated by information-delivery and logistics. Emotional cues about partner nagging, fear of irritability, self-criticism, and attachment to the hand-to-mouth ritual went largely unreflected.

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Maintained clear focus on smoking reduction with steady stepwise progress from exploration through a concrete plan and scheduled check-in. Not a 5 because the focus stayed on logistics rather than motivation, with no closing summary tying the plan to the patient's values.

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Started collaboratively with permission-seeking, but the balance tilted toward the clinician, who authored the weekly plan and ritual substitutes for approval rather than co-creating them. The patient's own expertise about his patterns was acknowledged but not integrated into plan design.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Repeatedly affirmed the patient's choices and accommodated his self-defined goal of cutting down rather than quitting. Falls short of 5 because heavy information-giving at times crowded out the patient's decision-making space, and his capacity for self-direction was rarely explicitly affirmed.

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Motivation was assumed to follow from logistics rather than drawn out from the patient; his own reasons for change were never explored. The confidence ruler was well-timed but the clinician did not follow up on why the patient chose a 7, and most change talk emerged from the patient's own reasoning rather than deliberate elicitation.

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Collaborative gestures like permission-seeking and agenda negotiation were present, but the clinician quickly slipped into expert-education mode with detailed pharmacological information and little input from the patient's own knowledge. The plan was largely counselor-drawn rather than built on the patient's ideas.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The confidence ruler elicited change talk and a specific commitment, and open choice questions prompted the patient to select options. However, evocation was shallow—the clinician never explored the reasons behind the confidence score, asked about the patient's own motivations, or deepened his spontaneous change talk.

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Did not argue against sustain talk and effectively reframed slips as information, softening the patient's all-or-nothing fears. However, the clinician passively accommodated sustain talk (e.g., the unwind ritual emphasis) rather than actively pivoting it toward change with double-sided reflections.
