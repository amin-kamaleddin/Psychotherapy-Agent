# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

---

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The psychiatrist is respectful and nonjudgmental, never moralizing despite Adrian's defensive minimization. However, warmth and affirmations feel formulaic and scripted ('Proud you made a plan,' 'You got this'), and genuine individualized positive regard is sparse.

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** There are some accurate reflections, such as 'weekends, friends, and the rush fill a gap' and acknowledging the infection was unpleasant. Reflections remain shallow and the clinician misses rich emotional material (secrecy from his mother, uncertainty about whether he misses the drug or the socializing), often pivoting quickly to advice.

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The session maintains clear focus on weekend meth use and narrows successfully to a concrete plan: Saturday 8–11 gaming, skipping Danny's, clinic follow-up, and a Sunday check-in. The score is limited by prescriptive, checklist-driven steering rather than guided, client-driven goal clarification.

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** There are collaborative moments—offering a menu of options and accepting Adrian's self-authored plan and backup (Marcus)—but the interaction skews expert-driven, with monitoring protocols and checklists supplied without negotiation. The plan ends up Adrian-shaped mostly because he pushed back, not through systematic power-sharing.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The psychiatrist offers genuine choices and respects some of Adrian's stated limits (no gum, texting Danny himself). Autonomy support is undermined by repeated prescriptive instructions given against Adrian's explicit wishes ('I heard you the first three times').

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** One open question ('what would feel doable to try?') evoked useful planning talk, and Adrian produced meaningful insights spontaneously. However, the clinician relies heavily on dispensing information rather than deliberately eliciting Adrian's own reasons for change, values, or worries.

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The psychiatrist invites Adrian's input and offers choices, but much of the session is advice-dense monologue that Adrian pointedly resists ('I don't need you writing my texts for me'). Adrian is not sufficiently treated as the expert on his own life; his insights about boredom and social triggers surfaced late and were underleveraged.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Adrian produced meaningful change talk ('sitting in that clinic with that infection sucked,' 'maybe not using at Danny's place,' 'part of me kind of wants to see if I can do it') and developed commitment through a concrete experiment. Change talk emerged largely from Adrian's own ambivalence rather than deliberate MI evocation or amplification of motivational statements.

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The psychiatrist never argues with sustain talk and offers a reasonable double-sided reflection ('fill a gap... change feels pointless. You're also clear that the infection was unpleasant'). However, sustain talk is often bypassed rather than strategically softened, and frequent pivots to prescriptive harm-reduction advice shift focus toward continued use rather than resolving ambivalence.
