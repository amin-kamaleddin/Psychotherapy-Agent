# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

---

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Warm, nonjudgmental tone throughout; the psychiatrist validated feelings and normalized doubt without labeling or disapproval. However, explicit affirmations of the client's own strengths and efforts (e.g., her persistence working out daily) were largely absent.

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Occasional accurate reflections were made, but many responses pivoted quickly from empathy to advice and bulleted action plans. Deep emotional disclosures — loneliness, maternal criticism, shame ('something's broken in me') — were never deeply reflected before problem-solving.

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 5
**Comments:** Consistent, clear focus on the weight-loss plateau and motivation/adherence with no topic drift. The session progressed logically from assessment to a concrete, behaviorally specific plan with follow-up.

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Some collaborative elements existed (asking permission, checking preferences, incorporating her choices), but most problem-solving was done by the psychiatrist who presented finished solutions. A stronger approach would co-construct plans ('What do you think might work?').

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Repeatedly offered choices ('Which sounds most helpful?', 'pick or tweak') and respected the client's decisions without pressure. Autonomy was somewhat undercut by the volume of clinician-generated prescriptive plans and little emphasis on the client owning her change process.

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Relied primarily on information delivery and advice rather than drawing motivation from the client; open-ended evocative questions about her reasons, hopes, or confidence were almost entirely absent. Motivation resided more in the psychiatrist's instructions than in elicited client values — the core MI deficit of this session.

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The psychiatrist positioned the client as expert in key moments and let her shape some decisions (choosing measures, the casual script). However, the interaction was heavily advice-dense with repeated bulleted prescriptive lists in an expert instructional mode — clinician-as-plan-writer rather than genuine power sharing.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The client generated meaningful change talk ('I want to actually stick with something for once'), aided partly by framing the plan as an experiment and offering choices. But the psychiatrist failed to reinforce or deepen these statements with evocative questions; change talk emerged despite, not because of, skillful evocation.

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Sustain talk was not amplified, and one decent reframe (experiment = data, 'learning instead of failing') effectively softened her doubt. However, no double-sided reflections were used, and hopelessness was often bypassed rather than resolved — e.g., immediately shifting to measurement strategies after 'I don't want to get my hopes up again'.
