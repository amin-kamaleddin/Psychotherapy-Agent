# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

---

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Nonjudgmental and respectful throughout, with no lecturing or shaming despite the patient's minimizing. Warmth was somewhat brief and functional, with few explicit affirmations of the patient's honesty or strengths.

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Occasional accurate reflections early on were shallow and repetitive, and reflective listening largely disappeared as the counselor shifted to dispensing scripts. Missed opportunities included reflecting the patient's emerging ambivalence and the meaning of his mother's mistrust, notably when he asked 'You don't even care if I smoke?'

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Effectively followed the patient's stated priority (mother conflict) and moved coherently toward a specific, measurable plan. The cannabis use itself was somewhat sidestepped, which is defensible given precontemplation but leaves the target behavior only indirectly addressed.

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Some collaboration occurred (asking the patient's preference early, adapting the script to his feedback), but much of the session resembled a consultant delivering pre-made plans. True co-creation was largely absent until the role-play, where the patient generated his own proposal.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Strong autonomy language ('your call,' 'make your own choices') with options offered rather than mandates, plus transparent confidentiality limits. Autonomy support often took the form of handing over pre-made scripts rather than reinforcing the patient's ownership of change.

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Motivation was treated as something to be equipped rather than evoked — nearly all content was counselor-supplied, with little exploration of the patient's own values, reasons, or hopes. The patient's spontaneous change talk occurred despite, not because of, evocative questioning; this was the session's central MI weakness.

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The counselor framed himself as a supporter rather than a lecturer, but repeatedly occupied the expert role by generating scripts and plans for the patient. The patient's contributions modified details while the core strategy remained counselor-authored.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** The change talk that emerged was client-generated rather than systematically evoked; the counselor rarely asked open questions about the patient's own reasons or confidence for change. No commitment-strengthening or scaling questions were used, and the counselor supplied solutions instead of drawing motivation out.

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The counselor appropriately avoided amplifying sustain talk or lecturing about cannabis, but did not actively redirect ambivalence using double-sided reflections. Sustain talk ('I'm not saying I'm gonna quit') passed largely unaddressed, and harm-reduction information was delivered didactically rather than woven into reflective pivots.
