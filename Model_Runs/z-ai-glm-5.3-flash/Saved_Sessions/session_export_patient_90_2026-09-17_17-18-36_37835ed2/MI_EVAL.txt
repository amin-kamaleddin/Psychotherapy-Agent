# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

---

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Maintained a respectful, nonjudgmental tone and immediately accepted the dog as nonnegotiable without pushback, with some implicit affirmations ('good call', 'you've got this'). Affirmations were somewhat sparse and formulaic, and opportunities to validate Marcus's emotional burden and attachment to Buster were missed.

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Opened with a decent reflection about Marcus feeling overwhelmed and acknowledged financial and dog-related concerns. However, significant vulnerable disclosures — 'maybe I've been doing it wrong all these years,' nighttime inhaler scares, and fear of 'blowing up my whole life' — were largely skipped over in favor of jumping to logistics.

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 5
**Comments:** Maintained excellent, consistent focus on asthma management from the opening question through to a concrete, time-bound three-part action plan. Each turn moved progressively toward specific commitments, and Marcus left with a clear plan and follow-up intention.

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Used collaborative framing, asked permission before offering information, and adjusted the plan based on Marcus's requests (spacer question, insurance wording). However, Marcus's own ideas rarely originated plan content — he functioned mostly as a selector among counselor-generated options rather than a co-author.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Repeatedly framed choices as Marcus's to make, used 'if any' phrasing, and respected his pacing about sending the message tomorrow. Advice density (scripts, exact technique steps, sticky notes) occasionally tipped toward directive helpfulness with little explicit reinforcement that change belongs to Marcus.

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** The weakest domain — relied heavily on providing information, scripts, and prescriptive steps rather than drawing out Marcus's own motivations and solutions. The 0–10 confidence question was the lone strong evocative move; Marcus's stated reasons (wife sleeping better, lower costs, Buster staying) and his fear of upending his life were never explored or expanded.

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Asked permission, offered choices, and incorporated Marcus's selections into the plan. However, the counselor frequently slipped into an expert/advisor role, supplying scripts, technique steps, and reminder notes with minimal input — Marcus himself noticed ('you got a sticky note and a text to send').

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Elicited meaningful change talk ('keeping the dog out of the bedroom... that one's easy,' confidence rating of 7) and the confidence scaling question was a good evocative move. Missed chances to deepen it — no exploration of why confidence was a 7, no elaboration on Marcus's own motivations, and no commitment language evoked; the counselor supplied most of the plan.

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Effectively avoided amplifying sustain talk, pivoting from barriers (finances, appointment fatigue, doubts about cheap inhalers) toward actionable options. One caution: the opening turn offered several problem-focused options at once, but overall concerns were redirected toward practical change without dismissal.
