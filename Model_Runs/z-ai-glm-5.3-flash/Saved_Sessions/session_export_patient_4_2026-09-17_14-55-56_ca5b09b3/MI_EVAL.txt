# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

---

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Consistently respectful and nonjudgmental; accepted the patient's correction ('family history — important') without defensiveness and updated the record as she wished. Warmth is somewhat procedural, with few explicit affirmations of her as a person.

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Adequate reflective efforts ('exhausting and maddening'; 'worse when alone makes complete sense'), but empathy is inconsistent and often overshadowed by task-focused menu questions. Deep emotional disclosures—cancer fear, lost work and social life—received logistics or data entry instead of attuned reflection.

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Clear, consistent focus on building the symptom timeline and preparing for specialist evaluation, with concrete detail advancing each entry. Direction is oriented toward assessment/documentation rather than a change goal, which limits it within broader MI aims.

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Genuine co-construction: the timeline was built entry by entry with her corrections and approval, and her framing ('busy, not stress') shaped the record. Collaboration remained within a clinician-designed template, and her request for the professional opinion reverted to expert delivery.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Repeatedly offered choice ('Which would feel most helpful?') and accepted her reframing without pressure toward a psychological explanation. Choices were counselor-generated menus rather than open exploration of what she wants to change.

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Motivation was not elicited from within the client; the counselor provided resources (test lists, templates) rather than drawing out her reasons or hopes. Her self-generated insights—symptom-free at the wedding, worse when alone, 'I can't argue with my own writing'—were recorded but never developed through evocative questions.

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The patient's contributions shaped the record and she felt understood ('You understood me'), but the counselor repeatedly positioned himself as provider of menus and templates. Her 'You're the professional, tell me honestly' reveals the expert role still resided with the clinician.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Change talk was limited to pursuing medical tests; the counselor did not deepen her emerging recognition of the stress-symptom pattern. Her pivotal spontaneous insight ('I can't argue with my own writing') received no response at all.

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Skillful, non-confrontational handling of 'it's not in my head' via reframing ('stress can change symptoms without meaning it's just psychological') and adopting her preferred wording. A 5 would require more strategic redirection toward change, as much of the session accommodated the biomedical focus.
