# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

---

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist maintained a warm, respectful, nonjudgmental tone with consistent validation of the patient's fears (e.g., 'That makes sense and it's understandable'). However, affirmations were somewhat formulaic and brief, with little deep normalization of years of avoidance or explicit unconditional regard.

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Accurate reflections captured both content and process, such as 'fear of failing keeps you from starting, and that worry feels like a heavy, exhausting weight.' Deeper emotional layers like shame about years of postponement and perfectionist identity were not fully explored, and reflections were often quickly followed by problem-solving.

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 5
**Comments:** The psychiatrist maintained exceptionally clear focus on the target behavior, steadily progressing from exploration to a fully specified action plan (Sunday 9:30 study block, 10-minute timer, breathing cue, sister's check-in, sticky note) without topic drift.

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The tone was friendly and the patient's contributions shaped the plan (choosing time, adjusting the check-in, selecting the affirmation), but the psychiatrist drove the agenda and supplied most solutions. The patient primarily approved or slightly modified counselor-generated ideas rather than co-creating as an equal expert.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Some autonomy-supportive elements existed ('Which morning and time feels least scary?'), but much of the session was advice-heavy with the psychiatrist proposing the schedule, message wording, and coping sequence. Choice was offered between counselor-generated options rather than fostering the patient's own solutions.

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The confidence scaling question with a follow-up successfully evoked the patient's idea to change the check-in to encouragement, and she was asked what would make ten minutes doable. However, most motivation and solutions came from counselor suggestions, and the confidence question came very late in the session.

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The psychiatrist invited the patient's input and incorporated her ideas (morning preference, sister check-in adjustment, affirmation choice), but repeatedly supplied the actual content including drafting the message and prescribing the breathing protocol. The counselor functioned largely as plan-designer, limiting true power sharing.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Change talk emerged largely from the patient spontaneously ('Maybe ten minutes is doable', 'I'd feel kind of proud if I did it') and was affirmed, plus a well-placed confidence scaling question was used. However, there was limited deliberate evocation of desire, ability, or reasons language early on, and missed opportunities to deepen her spontaneous change talk.

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The psychiatrist did not reinforce substantial sustain talk ('if I miss it, that's just another failure') and effectively reframed the ten minutes as a low-stakes experiment rather than a test. However, reframes were sometimes delivered as quick suggestions rather than double-sided reflections that first legitimized the sustain talk, with limited strategic softening of the all-or-nothing schema.
