# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

---

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Consistent respect and warmth without judgment, even when the patient disclosed stockpiled pills and past attempts ("Thank you for answering — that was really honest"). Normalizes ambivalence and affirms efforts ("You were really brave to send that"). Falls short of 5 due to somewhat formulaic, list-based warmth and limited explicit unconditional regard beyond crisis-safety phrasing.

---

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Solid reflective listening ("I hear how exhausted, scared, and stuck you feel"; "I can hear relief and surprise"). Reflections accurately capture the patient's emotional state but are often brief and immediately paired with safety questions, limiting depth. Missed opportunities to reflect more deeply on feelings of being a burden, loneliness, and the grief of closing the "exit door."

---

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 5
**Comments:** Exceptionally clear focus on the immediate target: safety regarding suicidal ideation and means restriction. Every turn advances the goal — risk assessment, securing the pills, activating social connection, grounding, contingency planning. No topic drift; each step builds logically, culminating in concrete completed actions (pills stored, text sent, cousin coming over).

---

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 5
**Comments:** Genuine partnership between equals. Asks permission before direct questions, stays present during hard tasks, co-constructs text drafts the patient edits to fit her voice, and negotiates contingency plans. Key decisions — pills-first sequencing, draft selection, "call, voice to voice" fallback — all originated from the patient.

---

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Actions repeatedly framed as the patient's choice ("Which would feel most doable?", "pick or tweak," "if you want"). Patient allowed to reject the word "suicidal," decline the ER, and choose her own fallback plan. Crisis constraints led to some binary counselor-generated options, and autonomy affirmations were less frequent in moments of doubt (e.g., fear of being "a burden" was not met with explicit reinforcement of self-direction).

---

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Notable weakness. Questions were overwhelmingly closed-ended risk items and forced-choice logistics; little effort to elicit the patient's reasons for living, hopes, or what the "small part" that wants help is attached to. Counselor supplied the solutions (drafts, grounding, safety script) rather than evoking them. Some credit for evoking her own contingency plan and inviting her to say what she needs to her cousin.

---

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Conveys that expertise resides with the patient — repeatedly asking which option "feels most doable," respecting her rejection of drafts, honoring her sequencing preference. Her contributions visibly shaped the session. A 5 is withheld because some turns were structured as counselor-proposed menus rather than open co-creation, with clear directive leadership in crisis management.

---

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Change talk was noticed and affirmed ("maybe I could put them somewhere harder to get to"; willingness to text her cousin; "today someone actually stayed"), with affirmations reinforcing her movement. However, change talk was not deliberately evoked or deepened — questions were almost exclusively safety- and logistics-focused, with no open exploration of reasons for living or hope for the future.

---

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Sustain talk ("Nothing works," "I'd feel pathetic sending it") was not amplified or reinforced, and the counselor gently pivoted toward doable actions. However, sustain talk was rarely engaged directly — no double-sided reflections or reframes. Statements like "skills don't work" and "everyone keeps sending me to places like this" went largely unaddressed rather than being acknowledged and redirected toward her own evidence of change.

---

**Overall Global Ratings Score:** **3.6/5**

*Summary:* A competent, well-structured crisis intervention embedded in an MI frame. Major strengths: impeccable directional focus on safety, strong collaboration and partnership, consistent autonomy support, and warm, nonjudgmental affirmation of difficult steps. The session produced real behavioral movement: means restricted, social connection activated, and the patient noting "today someone actually stayed." Areas for improvement: (1) deepen evocation of the patient's own motivation and meaning; (2) lengthen reflections rather than converting them into risk questions; (3) engage and reframe sustain talk rather than avoiding it; (4) deliberately deepen change talk.
