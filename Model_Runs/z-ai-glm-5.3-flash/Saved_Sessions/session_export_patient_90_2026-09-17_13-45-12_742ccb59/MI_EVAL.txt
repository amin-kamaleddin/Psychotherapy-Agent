# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

---

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The counselor maintained a respectful, nonjudgmental tone and responded non-defensively to Marcus's boundary about the dog. However, formulaic safety disclaimers inserted into nearly every turn diluted acceptance, and genuine individualized affirmations of Marcus's strengths were largely absent.

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** There was one solid early reflection, but after that reflective listening nearly disappeared. Emotionally salient disclosures—his love for his dog, phone anxiety, skipped doses, fear of judgment—were repeatedly bypassed in favor of safety scripts, task lists, and intake questions.

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The counselor kept consistent focus on asthma management, covering symptoms, medication access, triggers, and appointment barriers, and moved Marcus toward concrete steps. However, direction came from task-checklist momentum rather than helping Marcus clarify his own goals and values.

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** There were genuine collaborative moments, including menu-based choices and interactive role-play shaped by Marcus's responses. Collaboration was inconsistent, though, as the counselor frequently shifted into expert mode with numbered plans, scripts, and multi-question barrages.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The counselor repeatedly offered menus of options and honored Marcus's preferences (telehealth, noon callbacks, keeping the dog). Autonomy support was undermined by a prescriptive undertone—delivering an action plan before Marcus asked—and little reinforcement of Marcus's ownership of his care.

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Motivation was largely supplied by the counselor's structure; no open-ended motivational questions, importance/confidence rulers, or explorations of Marcus's own reasons for change appeared. Change talk that surfaced was a byproduct of task momentum, not deliberate evocation.

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Marcus was positioned largely as an executor of the counselor's pre-built plan, with checklists and scripts dominating. While his requests did shape session activities, his expertise about his own life was never explicitly honored, and his vulnerable moment ('Did I sound like an idiot?') went unaffirmed.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Marcus offered meaningful change talk, but it emerged from practical task structure rather than skillful evocation by the counselor. When Marcus expressed tentative willingness, the counselor moved straight to logistics instead of reflecting, deepening, or affirming the change talk.

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The counselor creatively sidestepped the biggest sustain talk trap by offering trigger-reduction alternatives that didn't require rehoming the dog. However, Marcus's minimizing, medication nonadherence rationale, and the firm dog declaration were never directly reflected or softened—sustain talk was avoided rather than skillfully addressed.
