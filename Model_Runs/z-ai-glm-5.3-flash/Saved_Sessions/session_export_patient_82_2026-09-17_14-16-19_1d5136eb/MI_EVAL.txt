# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

---

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The counselor maintained a respectful, nonjudgmental stance, reassured confidentiality early, and validated Lukas's hesitation without shaming. However, explicit affirmations of his strengths (honors student, self-discipline, athletic commitment) were sparse beyond acknowledging he hadn't drank since.

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Reflections were mostly brief and surface-level, with the counselor quickly pivoting to checklists and advice after short acknowledgments. Deeper emotional content — fear of being 'the boring one,' the tension between friendship and goals — received validation but little exploration of the meaning underneath.

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The session stayed tightly focused on the target behavior and progressed logically through risk assessment, values, refusal skills, role-play, exit planning, and commitment. Transitions between strategies were occasionally abrupt, and deeper ambivalence exploration was skipped to maintain tactical momentum.

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Strategies were offered as menus, decisions were delegated to Lukas, and the role-play was co-constructed with him shaping his own refusal line. However, several long, advice-dense responses (ready-made plans, pre-drafted texts) positioned the counselor as the primary solution-generator rather than an equal partner.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 5
**Comments:** Autonomy support was consistently strong: the counselor repeatedly offered choice, explicitly validated Lukas's harm-reduction stance without moralizing, and honored his request to keep parents and coach out of it. Lukas's sense of ownership was evident in his commitment language ('the stakes are mine... no promises though').

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** There were genuine evocative moments yielding client-generated motivation (varsity, mental clarity), but much of the session relied on counselor-supplied strategies and scripts rather than eliciting Lukas's own solutions. Drafting the text to Marcus and the exit plan himself rather than asking Lukas to generate them capped this rating.

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Lukas was positioned as decision-maker throughout, and the role-play was an effective collaborative, experiential method that produced his own authentic refusal line. The score falls below 5 because the counselor supplied most strategies rather than primarily drawing solutions from Lukas, though Lukas did contribute the Marcus idea and his own line.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The counselor effectively elicited strong change talk with targeted questions ('what's one concrete reason you want to stay alcohol-free?') and secured commitment language ('So the alarm, definitely'). Some motivational moments weren't deepened — e.g., Lukas's 'maybe I won't even want to' was met with post-season logistics rather than a reflection amplifying his growing confidence.

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The counselor avoided arguing with sustain talk and occasionally validated it, preventing resistance. However, sustain talk was often left unaddressed rather than strategically reframed — 'one party a month isn't a big deal' was met with refusal scripts instead of a double-sided reflection connecting his belief against his own goals ('I worked too hard to blow that').
