# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

---

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Consistently respectful and nonjudgmental—validated the patient's stance on drinking and his reluctance without lecturing or disapproval. However, warmth occasionally felt formulaic, and individualized affirmations of the patient's strengths (e.g., his honesty about slips, his fatherhood) were rare.

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Reflections were generally accurate (e.g., capturing his frustration with the prior doctor and the disconnect between results and lived experience) but stayed at the content level and rarely reached emotional depth. Minimization and defensiveness were accepted at face value rather than gently explored, and phrases like 'That makes sense' were repeated nearly verbatim.

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The session began with referral targets but drifted to sleep, and smoking and abnormal liver results were never revisited after the patient reintroduced drinking himself. A stronger session would have used summaries to maintain a thread connecting sleep, alcohol, and the referral concerns.

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Behaved as an equal partner, offering menus rather than directives and deferring to the patient's judgment; the patient visibly shaped the session's direction and plans. Collaboration was sometimes reduced to repetitive option-listing, and key ideas (two-drink cap, breathing exercise) were therapist-generated rather than co-created.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 5
**Comments:** Repeatedly reinforced patient choice with genuine opt-outs ('or we can leave it') and no pressuring language—the patient explicitly said 'I appreciate you not pushing it.' Minor caveat: frequent menu-offering risked feeling scripted rather than reflecting deep trust in self-direction.

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Patient-generated plans (nap, breathing-with-wife, soft-drink experiment) emerged authentically and the clinician never argued for change. However, the patient's own reasons for change around smoking or alcohol were never actively evoked—no values questions, no curiosity about his Sunday-morning regret.

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Consistently positioned the patient as the expert on his own life, asking 'What would be most helpful right now?' and letting his contributions steer the session. The clinician sometimes drifted into option-provider rather than co-thinker, and the patient's expertise was implicitly rather than explicitly affirmed.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Elicited genuine self-generated commitments (Saturday nap, breathing exercise with wife, tentative soft-drink experiment), which is a real strength. But this change talk was never deepened—no values exploration, confidence scaling, or commitment consolidation, especially around drinking where a promising opening ('That's when it matters, right?') went unexplored.

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Never reinforced or argued against sustain talk, reflecting the patient's minimizations neutrally and offering low-pressure alternatives. However, there was little strategic softening—no double-sided reflections pairing sustain talk with his stated values, and sustain talk largely lapsed rather than being transformed.
