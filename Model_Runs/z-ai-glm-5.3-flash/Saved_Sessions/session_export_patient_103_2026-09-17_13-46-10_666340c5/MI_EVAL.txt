# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

---

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 4/5
**Comments:** Consistently validated the patient's anger without judgment ("You're justified to be angry," "your time matters"), even during hostility. However, validation was somewhat formulaic and repetitive, and the counselor jumped quickly to option menus rather than fully honoring reluctance.

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 3/5
**Comments:** Some accurate reflections ("You sound exhausted, rightly angry, and worn down"), and the home-life pivot connected emotionally. But the counselor largely bypassed the emotional core of repeated sustain talk ("nobody in that building gives a toss"), responding with option menus instead; reflections stayed shallow.

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 3/5
**Comments:** Session stayed loosely focused and landed on concrete steps (car reset, apology to Adam). Early direction was weak, however — scattered option lists (grounding, complaints, telehealth, drafting messages) rather than a coherent focus on the target behavior (managing anger).

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 2/5
**Comments:** Interactions were largely counselor-directed: diagnostic-style option menus and step-by-step instructions rather than joint problem-solving. The patient rejected several suggestions before one aligned; little eliciting of his own ideas.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 3/5
**Comments:** Repeatedly asked "Which feels most helpful?" and offered choices, but always within counselor-defined menus. Advice was prescriptive (exact scripts, delivery instructions). Affirmation of the patient's own capacity was rare ("You're fixing it, not failing" was a lone positive).

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 2/5
**Comments:** Motivation was largely supplied via suggestions and scripts rather than elicited. One good evocative moment — asking about home life, which surfaced "That's not fair on them" — drove progress, but the counselor did not build on it with open questions about reasons, confidence, or success.

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 2/5
**Comments:** Counselor positioned themselves as option-provider with numbered menus and scripted lines, implying expertise resided with them. Patient contributions were incorporated only superficially; the car reset originated with the counselor, not collaboratively.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 3/5
**Comments:** The home-life question effectively evoked change-relevant reflection and ended with commitment language ("I'll try the car thing"). But this was largely incidental; no deliberate evocation or reinforcement (e.g., "What would be different if things went better?").

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 2/5
**Comments:** The counselor repeatedly reflected and amplified sustain talk ("You're right to be angry," "You're right — that's disrespectful"), essentially agreeing with reasons against engaging. No double-sided reflections or pivots to what the patient could control; "or I'll reschedule" even modeled walking away. Shift in focus came late, largely through the patient's own pivot to his kids.

---
**Overall Global Ratings Score:** **2.7/5**

The session de-escalated effectively and ended with a workable plan (car reset, apology to son), and the patient left calmer than he arrived — a genuine strength. The counselor was nonjudgmental and validating throughout. However, core MI skills were weak: validation repeatedly amplified sustain talk rather than softening it; the counselor functioned as an advice-giver with prescriptive scripts and option menus rather than a collaborative partner; and motivation emerged mostly by accident rather than through deliberate evocation. Improvement areas: use double-sided reflections to acknowledge system failures while pivoting to what the patient controls; ask open questions to elicit his own reasons and ideas; replace option menus with collaborative exploration; and affirm his capacities directly rather than only validating his anger.
