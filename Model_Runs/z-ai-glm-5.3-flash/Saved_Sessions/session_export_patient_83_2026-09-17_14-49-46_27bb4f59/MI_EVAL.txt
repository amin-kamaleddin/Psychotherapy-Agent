# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

---

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Maintained a respectful, nonjudgmental tone throughout, validating Mai's desire for clarity and affirming her plan without labeling or disapproval. Affirmations were somewhat generic and formulaic, lacking deep, individualized recognition of Mai's strengths or self-efficacy.

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Made some effort to understand Mai's perspective, but reflections were sparse and frequently overshadowed by rapid transitions into education and option-listing. Mai's emotional experience—feeling dismissed by her doctor, her relief at understanding—was acknowledged only superficially and never deeply explored.

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Maintained a clear, consistent focus on understanding and adhering to the new statin, with each exchange tied back to the medication and no aimless wandering. However, direction came from a counselor-set agenda rather than progressive negotiation, and some content (CK testing, jaundice) exceeded what Mai asked for.

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Asked for Mai's preferences at several decision points, but the interaction resembled a patient-education encounter more than a collaboration between equals. The counselor provided long information blocks, decided to draft the message, and supplied lab recommendations without first exploring what Mai already knew or wanted.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Consistently presented options rather than directives and respected Mai's decisions, such as switching to orange juice and handling the message herself. A 5 would require more explicit reinforcement of Mai's ownership of the entire change process rather than menu-offering.

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Made almost no effort to elicit Mai's own motivations, reasons, or confidence—no open questions explored why adherence mattered to her or how confident she felt. Motivation was generated through education rather than evocation, making this the weakest MI-consistent element of the session.

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Used permission-oriented questions that support a partner stance, but the session was dominated by expert-driven information delivery with long lists of side effects, lab recommendations, and drafted messages. Mai mostly received and confirmed information rather than co-creating the session's direction.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Indirectly supported change talk through clear education that helped Mai generate her own commitments ('I'll take one atorvastatin at night,' 'Orange juice from now on'). However, the counselor did not deliberately evoke or reinforce this change talk—no confidence questions or reflections amplifying her commitment language.

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Did not amplify or dwell on Mai's early sustain talk ('do I really need this? Pills aren't free'), which is good, but also did not actively soften or pivot it through double-sided reflections or reframing. The sustain talk faded as education resolved it—functional but passive rather than skillfully softened.
