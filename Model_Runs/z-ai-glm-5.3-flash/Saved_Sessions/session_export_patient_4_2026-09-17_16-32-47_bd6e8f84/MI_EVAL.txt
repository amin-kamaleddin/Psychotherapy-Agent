# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

---

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Consistently respectful and nonjudgmental; Aizada's rejection of the mental-health framing and fear of cancer are accepted without correction. Warmth is somewhat procedural, with explicit affirmation of her strengths coming only at the end.

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Reflections accurately capture her fear, exhaustion, and sense of being dismissed. They tend to be brief and quickly followed by option menus, limiting deeper emotional exploration of poignant disclosures (e.g., her mother-in-law, loneliness).

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Session stays focused on symptoms, medical validation, and practical steps toward being taken seriously. Some direction depth is lost when stress-linked insights (wedding, husband away) are redirected to logistics rather than explored.

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Inclusive, option-offering language and the role-play reflect strong collaborative process. Content generation is unevenly balanced, with the counselor proposing nearly everything and the patient merely selecting.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Choice is repeatedly emphasized, and the patient successfully vetoes or makes items optional. Dense menus and pre-drafted scripts subtly steer her toward the counselor's agenda, and final commitments aren't framed as her own decisions.

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** The counselor relies heavily on providing solutions rather than eliciting the client's own motivations. Key motivating moments—the pain vanishing at the wedding, worsening when the husband is away—were volunteered by the patient and passed over without open questions to deepen them.

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Input is invited and her choices incorporated, but the interaction remains expert-driven, with the psychiatrist generating nearly all content (checklist, script, wording) while Aizada stays mostly reactive.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Change talk emerged largely from the patient herself, supported by concrete, patient-chosen steps. The counselor rarely explicitly evokes or amplifies it (e.g., 'How confident are you that you could keep the log?') and heavily structures the plan instead of drawing commitments from her.

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Sustain talk ('It's not in my head,' 'fix the gut first') is acknowledged without argument, avoiding reinforcement. It is often sidestepped rather than softened—few double-sided reflections or strategic pivots, and the biomedical-vs-stress ambivalence is never explicitly resolved.
