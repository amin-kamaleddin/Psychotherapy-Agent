# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

---

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Consistently respectful and nonjudgmental, even when the patient minimized her drinking; validated her need for downtime and family norms. However, affirmations were brief and formulaic, missing chances to specifically affirm her strengths (e.g., successful smoking cutback, honesty about family history).

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Offered adequate reflective statements ('That sounds exhausting and frustrating') but stayed at a factual, summary level. Emotionally significant disclosures—guilt about wine replacing time with her son, the morning patience moment—were acknowledged only briefly rather than explored in depth.

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Maintained clear, consistent focus on alcohol use and stress management throughout assessment, goal-setting, and follow-up planning. Direction was sometimes overly agenda-driven, pushing toward a plan before adequately exploring motivation.

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Patient's own ideas (tea, sketching, texting Maria, social worker) were incorporated and follow-up timing was negotiated. But the core plan was delivered as a directive list of strategies rather than co-created, with little invitation for her input on which strategies would work.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Respected the patient's harm-reduction goal over abstinence, accepted her refusal to promise abstinence, offered choices, and framed the plan as a practical experiment. The prescriptive checklist emphasis somewhat undercut her ownership of the plan's design.

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Relied heavily on closed-ended assessment questions and then supplied advice, rather than eliciting the patient's own motivations and solutions. Her spontaneous motivations (more patience with her son, avoiding another failed quit attempt) were only briefly acknowledged instead of actively drawn out.

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** The session shifted quickly into expert-driven advice-giving, with a lengthy prescriptive strategy list delivered after minimal exploration. Though the patient's ideas were folded in, the framing conveyed the psychiatrist's expertise rather than drawing out the patient's own.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Change talk emerged spontaneously from the patient and the concrete plan functionally reinforced it. However, the psychiatrist passively received it—never reflecting back or amplifying her powerful statement about wanting more patience with her son, and asking no open questions to deepen it.

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Sustain talk ('two drinks isn't a problem,' 'everyone in my family drinks') was bypassed rather than worked with—no double-sided reflections, reframing, or normalization of ambivalence. The psychiatrist avoided arguing with it, but this was avoidance rather than skillful softening.
