# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

---

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 5
**Comments:** The psychiatrist consistently honors the patient's autonomy — he does not push diet advice, respects the patient's decision not to involve his wife's baking, and affirms his right to define success ('better than where you were'). The patient's self-critical streak is met with non-judgmental reframing rather than correction or confrontation.

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 5
**Comments:** Reflective listening is precise and emotionally attuned throughout, capturing both content and feeling ('I hear some weight in it, maybe even that self-critical voice, ready to pounce'). The clinician repeatedly reflects the patient's own words back in ways that deepen meaning, and the patient explicitly says the reflections 'land' and 'mean a lot'.

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 5
**Comments:** Every response keeps the conversation anchored to the patient's own goals — gym consistency, handling gym comparison, evening eating, and self-compassion. The clinician consistently steers away from perfectionism and comparison-based framing toward the patient's self-defined standard of progress.

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 5
**Comments:** The interaction is highly collaborative: nearly every turn ends with an open invitation ('what do you think might help?', 'what fits you?'), and all ideas — the pause, the headphones, the stocked kitchen — originate from the patient. The clinician acts as a guide rather than director, and the patient leaves having authored his own plan.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 5
**Comments:** The clinician explicitly returns ownership to the patient at every step: 'that reframe came entirely from you', 'you're handing yourself the credit for once'. Change talk is evoked and reinforced rather than prescribed, and the patient's plan is entirely in his own words — a hallmark of strong autonomy support.

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 5
**Comments:** Change talk is skillfully drawn out through open questions and strategic reflections ('what do you think would help you hold onto that reminder?'). The clinician highlights patient-generated insights — 'my reason, not out-lifting some twenty-year-old', 'the bad feeling almost becomes the reminder' — amplifying them without adding his own agenda.

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 5
**Comments:** The dialogue flows as a genuine back-and-forth in which the patient increasingly takes the lead, generating solutions and self-evaluations. The clinician builds on each patient contribution ('that might actually work for me', 'that's commitment in your own voice') rather than steering toward a predetermined conclusion.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 5
**Comments:** The clinician consistently recognizes, reflects, and deepens change talk: commitment language ('I'll talk to my wife this week', 'that's the plan') and behavioral intentions ('stop, stretch, feel my back') are each named and strengthened. Preparatory talk about reasons and ability ('my back hasn't felt this good in years') is likewise evoked and affirmed.

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 5
**Comments:** Ambivalence and self-doubt ('I can't promise I'll catch it every time', 'the diet's still the real battle') are met with acceptance and reframe rather than argument — e.g., 'you're not asking yourself to be perfect' and 'you called it a battle, not a lost cause'. Sustain talk is consistently neutralized without dismissing the underlying concern.
