# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

---

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 4 / 5
**Comments:** Warm, respectful, nonjudgmental tone maintained throughout. Directly countered the patient's self-doubt about laziness ("you're not being lazy — work is eating your time"), communicating unconditional positive regard without dismissing his concern. Acceptance was generally strong but not deeply individualized — affirmations like "That sounds like a strong, doable plan" were competent but somewhat formulaic, with few explicit affirmations of the patient's personal strengths or self-awareness (e.g., his insight into autonomy-based resistance to phone reminders).

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 4 / 5
**Comments:** Good reflective listening, particularly early on — accurately captured ambivalence ("unsure whether it's laziness or real barriers") and reflected "automatic, not willpower" and "all-or-nothing" concerns with genuine perspective-taking. Reflections became less frequent as the session moved toward planning. Emotional nuances — fear connected to his father's heart problems, discouragement and fatigue, self-questioning about laziness — were acknowledged once but not explored in depth.

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 5 / 5
**Comments:** Excellent, consistent focus on the target behavior (increasing exercise) across the entire session. Every exchange moved the plan forward: small step → place and time (lunch, far coffee shop) → start date (Monday) → success definition (3 of 5 walks) → cue and if-then fallback → rehearsal of the social commitment. No topic drift; each response ended with a question guiding the patient toward the next concrete detail.

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 4 / 5
**Comments:** Largely a joint problem-solving effort ("Two tiny options… which feels more doable," "we'll make it even smaller," "we can quickly rehearse the one-liner"). The patient's contributions materially changed the plan (far coffee shop, coworkers as cue, Dave as accountability), and confidence was checked in on repeatedly. Slightly below 5 because dense multi-question turns with pre-packaged suggestions, while efficient, tilted the exchange toward the counselor's agenda rather than fully co-created exploration.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 5 / 5
**Comments:** Consistent reinforcement of patient ownership ("Which feels more doable this week?", "Which of those would you most like to try first?"). Adapted immediately and without pressure when the patient rejected phone reminders and formal rehearsal, incorporating his preference for a casual invite to Dave. Respected his self-defined success criterion ("three times out of five, not perfect") and explicitly affirmed "not all or nothing" — strong support of client choice and self-direction.

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 4 / 5
**Comments:** Effectively drew out the patient's own motivations — father's heart problems, hatred of feeling drained, and desire for behavior that is "automatic, not willpower" all came from the client. The confidence ruler and "what would raise you from 4 to 7" question are textbook evocation techniques that produced measurable commitment growth (4 → 6 → 7 → 7.5). Deducted because the counselor at times supplied reasons and solutions (e.g., listing cues and accountability strategies) instead of persistently asking the patient to generate them.

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 4 / 5
**Comments:** Repeatedly positioned the patient as the expert ("Which feels more doable this week?", "What one cue will remind you to go?"); his own solutions visibly shaped the plan, demonstrating real power sharing. However, the counselor tended to front-load multiple preset options (10-minute walk vs. stretch, shoes by desk, calendar block), which occasionally steered rather than purely elicited — the patient often picked from the counselor's menu rather than inventing ideas unprompted.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 4 / 5
**Comments:** Skillfully evoked change talk via the confidence ruler, the "what would raise your confidence from 4 to about a 7?" question, and eliciting a specific success definition. The patient responded with clear change talk ("I'd like to try it," "I think Monday I can do this"), with confidence climbing from 4 to 7.5 across the session. Commitment was effectively consolidated by asking when he'd start and what he'd tell Dave. Points lost because solutions were occasionally offered too quickly, reducing opportunities for the patient to generate his own change arguments.

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 4 / 5
**Comments:** Sustain talk handled well through double-sided reflections ("It's normal to worry ten minutes won't matter and that late nights will derail you") and reframing ("you're not being lazy — work is eating your time"). When the patient worried "ten minutes feels almost pointless," the counselor pivoted toward making the behavior automatic rather than debating its value; no reinforcement of barriers or excuses. Slightly below 5 because some responses validated the barrier without actively pivoting the patient's attention back to his own reasons for change.

### Overall Global Ratings Score
#### **Score (1–5):** 4.2 / 5

---

*Summary (for supervisor file):* Strong MI session demonstrating solid intermediate-to-advanced skills. Major strengths: exceptional direction — systematic progression from ambivalence to a concrete, cued, if-then-protected plan (recurring 12:05 calendar block, casual invite to Dave, one-block fallback) — and excellent autonomy support, with the patient's preferences visibly shaping every element of the plan. Ruler questions and confidence-building were used skillfully, moving the patient from 4 to 7.5 confidence while eliciting genuine commitment language. Areas for improvement: (1) deepen emotional reflections — the father's cardiac history and self-questioning about laziness deserved fuller exploration; (2) reduce front-loaded option menus and multi-question turns to allow more client-generated solutions; (3) offer more specific, individualized affirmations of the patient's strengths rather than general plan praise. Overall: a competent, client-centered interview with clear forward momentum.
