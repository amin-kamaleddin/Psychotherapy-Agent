# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

---

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The counselor maintained a respectful, nonjudgmental tone throughout, validating the client's frustration rather than pushing back. However, affirmations were generic, missing chances to affirm specific strengths like her honesty or courage in discussing the topic with her partner.

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Some reflections landed ('You're torn—totally understandable') but were brief and formulaic, with little exploration of the emotional core of her ambivalence. The counselor frequently moved quickly from reflection to information delivery, leaving feelings like 'I'll talk to Marco tonight before I lose my nerve' unexplored.

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The session stayed focused on alcohol use during pregnancy and ended with a specific plan, timeframe, and scheduled follow-up. Some drift occurred into general health information (non-pregnant drinking guidelines, withdrawal warnings) that moved away from targeted behavioral work.

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The counselor offered choices and incorporated some of Isabella's ideas, but often presented pre-packaged solutions rather than building the plan jointly. The closing monologue of instructions ended the session in a directive rather than shared mode.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Autonomy was consistently honored, with choices offered and abstinence explicitly reframed as her decision, which resonated with the client. Prescriptive instructions ('commit to 1 week,' 'remove wine') and the 'I'm proud you chose this' statement carried subtle approval contingency and undermined full autonomy support.

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The client's motivations emerged largely spontaneously rather than through active evocation. The counselor never explored what the wine ritual meant to her, used no scaling questions, and shifted toward fact-provision instead of drawing motivation from within the client.

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Choice menus and implicit permission-seeking supported some power-sharing, but the counselor repeatedly assumed the expert role with counselor-generated plans and checklists. The client's expertise was never explicitly acknowledged, and 'I'm proud you chose this' placed the counselor in an evaluative position.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Conditions allowed change talk to emerge ('being a good mom is the most important thing to me'), but the counselor failed to strategically deepen it. No evocative questions, elaboration requests, or confidence/commitment scaling were used to amplify key moments.

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** The counselor responded to skepticism with counter-arguments and facts ('Anecdotes don't change population risk'), risking reactance in an autonomy-driven client. No double-sided reflections were used, and the evidence discussion took on a debate-like structure positioning the counselor as arguing for change.
