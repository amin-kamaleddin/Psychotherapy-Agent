# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

---

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Maintains a respectful, nonjudgmental stance throughout, validating frustration and affirming self-advocacy without dismissing the somatic focus. Warmth is somewhat formulaic, with little explicit normalization of the fear of cancer beyond the opening reflection.

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Accurate reflections early on, including a good reframe linking feeling better when busy to stress affecting symptoms. The session shifts to a checklist-driven style, leaving tearfulness, fear of cancer, and feelings of isolation under-explored.

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Session stays tightly focused on a concrete target — getting symptoms properly evaluated and preparing advocacy with Dr. Brown. Focus is maintained through lists and task completion rather than collaborative goal clarification around the stress-symptom link.

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Includes collaborative elements like rehearsing the script and offering options, but the flow is largely counselor-as-consultant delivering information. Counselor turns are dominated by instructions, leaving limited space for joint problem-solving or the patient's own theories.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Repeatedly offers meaningful choices, and the patient's decisions (firm script, sister coming, card) drive the plan. Tone sometimes presumes the plan before the patient chooses, and ownership of change could be affirmed more.

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** The one good opening question is never followed up with evocative exploration. Pivotal material — the wedding observation, worsening when her husband travels, and 'maybe my nerves play some small part' — is spontaneously offered by the patient but left unexplored in favor of advice-giving.

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Offers some genuine choice and power-sharing, but the interaction frequently becomes expert-driven with long lists of tests prescribed rather than explored. The patient's own ideas shape the plan only marginally.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Change talk emerges from the patient herself ('maybe my nerves play some small part,' 'something has to change') but is never noticed, reflected, or deepened. The key 'small part' admission is skipped over in favor of practical steps, with no open questions evoking desire, ability, reasons, or need.

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** The counselor neither reinforces nor actively softens sustain talk; 'I don't want anyone thinking this is stress' is met with implicit endorsement of the biomedical framing rather than a double-sided reflection. Sustain talk is passively allowed to dominate the agenda instead of gently pivoting toward ambivalence about the stress connection.
