# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

---

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist maintained a warm, respectful, nonjudgmental stance throughout, normalizing Matteo's ambivalence and never criticizing his minimizing. Affirmations were somewhat brief and formulaic, with little explicit recognition of his strengths beyond the plan itself.

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Strong reflective listening early on, including the double-sided reflection that directly prompted Matteo's insight ('it sounds like a lot when you say it like that') and an accurate reflection of the rounds/free-drinks risk. Reflections became less frequent later as the session shifted to practical planning, and the emotional meaning of peer pressure and social identity was not deeply explored.

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 5
**Comments:** The session maintained a clear, consistent focus on reducing drinking, linked directly to the injury and health context, with no topic drift. Each exchange built progressively toward a concrete, time-bound plan with a scheduled follow-up.

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The interaction was largely between equals, with Matteo's own ideas substantially shaping the final plan and the psychiatrist asking rather than prescribing at most decision points. Mid-session option-listing and rapid-fire tactic menus gave parts of the session a consultant-delivering-menu feel rather than fully co-created dialogue.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 5
**Comments:** Choice was consistently reinforced, with Matteo offered options and asked what felt best, and his 'I'm not promising anything' accepted without pressure. He retained full ownership of decisions, including his preference for in-person follow-up.

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Motivation was drawn from the client through open questions, choice of options, and the confidence ruler, which elicited Matteo's own problem-solving about raising his confidence. Evocation was somewhat technique-driven (rulers, menus) rather than exploring Matteo's deeper values and reasons for change.

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist consistently positioned Matteo as the expert on what was realistic for him, sought permission before sharing screening information, and Matteo's contributions genuinely shaped the plan. A mid-session burst of prescriptive suggestion lists edged toward an expert-delivering-options stance.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist effectively evoked change talk by asking what Matteo wanted and which options were realistic, producing clear commitments ('capping it at three... I can try it this week') and skillful use of a confidence ruler. Commitment language was not deepened further, such as exploring why the change matters to Matteo personally.

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The psychiatrist did not reinforce sustain talk and used double-sided reflections, but did not actively soften or reframe Matteo's minimization ('could've happened anyway'), his view of quitting as 'extreme,' or fear of roommate ridicule. These sustain statements were largely passed over rather than gently pivoted toward change.
