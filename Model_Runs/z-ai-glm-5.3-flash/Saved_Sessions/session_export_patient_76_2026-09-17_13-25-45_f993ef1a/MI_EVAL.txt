# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** ☐1 ☐2 ☐3 **☑4** ☐5
**Comments:** Consistent warmth and nonjudgmental regard ("Thank you for telling me," "You're doing really important work," "that's brave"). No labeling or criticism despite patient's minimization ("it's not that serious"). Affirmation is somewhat formulaic, and repeated safety disclaimers at nearly every turn feel procedural rather than personally warm — prevents a 5.

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** ☐1 ☐2 **☑3** ☐4 ☐5
**Comments:** Accurate reflections early on ("being tired all the time is really heavy") and one excellent complex reflection ("It makes sense it feels weird — you're not used to someone noticing"). However, session is dominated by checklists and directives. Deeper hopelessness ("Nothing lasts") and the meaning of "people usually don't notice" are acknowledged only briefly; "it's not the same" about alternatives is not explored empathically.

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** ☐1 ☐2 ☐3 **☑4** ☐5
**Comments:** Clear, appropriate focus on safety and reducing self-harm, with concrete steps followed through (box secured, text sent, timer set, urge rated 4/10). Strong on action; loses a point because immediate crisis management dominates and reliance on cutting as sole coping strategy is never explored as a change goal.

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** ☐1 ☐2 **☑3** ☐4 ☐5
**Comments:** Partial collaboration ("we can make a brief safety plan," "Want to make this together?", joint alphabet game; stayed present as requested). Power balance tilts expert-driven: plan content dictated, text to Priya supplied verbatim, patient complies passively ("Okay, okay"). Her own ideas do not substantially shape the plan.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** ☐1 ☐2 **☑3** ☐4 ☐5
**Comments:** Some choice offered ("what would you prefer to start with?", A/B/C options, "Is that okay?"), but pressure language present ("I strongly encourage you to secure the sharp items now... rather than waiting") and repeated directives. Some directness warranted in safety context, but ownership framing (e.g., "You chose to put them away") is absent.

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** **☑1** ☐2 ☐3 ☐4 ☐5 — *recorded as 2/5*
**Comments:** Motivation largely supplied by counselor's agenda and safety directives rather than drawn from the patient. Almost no open questions exploring her reasons for change, hopes, or values. Change talk ("lower than usual," "actually doing something") arises incidentally and is not developed; effort centers on instructing and distracting rather than eliciting.

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** ☐1 ☐2 **☑3** ☐4 ☐5
**Comments:** Some collaborative elements ("Want to make this together?", offering choices), but much of the session is expert-driven: bulleted safety plans, prescribed texts, prescribed grounding steps, repeated instruction. Patient's contributions influence the session only at the margins; her expertise about what helps ("the cutting actually works") is not drawn upon.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** ☐1 ☐2 **☑3** ☐4 ☐5
**Comments:** Some change behavior evoked: agreed to delay, secured sharp objects, texted Priya, tried ice and tea; reported "maybe a 4... lower than usual" and "actually doing something instead of the other thing." But this emerged mostly from action directives rather than intentional evocative questions; opportunities to deepen ambivalence into commitment language were missed.

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** ☐1 ☐2 **☑3** ☐4 ☐5
**Comments:** Sustain talk is not reinforced and moves quickly toward alternatives, appropriate in a safety context. However, sustain statements ("The cutting actually works," "Nothing lasts") are largely bypassed rather than acknowledged and gently redirected. No double-sided reflections or strategic pivots; responses proceed to the next concrete step, missing chances to resolve ambivalence.

---

**Overall Global Ratings Score:** **3.1/5**

Strong as an acute safety intervention (prompt risk assessment, grounding exercise, concrete safety plan, presence through the 30-minute delay, measurable progress). Weak as a motivational interview: dominated by checklists and directives rather than reflective listening and evocation; core ambivalence unexplored; sustain talk bypassed; autonomy and patient expertise underused. Improvement areas: (1) more complex reflections and open questions about her own reasons for change; (2) evoke rather than prescribe; (3) co-construct the safety plan using her ideas; (4) affirm her ownership of chosen steps; (5) use double-sided reflections to soften sustain talk while keeping the safety focus.
