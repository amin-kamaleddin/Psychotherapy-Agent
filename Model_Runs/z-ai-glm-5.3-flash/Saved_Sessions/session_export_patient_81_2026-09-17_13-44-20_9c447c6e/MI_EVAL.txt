# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

---

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Maintained a respectful, nonjudgmental tone despite the client's defensive statements ('I'm not an addict') with no labeling or confrontation. Affirmations were formulaic and lacked individualized warmth or explicit validation of the client's emerging self-awareness.

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** There were moments of accurate understanding (fear of losing visibility, money stress), but reflections were brief and quickly pivoted to questions or advice menus. Deep emotional content — fear of being 'boring Lukas,' shame over the vanished acting fund, numbing on 'alone Tuesdays' — was acknowledged but not deeply explored, and the opening bundled five intake questions mechanically.

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The session stayed anchored to substance use and its consequences (money, rent, auditions, relationships), steering toward concrete next steps that crystallized into two specific commitments. Occasional drift into financial problem-solving occurred without linking back to use.

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Offered menus of choices and asked permission, but rapid-fire structured questions and ready-made drafts created an expert-layperson dynamic. The client's own ideas did not materially shape the session content or plans.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Repeatedly affirmed choice ('pick one to start with,' 'whenever you prefer') and tolerated the client's stated boundary without pressure; the closing contract was entirely the client's framing. Fell short of 5 because suggested experiments and scripts were prescriptive rather than purely eliciting the client's own solutions.

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Asked some good evocative questions (fears of stepping back, what partying means, the readiness scale) that drew out motivations around acting and belonging. However, motivation was often bypassed in favor of supplying solutions, and the client's richest self-discovery ('which Lukas actually gets the dream?') was met with reassurance rather than deepened exploration.

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Gave the client real input through choices between options and experiments, but the overall pattern was expert-driven with pre-packaged scripts, schedules, and experiment menus. Little explicit acknowledgment that the client holds expertise on his own scene, identity, and social world.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Elicited meaningful change talk ('it does sound worse than I thought,' 'which Lukas actually gets the dream?'), but did not deliberately reinforce or deepen it — pivotal statements like 'I traded my reel for them' passed without affirmation or follow-up. No confidence questions or commitment-strengthening summaries of the client's own reasons for change.

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Did not amplify sustain talk and occasionally reframed ('stepping back doesn't have to be all-or-nothing'), but largely tolerated sustain talk without strategic double-sided reflections pivoting toward change. The discrepancy between the client's party identity and acting dreams — which he raised himself — was left unexploited.
