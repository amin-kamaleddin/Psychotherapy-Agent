# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 4/5
**Comments:** Respectful, nonjudgmental tone throughout; thanked the patient for his honesty and validated his perspective. Affirmations present ("Nice job with the script," "Good — that's a solid, doable plan"). Warmth was somewhat brief and technique-focused; deeper unconditional positive regard during patient pushback could be more explicit.

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 3/5
**Comments:** Some accurate reflections early on ("a private way to unwind after hard work is important"; the wife's pressure feeling heavy). However, the counselor shifted quickly to information-giving and menu options. Emotional openings (fear of failing, "it might flop," "sounds less defensive out loud") were missed; reflective listening was adequate but often shallow.

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 4/5
**Comments:** Clear, consistent focus on smoking — function of smoking, delay experiment, wife's nagging as a barrier, and concrete next steps with follow-up. Point lost as the wife-communication focus occupied a large portion without periodic summaries tying the work back explicitly to the patient's smoking goals.

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 3/5
**Comments:** Partial collaboration — patient picked options and co-authored the script. However, the counselor often presented pre-packaged solutions (numbered lists, two scripts) and retained control of content and direction ("Two quick, practical options," "Small tweak for firmer boundary"). Collaborative in form, counselor-driven in substance.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 5/5
**Comments:** Clear strength. Repeated explicit choices ("Which feels most useful right now?", "Which of these would you try now?"); "You don't have to buy the whole toolkit"; accepted the patient rejecting breathing exercises; permitted minimal commitments ("I'll try it once") without pressure. Script centered the patient's right to decide.

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 2/5
**Comments:** Rare and weak efforts to elicit the patient's own motivations. The counselor supplied information, tools, and scripts rather than probing personal reasons for change. Patient motivations emerged spontaneously (wife's support, health concerns, curiosity about the delay) but were not drawn out or developed.

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 3/5
**Comments:** Patient's expertise acknowledged; he authored his own script and chose options. However, the counselor frequently fell into expert/advice mode — bullet-pointed toolkits, physiological explanations, and wording corrections — with rapid, prescriptive pacing that somewhat undermined power-sharing.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 3/5
**Comments:** Conditions for change talk created via choices and commitment requests; patient produced meaningful change talk ("I'll try it once," "maybe worth mentioning to the doc," "maybe I'll flip through that pamphlet"). However, this language was not strategically deepened — no elaboration questions, no confidence exploration, and the strongest closing change talk (pamphlet) went unaffirmed.

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 3/5
**Comments:** The counselor did not argue with sustain talk ("I feel fine," "not ready to quit") and largely avoided reinforcing it. However, sustain talk was often bypassed rather than reflected or gently pivoted. The script work rehearsed "I'm not ready to quit" language — autonomy-consistent but keeping attention on reasons not to change. Redirection was inconsistent.

---

**Overall Global Ratings Score:** **3.3/5**
