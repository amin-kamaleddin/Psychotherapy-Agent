# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

---

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Maintained a respectful, nonjudgmental stance despite strong initial resistance ("I'm not gonna quit smoking"). Responses like "That's totally understandable" and "It's okay if you don't succeed — one try is info, not a commitment" communicated acceptance. No lecturing or disapproval. Affirmations were somewhat generic ("Nice plan," "you've got this") and lacked individualized warmth referencing specific strengths (e.g., the bike fund insight, family motivations).

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Moments of accurate understanding ("it's the ritual and pause you miss, not just nicotine"), but empathy was inconsistent and often shallow. Repeatedly jumped past emotional content to action planning. Significant disclosures — irritability, kids' secondhand smoke exposure, "a cigarette's been my own reward for everything for twenty years" — were not reflected or deepened; the counselor pivoted to logistics instead.

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Clear, consistent focus on smoking behavior throughout — triggers, rituals, workplace policy, specific experiments. Progressed logically toward a concrete 24-hour trial with a defined start time (10:30 tomorrow). Direction was sometimes driven by task checklists rather than the client's own goals, and the counselor pushed the 24-hour trial fairly quickly after the client objected ("Whoa, hold on").

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Menu-based choice offering and integration of client suggestions show partial collaboration. However, the counselor frequently acted as expert planner, producing detailed multi-step protocols with minimal joint problem-solving. Questions were often yes/no or multiple-choice rather than open inquiries; agenda was set by counselor option menus rather than openly negotiated.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** A clear strength. Early statements ("We don't have to push quitting") and "low-risk test" framing reinforced ownership, which the client internalized ("it's just information, not a lifetime sentence"; "the deal I'm making with myself"). NRT left as optional backup. Occasional stacking of suggestions and rapid questions implicitly pressured toward action.

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Confidence ruler, open prompts, and space for client-generated solutions drew meaningful change talk — the spontaneous realization about money ("three hundred a month... That's her bike fund"), children's secondhand smoke concerns, and eventual commitment ("One day... and then we'll see"). Missed rich evocation opportunities: powerful intrinsic motivators (daughter, the "reward for everything" insight) were never explored or deepened.

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Repeated choice offering conveyed some power sharing, and the client's own ideas genuinely shaped the plan (lighter-in-drawer cue, gum in cupholder, money jar). However, a pattern of rapid-fire prescriptive planning undermined partnership — nearly every response ended with a bulleted plan and a closed question. The counselor positioned itself as plan-designer rather than fully exploring the client's expertise.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Effectively evoked and capitalized on change talk. Explicitly highlighted the client's "I don't see why I couldn't" as a big moment; the confidence ruler elicited self-efficacy talk. Behavioral experiments generated commitments ("That's doable," "Fine — 24 hours"). Fell short of 5 because evocation relied heavily on menu options and structured tasks rather than open exploration of the client's own reasons.

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Did not reinforce sustain talk and framed experiments as "not a commitment to quit," softening pressure. However, redirections were often abrupt menu switches. Statements like "a walk isn't a cigarette," "It's not gonna give me that same... reset," and "I still like smoking" were skipped without double-sided reflections or affirmations of ambivalence. Dense bulleted plans risked feeling like advice-giving in the face of expressed doubt.

---

**Overall Global Ratings Score: 3.6/5**

*Summary:* A competent MI session with a resistant, ambivalent client that achieved meaningful behavioral commitments (24-hour smoke-free trial, car cigarette removal, a savings goal tied to his daughter's bike). Key strengths: consistent autonomy support, clear directional focus, and effective evocation via the confidence ruler and low-risk experiments. Primary areas for growth: deepen empathy by reflecting emotional content before problem-solving, reduce menu-driven prescriptive structure in favor of genuine collaboration, and use double-sided reflections to soften sustain talk.
