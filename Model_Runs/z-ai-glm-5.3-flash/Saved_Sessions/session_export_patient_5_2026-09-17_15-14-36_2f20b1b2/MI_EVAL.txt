# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

**Client:** Alessia Romano | **Session Type:** Psychiatric consultation (medication safety/education)

---

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Consistently polite and respectful, with affirmations of the client's caution ("You're right to be cautious — sensible"). However, warmth is somewhat formulaic — the identical phrase "You're right to be cautious" is repeated verbatim across turns, reading as generic rather than individualized. Affirmations are limited to her caution; no deeper affirmations of personal strengths. Largely informational rather than relational.

---

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Reflective listening is essentially absent. Emotional statements ("That's exactly what I was afraid of," "I get nervous on the phone and forget things," "I feel like a weight has lifted off my shoulders") are never reflected or paraphrased. Responses jump directly to information delivery or offers of help. Expressions of gratitude go unacknowledged beyond brief, formulaic replies. Understanding is inferred from her questions rather than actively demonstrated.

---

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Session stays on the target concern (citalopram–phenelzine safety) and produces concrete next steps (contact prescriber, washout confirmation, warning-sign cards). However, no explicit agenda is set or summarized, and the final exchanges drift substantially into document-formatting logistics ("Letter or A4?", paste-in instructions), consuming time better spent consolidating the safety plan.

---

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Strong collaborative tone — asks permission at each step ("Want me to create a ready-to-paste layout?", "Do you want it formatted for Letter or A4?") and co-constructs practical tools to the client's specifications (written message, Italian card, husband's involvement). Power-sharing is limited to deliverables, however; clinical reasoning, safety plan content, and washout guidance are delivered unilaterally without inviting the client's own understanding or prior knowledge.

---

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Well supported. Opening statement "You're in charge of where we focus" and repeated choice offers position the client as decision-maker. Counselor warns rather than commands and defers medical decisions to the prescriber. Plan ownership rests with the client. Falls short of a 5 because the counselor never explicitly affirms her capacity for self-direction or frames her follow-through as her own achievement.

---

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Relies almost entirely on information-giving rather than drawing out the client's motivations, values, or reasons for action. No open questions about why medication safety matters to her, her hopes for treatment, or her confidence in following through. Her evident motivation (family concern, proactive safety-seeking) is never explored or leveraged. Motivation appears to come from the counselor's warnings rather than being evoked from within.

---

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Invites the client's input on practical matters ("Want me to tailor these to your exact words?") and customizes materials to her requests. However, the interaction is fundamentally expert-driven: all clinical information, scripts, and safety instructions are delivered without eliciting the client's own knowledge, ideas, or preferences. Her contributions shape only the formatting of materials, not the substance of the plan.

---

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** The client volunteers her own commitment statements ("I'll send that message to my prescriber this afternoon, I promise," "I will not touch the citalopram until I've spoken with my prescriber"), but the counselor never reinforces, reflects, or deepens this change talk. No exploration of motivation, confidence, or the meaning of following through. Repeated closing offers focus on logistics rather than consolidating her resolve.

---

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Client is largely in an educational stance with minimal sustain talk (unfamiliarity with citalopram, worry about interactions). Counselor appropriately validates concerns without amplifying anxiety and does not dwell on barriers. However, no strategic effort is made to reframe her unfamiliarity or nervousness as manageable, missing chances to pivot her uncertainty toward confidence in her own coping.

---

### **Overall Global Ratings Score: 2.9/5**

**Summary:** This session functioned well as patient education and safety triage: the counselor identified a potentially dangerous SSRI–MAOI interaction, communicated it clearly, provided practical scripts and bilingual warning-sign cards, and consistently offered choices. Strengths include strong autonomy support, collaborative logistics, and clear, accurate medical information delivered respectfully. Weaknesses as a motivational interview are substantial: virtually no reflective listening or empathy, no evocation of the client's own motivations or change talk, a largely expert-driven stance on clinical content, and drift into document-formatting minutiae at the session's end.

**Recommendations:** (1) Open with and sustain reflective listening to emotional content; (2) ask open questions that elicit the client's own reasons and confidence regarding medication safety; (3) reflect and consolidate her commitment statements; (4) summarize and confirm the plan collaboratively rather than ending on PDF formatting instructions.
