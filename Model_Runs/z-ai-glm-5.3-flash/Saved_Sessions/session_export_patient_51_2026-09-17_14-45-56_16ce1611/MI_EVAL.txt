# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

---

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The counselor is consistently polite and respectful, offering thanks and acknowledgments, but affirmations are generic and repetitive. Repeatedly extending the session after the client stated multiple times she had an appointment undermined respect for her autonomy and schedule.

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Reflective listening is minimal, limited to superficial content reflections like 'You sound relieved and more in control.' The counselor never explores the client's emotional experiences—her worry, frustration with the prescriber, or relief at catching the danger early.

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The counselor maintains strong, consistent focus on the target behavior of safely transitioning from phenelzine to citalopram, with a concrete follow-up plan secured. The main weakness is that direction is maintained through information delivery rather than collaborative goal-setting.

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** The interaction is largely one-directional, with the counselor delivering expert checklists while the client absorbs and politely deflects. Plans are prescribed rather than co-constructed, and end-of-message questions feel appended rather than reflecting shared decision-making.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The counselor mixes autonomy support (asking when to check back, what would build confidence) with directive pressure. Repeatedly extending the session against the client's explicit wishes and ignoring her script refusal only partially supported her sense of choice, particularly around pacing.

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** The approach is predominantly educational, reciting washout rules and warning signs rather than drawing motivation from the client. Only two late, brief confidence questions attempted to evoke the client's own motivations and resources.

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** The counselor occupies the expert role for most of the interaction, delivering dense medical instruction with little invitation for the client's own knowledge to shape the session. The client's meaningful contributions originated from her, not from collaborative elicitation.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Evocative questions about confidence elicited useful client language and self-generated commitment ('I'll call my prescriber this afternoon'). However, opportunities to deepen change talk were missed, such as when the client said 'maybe this is worth sorting out properly' and received safety information instead.

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The client's sustain talk was mild (time constraints, declining a script), and the counselor did not amplify resistance or reinforce status quo thinking. However, there was little skillful redirection—repeated follow-up questions despite her stated need to leave functionally invited disengagement.
