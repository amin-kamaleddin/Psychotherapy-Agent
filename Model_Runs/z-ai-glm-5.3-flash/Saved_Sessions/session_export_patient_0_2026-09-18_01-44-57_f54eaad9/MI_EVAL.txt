# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

---

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 5
**Comments:** The clinician consistently accepted the patient's ambivalence without judgment, validating both his desire for weekend use and his concerns about safety and isolation. The patient explicitly noted he expected a lecture but instead felt free to think and speak openly.

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 5
**Comments:** The clinician repeatedly reflected both sides of the patient's ambivalence ('the infection was rough AND your weekends are yours') and captured deeper emotional content, including the loneliness of hiding use from family. The patient responded by disclosing things he had never said aloud before.

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 5
**Comments:** The conversation stayed focused on what the patient wanted to be different—safety, honesty, and connection—while following his lead. Each response gently steered toward his own reasons and ideas rather than a clinician-driven agenda.

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 5
**Comments:** The clinician framed the patient as the expert ('you're the expert on your own life') and explicitly deferred to his authority on decisions like telling his sister. The patient generated his own plan (writing down what to say, being careful about supply) at his own pace.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 5
**Comments:** The clinician repeatedly reinforced the patient's autonomy: 'your pace, your terms,' 'I'm not going to tell you who or whether.' The patient echoed this, noting no pressure and that he wasn't signing up for anything he didn't choose.

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 5
**Comments:** The clinician consistently drew out the patient's own motivations and ideas with open questions like 'what ideas do you have for protecting yourself?' and 'what would it mean to you?' This evoked strong change talk, including 'I don't want a repeat of that' and the plan to confide in his sister.

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 5
**Comments:** The dialogue functioned as a genuine back-and-forth, with the clinician building on each patient statement rather than directing. The patient increasingly took ownership, ending by proposing he return and report back—a sign of true partnership.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 5
**Comments:** The clinician skillfully elicited and reflected change talk: desire to be safer, recognizing the unmanageable aftermath of the last batch, wanting someone in his corner, and committing to concrete small steps. The patient closed with spontaneous commitment language ('I'll sit with it,' 'I'll come back').

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 5
**Comments:** Sustain talk ('weekends are mine,' 'not signing up for rehab') was met with reflection rather than challenge, keeping it from hardening. The clinician acknowledged it alongside change talk, allowing ambivalence to resolve naturally into the patient's own modest plan.
