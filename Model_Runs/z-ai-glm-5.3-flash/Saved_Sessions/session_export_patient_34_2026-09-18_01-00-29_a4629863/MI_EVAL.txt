# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

---

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The clinician consistently affirmed the patient's autonomy ('that's your call', 'you're the expert on your friends') and explicitly removed judgment around the referral. Scored 4 rather than 5 because a few affirmations were somewhat repetitive ('protecting your record and your time' used twice) and autonomy was occasionally framed around the clinician's own agenda rather than fully patient-defined.

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 5
**Comments:** The clinician repeatedly demonstrated accurate empathy, validating the patient's sense of unfairness ('that's a frustrating spot', 'I get how that stings') and his FOMO around peer pressure. Reflections accurately captured both surface content and implicit feelings (stress, pride, enjoyment).

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The session maintained a clear focus on the conduct referral and drinking behavior, and the clinician skillfully linked the patient's own stated priorities (record, time, stress-free fun) to behavior change. Scored 4 because the direction was occasionally somewhat narrow — e.g., not exploring the patient's actual drinking frequency or the meaning of 'normal college stuff' — though overall the conversation stayed on a meaningful target.

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 5
**Comments:** Strong collaborative stance throughout: the clinician explicitly positioned himself as a non-judge ('I'm not here to relitigate security's decision'), asked permission implicitly before probing, and consistently sought the patient's ideas ('what's your take?', 'what would tell you it's working?'). Solutions were generated from the patient rather than prescribed.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 5
**Comments:** Patient autonomy was strongly supported: the clinician accepted the patient's disclaimer that he wouldn't become a hermit, explicitly credited conclusions as the patient's own ('that's your own conclusion, not mine'), and framed the 21st birthday as the patient's natural leverage point rather than an external threat.

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 5
**Comments:** Excellent evocation of the patient's own change talk: reasons (record, wasted time), preferences ('more fun with just my friends'), commitment ('I'd skip the huge ragers'), and self-generated coping strategies (the humor-based deflection). The clinician consistently drew answers out rather than supplying them.

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The clinician worked as a partner, checking understanding ('I want to make sure I caught that right') and building on patient ideas, but the exchange was somewhat interview-driven with long therapist turns. Scored 4 rather than 5 because the patient was occasionally positioned as the expert being consulted rather than a full co-author of the session direction.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 5
**Comments:** The clinician skillfully cultivated change talk through open questions about goals, success criteria ('what would tell you this approach is working?'), barriers, and future vision. Patient change talk escalated from ambivalence to clear commitment language ('keeping busy with the small stuff sounds way better than sitting around being tempted').

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Sustain talk (minimization, 'not a big deal', 'everyone does this', FOMO) was met with reflection and reframing rather than argument, and the clinician avoided the righting reflex. Scored 4 because some reflections doubled as mild reframes toward change rather than fully neutral reflections of the ambivalence, e.g., treating 'don't be so loud' as already a practical idea early on.
