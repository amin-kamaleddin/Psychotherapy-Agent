# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

---

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist is consistently respectful, nonjudgmental, and validating, with strong affirmations of the patient's insight. It is not a 5 because some affirming language subtly steers toward a change-oriented frame rather than explicitly affirming respect regardless of the patient's decision.

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist uses accurate and complex reflections of the patient's ambivalence, coping function of smoking, and concern for her son. It is not a 5 because the empathy sometimes remains at summarizing behavior and conflict rather than fully deepening into stronger emotional undercurrents such as shame, fear of losing control, or exhaustion.

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The session generally stays connected to the target issue of smoking, its impact on the child, and coping, moving from broad ambivalence to a more specific behavior plan. It is not a 5 because the conversation occasionally drifts into general stress details and lacks a clear closing summary or explicit direction toward a concrete next step.

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 5
**Comments:** The interaction is strongly collaborative, with the psychiatrist working from the patient's own ideas rather than imposing a plan. The patient's concerns, support person, and short text-message plan drive the direction of the session.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist supports autonomy well by avoiding directives and inviting the patient's own judgment throughout the session. It is not a 5 because some questions subtly assume a no-smoke frame, which could be balanced with more explicit affirmation of the patient's right to choose either option.

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 5
**Comments:** The psychiatrist consistently uses open, client-centered questions that elicit the patient's own motivations, insights, and strategies. This is a strong example of evocation because the patient generates much of the change talk and plan.

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 5
**Comments:** The psychiatrist consistently treats the patient as the expert on her own life, stress, coping, and child. The session is driven by the patient's own ideas and concerns rather than by the psychiatrist's assumptions or plan.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist does a good job evoking and reinforcing change talk, including the patient's plan to avoid smoking inside and texting her sister before smoking. It is not a 5 because the psychiatrist does not consistently deepen the change talk with readiness, confidence, or commitment questions and limited follow-up on the strongest statements about her son's health and maternal identity.

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist handles sustain talk appropriately without arguing, dismissing, or challenging the patient's concerns. It is not a 5 because the psychiatrist does not always actively pivot away from sustain talk by immediately rebalancing it with stronger values-based reflections or reasons for change.
