# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

---

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The psychiatrist is generally nonjudgmental, respectful, and affirming, with a supportive tone. However, acceptance can feel generic and mildly evaluative when tied to plan compliance or prescribed coping.

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** There are some useful reflections of ambivalence, but empathy remains limited by a quick shift into practical problem-solving. Deeper emotional themes such as shame, identity, and feeling “marked” are not sufficiently explored.

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The session stays focused on relapse prevention, staying out, and practical next steps. However, the direction becomes repetitive and misses opportunities to use the client’s deeper concerns to guide change.

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** The psychiatrist offers choices and makes some adjustments, which supports collaboration at times. The interaction often remains expert-driven, with the psychiatrist supplying the tools, structure, and agenda.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** There are acknowledgments of choice, but the style is frequently directive and plan-centered. The client’s comment about not feeling “a prisoner on lockdown” shows that autonomy support is inconsistent.

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** The psychiatrist asks some initial evocative questions and affirms the client’s strengths, such as 22 days clean. However, the session often moves into advice-giving rather than drawing out the client’s own motivations, values, and reasons for change.

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Some collaborative language is used, but the psychiatrist repeatedly constructs the agenda and proposed plan. The client explicitly feels that he is being handed “cards to play,” suggesting a weak partnership stance.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** The client offers meaningful change talk, including 22 days, not wanting to lose Fiona, and “not yet.” The psychiatrist does not consistently reinforce, amplify, or deepen this change talk through evocative follow-up.

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The psychiatrist generally avoids arguing with the client’s sustain talk and sometimes acknowledges its validity. However, responses often pivot quickly into planning rather than using double-sided reflections or deeper ambivalence work.
