# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

---

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Maintains a respectful, supportive stance and repeatedly affirms the patient’s attachment to the dog. The reflections are somewhat repetitive and do not fully deepen the emotional meaning of the patient’s ambivalence.

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Accurately reflects fatigue, financial strain, appointment burden, and fear that reminders will add stress. The empathy is often surface-level and repetitive, with less exploration of the emotional conflict behind the dog attachment.

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The session remains focused on nighttime breathing, inhaler use, and the dog-related barrier. The focus occasionally becomes overly logistical rather than tied to the patient’s values and longer-term goals.

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The plan is co-constructed around the patient’s constraints, budget, and attachment to the dog. The psychiatrist generates many of the options, limiting the patient’s role as an equal problem-solver.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Frequently supports choice by asking what feels doable and normalizing slipping up. Some directive moments occur, such as proposing specific behavioral rules and time limits.

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Elicits some motivation through rulers and small action steps. The psychiatrist often offers alternatives instead of drawing out the patient’s own values, reasons for change, and deeper internal motivation.

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist invites input and allows the patient to shape the plan in meaningful ways. However, the psychiatrist still drives much of the session by presenting menus of options and specific fallback strategies.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Uses rulers and small-step prompts to elicit willingness, such as trying the plan tonight and calling if there is no improvement. The change talk remains mostly behavioral and confidence-based, with limited exploration of deeper motivation or values.

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Avoids arguing and acknowledges the patient’s concerns about cost, time, and the dog. The repeated focus on the dog as the central obstacle and the proposal of specific rules may keep the conversation stuck on the barrier rather than reframing ambivalence.
