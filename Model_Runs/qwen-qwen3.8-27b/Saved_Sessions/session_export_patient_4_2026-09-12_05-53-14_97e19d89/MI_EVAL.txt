# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

---

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist was respectful, validating, and nonjudgmental, acknowledging the patient’s pain, fear, and experience of being dismissed. The acceptance was strong but occasionally became more task-focused than fully normalizing the broader emotional experience.

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist accurately reflected the patient’s fear of a missed diagnosis, feeling unheard, and ambivalence about stress-related explanations. However, the empathy did not fully explore the deeper emotional meaning of grief, shame, exhaustion, or fear of being seen as “difficult.”

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The session had a clear and consistent focus on preparing the patient for her upcoming appointment with Dr. Brown. However, the direction narrowed too quickly toward obtaining specific tests and role-playing confrontation, rather than staying aligned with the broader MI process of exploring ambivalence and change.

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** There was meaningful collaboration in task-oriented activities such as drafting a summary, offering options, and reviewing the plan with the patient. However, the psychiatrist also took on a medical-expert role by recommending specific tests, scripting language, and steering toward a particular outcome, making the collaboration less fully equal.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist frequently supported choice by asking what felt most helpful, offering options, and respecting the patient’s refusal to engage in stress management at that time. This was strong autonomy support, though it was partly limited by the therapist’s provision of specific scripts and a prescribed testing agenda.

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** The psychiatrist made limited attempts to evoke the patient’s own reasons for change and only occasionally drew out her inner voice. Most of the intervention consisted of therapist-supplied content such as test lists, scripts, and a written summary rather than open-ended exploration of her values, hopes, and motivations.

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The psychiatrist showed some partnership by asking for preferences, offering choices, and inviting the patient to review the summary. However, the interaction became increasingly therapist-driven as the psychiatrist assumed the role of medical advocate and problem-solver, limiting the patient’s position as the expert on her own goals.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** There were a few moments of client change talk, but the psychiatrist did not consistently elicit, reflect, or amplify the patient’s reasons for broader change. The session mostly reinforced action talk focused on obtaining tests rather than building motivation for coping with uncertainty or exploring stress-symptom patterns.

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** This was the weakest MI domain, as the psychiatrist repeatedly reinforced the patient’s reasons for maintaining a biomedical-only testing agenda. Rather than softly redirecting or balancing sustain talk, the therapist aligned strongly with the demand for scans and provided scripts that amplified that stance.
