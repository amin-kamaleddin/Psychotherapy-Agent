# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

---

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist used a respectful, nonjudgmental tone and validated the patient’s ankle concerns and reluctance to overreact. The warmth was practical rather than deeply normalizing alcohol-related ambivalence or broader injury risk.

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** There were accurate reflections of frustration, functional limitation, and the patient’s desire for clarity without overreacting. Empathy remained mainly factual and did not explore deeper feelings such as shame, fear of serious injury, or ambivalence about drinking.

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** The session was well organized around ankle assessment and follow-up, but it did not connect the injury context to the stated MI target of alcohol use. The patient’s mention of rolling the ankle outside a bar was a clear opportunity to gently link injury risk to drinking, but it was not followed.

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist repeatedly invited patient input, adapted to preferences, and co-developed practical next steps such as a call script and checklist. The collaboration was strong but stayed largely within logistical ankle care rather than a broader health or drinking-related agenda.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Autonomy support was strong through permission-seeking, offering options, avoiding pressure, and respecting the patient’s decision to make the call independently. It could have been strengthened by more explicitly supporting the patient’s ownership over alcohol-related health choices.

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** The psychiatrist elicited some of the patient’s own reasons to seek care for the ankle and reinforced the decision to call. However, there was little evocation of the patient’s internal motivations, values, or confidence related to alcohol use or harm reduction.

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist demonstrated a collaborative stance by asking for permission, checking in on preferences, and respecting the patient’s declining role-play. Partnership was practical and medical, but did not extend to positioning the patient as the expert on values, drinking choices, or broader health priorities.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** The psychiatrist elicited some ankle-related change talk and reinforced the patient’s practical decision to get the injury checked. There was no meaningful effort to elicit change talk about drinking, injury risk, health values, self-control, or confidence to reduce harm.

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The psychiatrist handled some ankle-related sustain talk appropriately by reflecting the patient’s desire not to make a big deal or overthink the injury. The main limitation was not engaging sustain talk around alcohol use or social drinking, and no stronger double-sided reflections were used.
