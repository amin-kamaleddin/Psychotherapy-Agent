# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

---

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist maintained a respectful, nonjudgmental stance and normalized Claudia’s ambivalence and possible slips. Acceptance was slightly limited because affirmations were often tied to her compliance and plan-making rather than to her as a person.

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Reflections accurately captured Claudia’s ambivalence, desire for simplicity, and shifting confidence. Empathy could be deeper by exploring the emotional and relational meaning of drinking, such as loneliness, self-criticism, or her wind-down ritual.

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 5
**Comments:** The session stayed tightly focused on reducing alcohol use and moved efficiently from ambivalence to a concrete Tuesday plan. The therapist used focused questions and clear check-ins to keep the conversation practical and goal-directed.

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 5
**Comments:** Claudia’s ideas strongly shaped the plan, including the two-glass limit, pantry rule, tea strategy, backup option, and follow-up timing. The psychiatrist acted as a partner by confirming, integrating, and refining the client’s choices rather than imposing solutions.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist repeatedly emphasized Claudia’s control and asked for her preferences throughout the session. It would be stronger if the therapist first invited Claudia to generate more of the plan before offering a menu of options.

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The therapist used rulers and open questions to draw out some motivation, but often moved quickly into behavioral planning. More evocative questions could have deepened Claudia’s personal reasons, values, and confidence in change.

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist maintained a collaborative stance and invited Claudia to choose among options and approve each step. A fully partnership-centered approach might have had the client generate more plan content before the therapist supplied specifics.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The session elicited some change talk through open questions and rulers, but the therapist did not consistently amplify or deepen it. Change talk was often used as a bridge to planning rather than as a source for further motivational exploration.

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Sustain talk was handled respectfully, especially around the “just one more” worry and the 10-minute pause. Advanced techniques such as double-sided reflections could have more actively softened ambivalence and highlighted the tension between wanting and fearing change.
