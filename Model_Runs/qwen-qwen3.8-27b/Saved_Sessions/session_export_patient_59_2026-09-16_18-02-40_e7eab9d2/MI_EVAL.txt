# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

---

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist uses respectful, nonjudgmental language and avoids shaming or arguing with the patient. Acceptance is strong but sometimes procedural, and it could more explicitly validate the discomfort of being mandated, pressured, or labeled.

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Reflections accurately track the patient’s practical concerns about school, probation, friends, and keeping the stakes manageable. Empathy is more cognitive than emotional, with missed opportunities to name frustration, defensiveness, fear, or the feeling of being tested.

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The session stays generally connected to marijuana-related risk and probation compliance without major drift. Direction becomes somewhat trapped in the patient’s “paperwork” frame and could be more strategic in moving toward a concrete risk-reduction goal.

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 5
**Comments:** The psychiatrist behaves as a partner rather than an expert, repeatedly inviting input and letting the patient’s ideas drive the conversation. The patient’s practical plan clearly shapes the session, though a shared agenda could have been made even more explicit.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 5
**Comments:** Autonomy support is a clear strength, with consistent affirmation of the patient’s freedom, choice, and ownership. The psychiatrist avoids direct advice, pressure, or controlling responses even when the patient challenges the process.

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Open questions are used throughout and avoid providing external reasons for change. Evocation remains fairly superficial, focusing on compliance and logistics rather than deeper values, future goals, or internal motivation.

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist maintains a collaborative stance and keeps the patient’s perspective central. Partnership is client-led but somewhat one-way following, with less explicit co-creation of agenda or broader goals.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The psychiatrist elicits some practical change talk, such as staying out of trouble, studying, and avoiding risky situations. However, the change talk is not consistently amplified, reinforced, or deepened into stronger desire, need, or benefit language.

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** This is the weakest area, with the psychiatrist often mirroring sustain talk rather than gently softening or redirecting it. There is limited use of double-sided reflections or discrepancy between the patient’s minimization and the realistic consequences of continued risk.
