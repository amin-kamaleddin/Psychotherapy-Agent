# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

---

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist maintains a nonjudgmental, respectful stance and validates the patient's concerns about the dog, finances, and overwhelm. Acceptance is strong, though sometimes it feels procedural rather than deeply warm.

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The psychiatrist reflects some practical and emotional stress, including ambivalence about the dog and financial burden. However, reflections often remain surface-level and miss deeper feelings such as exhaustion, resentment, loss, and fear of losing control.

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The session stays broadly focused on asthma management, medication access, cost, and practical barriers. The psychiatrist helps narrow overwhelm into actionable topics, though there is occasional drift into logistical problem-solving.

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The psychiatrist invites the patient to choose among options and adjusts plans based on his feedback. However, much of the collaboration is limited because the psychiatrist supplies most of the solutions and the patient mostly selects from them.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist uses open choices, honors the patient's limits, and avoids pushing the dog issue or adding unwanted tasks. Autonomy is strongly supported, though the psychiatrist's frequent provision of scripts and options can position them as the source of the plan.

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The psychiatrist uses some open-ended questions and invites the patient to identify what feels doable. However, evocation is often interrupted by advice, options, and problem-solving, limiting deeper exploration of the patient's own values and reasons for change.

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The psychiatrist demonstrates partnership by asking for preferences and allowing the patient to direct the session. Still, the psychiatrist often takes on an expert problem-solver role, making the collaboration feel somewhat uneven.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** The patient offers some change talk, such as acknowledging that his asthma is out of control and expressing willingness to try short practical steps. The psychiatrist rarely asks evocative questions or builds on the patient's change language, so change talk is not consistently cultivated.

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The psychiatrist acknowledges the patient's reasons for maintaining the status quo and does not argue against keeping the dog. However, when dog-related triggers are raised and resisted, the psychiatrist drops the topic rather than exploring ambivalence more deeply.
