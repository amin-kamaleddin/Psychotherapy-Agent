# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

---

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist maintains a respectful, nonjudgmental, and autonomy-respecting stance, often validating Marisol’s decision and framing it in practical, non-pathologizing terms. It falls short of a 5 because the acceptance is somewhat procedural and rarely affirms her broader worth, resilience, or the tension between her values and the dentist’s concerns.

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist shows solid reflective listening and accurately captures Marisol’s emphasis on autonomy, practicality, and avoiding overthinking. However, the empathy lacks full nuance because it underdevelops the emotional and identity-related meaning of the piercing, and some reflections feel repetitive or formulaic.

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The session has some focus on oral health and practical monitoring, but the target behavior is not consistently clear or aligned with the referral concern. It becomes somewhat circular, with repeated similar questions that lack strategic progression toward a specific change goal.

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist demonstrates a strong collaborative stance by inviting Marisol to define priorities and co-constructing a plan around her own criteria. The main limitation is that repeated focus on backup or check-in can make the interaction feel less equal in places.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist strongly supports Marisol’s autonomy by repeatedly emphasizing that the decision is hers and avoiding direct pressure or advice. However, continued prompting for backup or check-in after she clearly declines creates a subtle sense of agenda persistence.

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The psychiatrist uses several evocative questions that encourage Marisol to generate her own criteria for monitoring and self-management. These questions are helpful but remain somewhat superficial and repetitive, with limited effort to draw out deeper values, discrepancy, or stronger motivation.

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The session is generally collaborative, with the psychiatrist offering choices and inviting Marisol to shape the conversation. However, continuing to ask variations of the same reliability question after she says she does not want more shifts the interaction slightly toward a counselor-led agenda.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** There are very few authentic instances of change talk, and the psychiatrist does not consistently cultivate them. Questions tend to produce practical problem-solving language rather than meaningful motivation, desire, ability, or reasons for change related to the referral concern.

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** The client’s sustain talk is prominent, and the psychiatrist validates her preference for simplicity and no extra work. He avoids arguing, but he does not effectively use double-sided reflections, evocative discrepancy, or gentle pivots to help her hold both the meaning of the choice and the health concern.
