# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

---

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist uses a respectful, nonjudgmental tone and validates Jason’s perspective with supportive statements. Acceptance is strong but remains somewhat formulaic and does not fully normalize his ambivalence or fear of being labeled “reformed.”

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Reflective listening is present at a surface level, accurately capturing Jason’s frustration and desire to keep things light. Empathy is limited in depth because the psychiatrist does not explore underlying emotions, values, ambivalence, or the meaning of being seen as “reformed.”

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The session maintains a clear practical focus on preparing for the conduct meeting and selecting a brief statement and follow-up step. However, the direction is more administrative and sanction-focused than MI-focused, with limited guidance toward Jason’s values or internal motivation.

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The psychiatrist invites Jason’s input and adjusts wording based on his feedback, showing some collaboration. The interaction is uneven because the therapist largely controls the agenda and supplies most of the plan rather than co-creating it with Jason.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Jason is given choices and is allowed to select statements and steps, which supports autonomy somewhat. Autonomy support is weakened by the therapist’s repeated directives, exact scripts, and prescriptive advice about what Jason should do.

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** The psychiatrist asks a few open or choice-based questions but mostly supplies the client’s answers rather than eliciting Jason’s own reasons for change. Jason’s values, concerns, confidence, and internal motivation are not consistently drawn out or developed.

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** There are some partnership gestures, such as asking if Jason wants to work together and offering choices. The overall stance remains expert-driven, with the psychiatrist becoming the main author of the statement and plan rather than equal partner.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Jason offers some change-related statements, such as leaving early, completing the module, and saying it “won’t be my pattern.” The psychiatrist rarely reflects, amplifies, or expands these statements, so change talk remains limited and underdeveloped.

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Jason’s sustain talk is noticeable, including minimizing the incident, wanting to avoid sounding “reformed,” and wanting to keep the meeting low-key. The psychiatrist accepts this framing but does not use double-sided reflections, gentle pivots, or discrepancy work to soften resistance.
