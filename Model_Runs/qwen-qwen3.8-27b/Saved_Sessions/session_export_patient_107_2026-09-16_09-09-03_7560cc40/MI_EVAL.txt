# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

---

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist is generally respectful, nonjudgmental, and supportive of the patient’s ambivalence. The stance becomes slightly less consistently warm when it shifts into procedural or expert-instructive reminders.

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Reflections accurately capture the patient’s ambivalence about coffee, focus, and health concerns. They remain somewhat surface-level and repetitive, missing deeper fears about losing routine, control, or competence.

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The conversation stays focused on coffee use, jitters, blood pressure risk, and next steps. It becomes repetitive and logistical rather than progressively guiding the patient toward a personally meaningful target.

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The psychiatrist asks for input and accepts the patient’s preference to focus on blood pressure testing first. Collaboration is limited because the psychiatrist supplies most of the structure, options, and decision rules.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The patient is regularly offered choices and the psychiatrist avoids coercive language. Repeated instructional details and pre-made options can make the process feel more directed than fully patient-owned.

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Questions are mostly informational rather than motivational. The psychiatrist provides reasons, options, and guidance instead of systematically evoking the patient’s own values, confidence, or reasons for change.

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The psychiatrist shows some partnership by asking what the patient wants to focus on and respecting the preference for blood pressure data. The interaction often feels clinician-led because of frequent expert information and decision rules.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** The patient offers some change-oriented statements, but the psychiatrist does not strongly amplify, reinforce, or build on them. There is limited use of scaling, values exploration, or questions that would elicit stronger client-generated change talk.

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** The psychiatrist appropriately avoids arguing with sustain talk and validates the routine’s benefits. However, the response pattern mostly preserves the status quo and uses little double-sided reflection or discrepancy work to gently explore the costs of maintaining the current pattern.
