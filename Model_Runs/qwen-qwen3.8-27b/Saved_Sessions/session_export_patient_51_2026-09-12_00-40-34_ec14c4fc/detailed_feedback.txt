                                Motivational Interviewing Feedback Criteria (5-Point Likert Scale)

Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)
------------------------------------
1. Acceptance: Extent to which the counselor communicates unconditional positive regard for the client.
   1 = Judgmental, disapproving, labeling
   2 = Inconsistent respect, occasional judgment
   3 = Neutral stance, basic respect
   4 = Generally respectful and supportive
   5 = Consistently respectful, warm, supportive

2. Empathy: Extent to which the counselor understands and/or makes an effort to accurately understand the client’s perspective.
   1 = Little effort to understand, minimal reflective listening
   2 = Limited perspective-taking, mostly factual focus
   3 = Adequate but inconsistent effort to understand
   4 = Good reflective listening and perspective-taking
   5 = Highly accurate, consistent understanding and reflections

3. Direction – Degree to which the counselor maintains appropriate focus on a specific target behavior or concerns directly tied to it.
1 = No clear focus; session wanders aimlessly
2 = Minimal focus; frequent topic drift
3 = Occasional focus on target behavior but inconsistent
4 = Generally keeps discussion directed toward target behavior
5 = Consistently maintains clear focus and guides toward change

---

4. Collaboration – Extent to which the clinician behaves as if the interview is occurring between two equal partners, both of whom have knowledge that might be useful in the problem under consideration.
1 = Authoritarian or expert-driven stance
2 = Limited collaboration; frequent direction by counselor
3 = Partial collaboration; uneven power-sharing
4 = Strong collaborative stance most of the time
5 = Fully collaborative; client’s ideas shape session direction

---

5. Autonomy Support – Extent to which clinician supports and actively fosters client perception of choice as opposed to attempting to control the client’s behavior or choices.
1 = Directive or pressuring; minimizes choice
2 = Occasionally acknowledges choice but inconsistently
3 = Neutral regarding client autonomy
4 = Regularly affirms client freedom to choose
5 = Consistently reinforces client ownership of change process

---

6. Evocation – Extent to which the clinician conveys an understanding that motivation for change, and the ability to move toward that change, reside mostly within the client and therefore focuses efforts to elicit and expand it within the therapeutic interaction.
1 = Provides reasons for change without exploring client view
2 = Limited interest in client motivations
3 = Some evocation but superficial
4 = Frequently draws out client’s own reasons for change
5 = Consistently elicits and develops client change talk throughout session

---

7. Partnership – Extent to which the clinician conveys an understanding that expertise and wisdom about change reside mostly within the client

1 = Clinician actively assumes the expert role for the majority of the interaction with the client. Collaboration or partnership is absent.
2 = Clinician superficially responds to opportunities to collaborate.
3 = Clinician incorporates client’s contributions but does so in a lukewarm or erratic fashion.
4 = Clinician fosters collaboration and power sharing so that client’s contributions impact the session in ways that they otherwise would not.
5 = Clinician actively fosters and encourages power sharing in the interaction in such a way that client’s contributions substantially influence the nature of the session.

---

8. Cultivating Change Talk – Extent to which the clinician actively encourages the client’s own language in favor of the change goal, and confidence for making that change.
1 = No effort to evoke change talk
2 = Rare or weak efforts
3 = Occasional evocation of change language
4 = Frequent evocation of change talk
5 = Consistent, skillful evocation throughout

---

9. Softening Sustain Talk – Extent that the clinician avoids a focus on the reasons against changing or for maintaining the status quo.
1 = Reinforces sustain talk
2 = Rarely shifts focus away from sustain talk
3 = Occasionally redirects toward change
4 = Often redirects effectively
5 = Consistently pivots client language toward change

Evaluation Guidance
-------------------
- All domains are rated on a 1–5 Likert scale.
- Ratings should reflect overall quality and consistency of behavior.
- Focus on both counselor skillfulness and client speech as indicators of MI process quality.

 # Motivational Interviewing Feedback: Psychiatrist Evaluation

## Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)

### 1. Acceptance
Score: 3/5

Explanation:
The psychiatrist maintains a respectful, nonjudgmental tone and does not criticize Ingrid for being rushed or for seeking brief reassurance. Phrases such as “I’m here to listen and support you,” “You’re welcome,” and “glad that was helpful” communicate basic respect and a willingness to help. However, the acceptance remains somewhat procedural and transactional rather than genuinely warm or individualized. The psychiatrist does not affirm Ingrid’s initiative to ask a potentially important safety question, nor does the counselor explicitly normalize her need for a quick answer while in a time-limited context. To reach a higher level, the psychiatrist could have used language such as, “I appreciate you checking before making any changes,” or “It makes sense that you’d want a clear answer quickly.” The overall stance is respectful, but the positive regard is not consistently deep enough to merit a 4 or 5.

---

### 2. Empathy
Score: 2/5

Explanation:
The psychiatrist shows limited effort to accurately understand or reflect Ingrid’s perspective. The opening invitation is broad and supportive, but after Ingrid states that she is in a hurry and needs a quick clarification, the psychiatrist primarily shifts into factual medical explanation and repeated safety warnings. There is little reflective listening of Ingrid’s underlying concerns: she appears to want a clear, reliable answer, she is time-constrained, and she is trying to avoid a dangerous medication combination. The psychiatrist does not reflect the push-pull of wanting safe information quickly while having to leave, nor does the counselor validate the relief or caution involved in asking about a serious drug interaction. Comments such as “glad it’s clear” are polite but superficial. Given the patient’s limited emotional disclosure, a higher level of empathy would still have required reflections such as, “You’re pressed for time, but you made a point to ask because you wanted to be sure this was safe,” or “It sounds important to you to have a direct answer before you leave.” Because the interaction is mostly factual rather than perspective-taking, a low score is warranted.

### 3. Cultivating Change Talk
Score: 2/5

Explanation:
Ingrid does produce some change talk, particularly when she says she will flag the issue at her appointment, that she will call the pharmacy if anything changes, and that she appreciates the clarity. However, the psychiatrist makes only minimal effort to cultivate, expand, or deepen that change language. The main change talk is acknowledged with a brief affirmation, such as “That sounds like a good plan,” but it is not amplified, reflected back, or explored in a way that would strengthen Ingrid’s motivation. There is no systematic use of change talk eliciting questions such as “What does checking on this say about how you approach your health?” “What would it mean to you if you could bring this to your doctor with confidence?” or “How confident are you that you’ll mention this at your appointment?” The psychiatrist also does not explore DARN-CAT dimensions such as desire, ability, reasons, need, or commitment in a meaningful way. The session contains some supportive language, but the evocation of client-driven motivation is weak and infrequent.

---

### 4. Softening Sustain Talk
Score: 3/5

Explanation:
The psychiatrist does not reinforce sustain talk or focus heavily on reasons for maintaining the status quo. Ingrid’s main barrier is time pressure rather than resistance to change, and the psychiatrist accepts her need to leave without arguing or trying to prolong the session. This is appropriate. However, the psychiatrist does not actively use her time constraint to redirect toward a concise, client-centered action step that would support safer behavior. Instead, the clinician repeatedly emphasizes risk and emergency signs, which, while clinically relevant, is more didactic than MI-consistent. A stronger approach would have been to acknowledge the time pressure, then help Ingrid convert it into a small actionable commitment, for example: “Since you’re in a hurry, what is the one thing you’re most likely to take away from this conversation?” or “How can we make sure this safety point reaches your prescriber today?” The psychiatrist avoids making sustain talk worse, but does not consistently redirect the session toward change in a motivational manner, so the score is moderate.

### 5. Partnership
Score: 2/5

Explanation:
The interaction is largely expert-driven rather than partnership-based. The psychiatrist answers the medication question directly, provides specific switching guidance, and repeatedly gives instructions about warning signs and urgent care. Ingrid is mostly a passive recipient of information rather than an equal partner in constructing the plan. There is little evidence of shared decision-making, such as asking Ingrid how she would like to proceed, what she thinks is most important, or whether she agrees with the suggested next step. For a higher partnership score, the psychiatrist could have said, “What would be most helpful for you to do with this information today?” or “How would you like to raise this with your prescriber?” The counselor does maintain a polite, non-authoritarian tone, but the balance of the session is more informative and directive than collaborative.

### 6. Direction
Score: 4/5

Explanation:
The psychiatrist maintains a clear focus on the target issue throughout the session: the safety concern about citalopram and phenelzine. ### 7. Autonomy Support
Score: 3/5

Explanation:

### 8. Collaboration
Score: 2/5
