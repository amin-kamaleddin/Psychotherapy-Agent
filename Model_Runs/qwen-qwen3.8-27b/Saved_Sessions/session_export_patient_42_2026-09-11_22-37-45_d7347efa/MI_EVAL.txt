# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

---

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist maintains a respectful, nonjudgmental stance and validates Elise’s social concerns without arguing or labeling. Acceptance is generally supportive, though sometimes formulaic and more focused on surface content than deeper feelings of belonging and shame.

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** There is adequate perspective-taking around smoking as a social ritual and the effort of explaining herself to friends. Reflections are often shallow and quickly shift to practical problem-solving, missing the emotional weight of her ambivalence and fear of being seen as “weird.”

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** The session does not consistently evoke or amplify Elise’s own reasons, values, hopes, or desires for change. Much of the work focuses on selecting behavioral strategies rather than developing her internal motivation.

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Elise’s preferences are incorporated, such as small steps, avoiding a quit day, using coffee/phone, and choosing “count me in.” The session is collaborative in form, but the psychiatrist still supplies most of the content and strategies, keeping the power balance uneven.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Elise’s choice is repeatedly supported through options, flexibility, and normalization of a low-pressure approach. The repeated focus on selecting a plan, rating confidence, and committing to a small step introduces mild pressure, but overall autonomy is strongly supported.

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Some evocative questions are used around readiness, doability, and confidence ratings. The evocation remains superficial because the psychiatrist frequently provides techniques and scripts instead of eliciting Elise’s own deeper motivations and ideas for change.

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Elise is invited to choose what to focus on, and her preferences influence the session. However, the psychiatrist largely takes the expert role by supplying most of the plans, scripts, and strategies, making the interaction more counselor-led than a true partnership.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Efforts to elicit change talk are weak and infrequent, with limited exploration of what cutting back would mean for her health, self-respect, relationships, or future. Elise’s own reasons for change are not consistently drawn out or amplified.

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Sustain talk is generally acknowledged without argument or confrontation, and concerns about social fitting are validated. However, the psychiatrist often responds with additional coping scripts rather than using double-sided reflections or reframing to help resolve the conflict.
