# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

---

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist maintains a respectful, nonjudgmental, and supportive stance while acknowledging the client’s progress and practical concerns. However, the warmth remains somewhat formulaic, with less deep emotional validation around shame, fear, anger, and relationship stress.

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Reflective listening and perspective-taking are strong, especially in summarizing the client’s ambivalence and relapse worries. The main limitation is that empathy is mostly content-focused rather than fully attuned to deeper emotional states such as fear, tension, shame, or frustration.

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 5
**Comments:** The session stays consistently focused on reducing anger when drinking and making alcohol use safer and more controlled. The psychiatrist effectively moves from target behavior to concrete cues, confidence, and backup plans without wandering into unrelated topics.

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The plan is clearly shaped by the client’s own ideas, such as the “break” cue, non-alcoholic beer, sticky note, two-can limit, and couch backup. However, the psychiatrist still controls much of the structure through repeated options and checklist-like confidence/safety sequences.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist supports the client’s choice by using language such as “Would you be willing…?” and allowing the client to set his own limit and plan. The sense of autonomy is slightly reduced by the frequent therapist-generated lists and repeated structured prompts.

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The client is frequently asked to identify his own motivations, strategies, cues, and confidence levels, leading to a strong client-generated plan. Evocation is somewhat limited by therapist-suggested options and less exploration of deeper values, identity, or long-term relationship goals.

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist generally treats the client as the expert on his own behavior and allows the client’s ideas to drive the plan. A stronger 5 would require more explicit power-sharing, co-created agenda-setting, and fewer therapist-led options.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist effectively elicits and affirms change talk around cutting down, staying calm, rehearsing the “break” cue, and following a two-can limit. The work would be stronger with deeper exploration of the client’s values, safer household, and long-term goals.

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Sustain talk and ambivalence are handled respectfully through normalization, affirmation, and redirection toward small manageable steps. The psychiatrist does not argue or minimize the client’s concerns, though the handling would benefit from more nuanced double-sided reflections and ambivalence resolution.
