# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

---

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist is respectful and nonjudgmental, using validating statements that reduce shame. Acceptance is sometimes formulaic and does not fully acknowledge deeper emotional concerns.

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The clinician clarifies experiences and acknowledges difficulty, such as a “tough spot” and the timer feeling like a leash. Reflections remain surface-level, missing deeper worry about regret, partner burden, and feeling managed.

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The session stays focused on evening anger, alcohol use, and building a concrete safety plan. It sometimes narrows to wording and logistics, with less linking back to broader goals such as safety and connection.

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The final plan is co-constructed, with the client modifying the break plan, timer, and apology language. Collaboration could improve by eliciting more client-generated solutions before offering strategies.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist repeatedly asks permission, offers choices, and respects the client’s decision to cut down rather than stop. Autonomy support could be stronger by more explicitly reinforcing the client’s ownership and internal capacity.

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Early evocative questions elicit useful information, but much of the session shifts into advice and plan construction. The client’s values, confidence, and reasons for change are not deeply explored.

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The client’s input shapes the plan, including the break word, moving bottles, walk, and simple apology. The psychiatrist often leads with suggestions and scripts, reducing explicit power-sharing and client expertise.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The clinician evokes some change talk by affirming progress and asking whether to cut down or stop. Change talk is not consistently amplified or linked to values, leaving ambivalence underdeveloped.

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The psychiatrist avoids arguing with the client’s preference to keep cutting down and handles resistance without escalation. However, there is limited use of double-sided reflections or strategic reframing to resolve ambivalence.
