# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

---

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist uses a respectful, supportive stance and normalizes the patient’s frustration and fear. The acceptance is somewhat procedural and less affirming of the person beyond her symptoms and medical request.

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Reflections clearly identify fear, exhaustion, urgency, and determination, and they help the patient feel heard. However, empathy remains shallow and misses the deeper meaning around loneliness, stress, and ambivalence about needing proof.

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The session stays organized around the urgent GI concern, the request for tests, and the email to Dr. Brown. Direction is clinically useful but not consistently MI-focused on a broader coping, uncertainty-tolerance, or stress-related target.

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The psychiatrist invites input on the email and offers some options, which keeps the interaction less authoritarian. The collaboration is limited because the psychiatrist mainly functions as the medical expert supplying the workup and message.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** This is a clear strength: the psychiatrist asks permission, offers choices, respects the patient’s decision-making, and leaves the email action to her side. The score is lowered slightly by the directive problem-solving approach embedded in the medical workup and drafted message.

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** There are a few open-ended questions early on, but they are mainly used for medical information-gathering. The psychiatrist does not sufficiently evoke the patient’s own values, reasons, confidence, or motivations for coping with fear, uncertainty, or stress-related symptom patterns.

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The psychiatrist invites the patient to select among options and modify the email, which shows partial partnership. The therapeutic agenda remains largely expert-driven rather than fully co-created with the patient as the expert on her own body and lived experience.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** There are some helpful questions, but the focus stays on tests, a second opinion, and the diagnosis. There is little effort to elicit or amplify the patient’s own desire, ability, or reasons for engaging with coping, fear reduction, or the stress-symptom connection.

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** The psychiatrist does not argue with or dismiss the patient’s biomedical explanation, which is appropriate. However, there is limited MI-consistent softening, reframing, or exploration of the ambivalence between needing proof and recognizing stress/loneliness-related flare-ups.
