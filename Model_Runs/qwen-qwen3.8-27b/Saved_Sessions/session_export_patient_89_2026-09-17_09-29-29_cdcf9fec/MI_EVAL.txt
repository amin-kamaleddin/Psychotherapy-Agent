# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

---

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist maintains a respectful, nonjudgmental stance and normalizes realistic starting points. Acceptance could be stronger by more explicitly affirming Marcus as a person and naming emotional pain from past dieting or failure.

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Reflective listening accurately captures Marcus's ambivalence, practical constraints, and mixed feelings about dancing versus sidewalk movement. It could explore deeper emotions such as embarrassment, fear of judgment, or frustration from prior failures.

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 5
**Comments:** The session stays consistently focused on increasing physical activity and improving energy. It moves clearly from a small first step to sidewalk movement, then to a low-pressure drop-in class, without topic drift.

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 5
**Comments:** Marcus's own ideas—sidewalk waiting, parking farther, stairs, dance music, and drop-in class criteria—shape the direction of the session. The psychiatrist builds on those ideas rather than imposing an external plan.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 5
**Comments:** Autonomy is strongly supported through repeated emphasis on choice, ownership, and no pressure. Marcus is positioned as the decision-maker throughout, with minimal advice-giving or implicit expectation.

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist effectively draws out Marcus's own motivations, preferences, and language for a successful first experience. The score could be higher with more advanced evocative techniques such as scaling, eliciting more ability talk, or amplifying commitment language.

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist positions Marcus as the expert on his own body, limits, and schedule, allowing his contributions to guide the session. Some therapist-supplied phrases, such as “realistic bridge” and “happy tired,” subtly frame the problem rather than leaving all meaning-making to the client.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Open questions successfully evoke change talk about sidewalk movement, dance music, low-pressure drop-in classes, and wanting to feel “happy tired.” The psychiatrist could further deepen change talk by explicitly working with readiness, confidence, ability, and commitment language.

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Sustain talk is reframed as reasonable and protective rather than argued with, especially around time, cost, knees, and fear of judgment. It could be more consistently pivoted into stronger change talk or self-efficacy.
