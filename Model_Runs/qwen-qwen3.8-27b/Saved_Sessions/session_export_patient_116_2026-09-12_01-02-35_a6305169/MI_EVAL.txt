# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

---

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist repeatedly normalizes and validates Samir’s resistance in a respectful, nonjudgmental way. It falls short of a 5 because repeated medical risk framing subtly undercuts unconditional acceptance.

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Reflections capture some important themes, such as not wanting meds and feeling managed, but remain fairly surface-level. The therapist often shifts to education rather than fully attending to the emotional meaning of Samir’s experience.

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** There is some focus on medication nonadherence and related concerns, but the conversation drifts among risk, sleep, anxiety, and coping tools. It lacks a consistent, clearly strategic direction tied to Samir’s values or target behavior.

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The therapist offers choices and asks for preferences, showing some collaborative intent. However, the session often feels expert-led because the psychiatrist repeatedly introduces structured plans, options, and medical content.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist frequently affirms Samir’s choice and uses optional language such as “no pressure if not.” Autonomy support is partially undercut by repeated risk education and ongoing prompts for checks, prescriber contact, or coping experiments.

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** The therapist mostly asks closed or option-generating questions rather than open-ended questions about values, goals, or intrinsic motivation. There is limited effort to draw out Samir’s own reasons, confidence, or change talk.

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** There are some collaborative moves, such as asking what Samir would like to work on and offering choices. The interaction still feels more guided than truly partner-led because the psychiatrist steers with structured plans and medical framing.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** The session elicits mostly sustain talk, such as not liking meds and preferring to forget about it, with little strategic effort to develop change talk. The therapist misses opportunities to use Samir’s language to explore desire, ability, or reasons for change.

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Sustain talk remains central, and the therapist often responds with education, risk information, or problem-solving rather than rolling with resistance. Occasional gentle pivots occur, but they are not consistently skillful in redirecting Samir’s ambivalence.
