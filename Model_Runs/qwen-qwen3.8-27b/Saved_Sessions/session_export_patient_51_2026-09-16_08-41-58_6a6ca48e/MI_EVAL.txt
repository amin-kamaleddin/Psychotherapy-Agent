# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

---

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Warm, respectful, and validating tone throughout, with clear nonjudgment toward the patient’s time pressure. Slightly limited because the psychiatrist repeatedly added safety warnings and script offers after the patient had already signaled a need to leave.

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist accurately reflected the patient’s practical concerns about medication safety, avoiding guesswork, and time constraints. Empathy was stronger for cognitive/logistical concerns but did not fully mirror the emotional push-pull or anxiety around making a medication mistake.

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The session stayed focused on the core issue: citalopram/MAOI safety, confirming the prescriber, updating the chart, and recognizing serious symptoms. The main limitation was repetition after the patient repeatedly indicated she needed to leave, which reduced efficiency and pacing.

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist invited input, checked medication status, and adjusted the plan based on the patient’s responses, especially around the optional script. The interaction remained largely clinician-led in medical content, though the script process was genuinely collaborative.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist supported choice by offering the script as optional, asking whether to send it now or later, and not pressuring immediate action. Repeated offers and safety reminders made the clinician’s agenda slightly more present than necessary after the patient’s boundaries were clear.

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** There were some evocative moves early, such as asking for the main focus, importance rating, and current medication use. However, much of the session shifted into informational/instructive mode without deeply eliciting the patient’s own motivations, values, or confidence.

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist used collaborative language and asked the patient about her preferences, allowing her responses to shape the next steps. Partnership could have been strengthened by more explicitly recognizing the patient’s expertise about her own schedule, medications, and practical constraints.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The patient offered clear change talk about checking the interaction, avoiding clashes, and calling her prescriber, and the psychiatrist affirmed this appropriately. The change talk was present but not deeply developed, with limited follow-up on what safety means to her or how to make the call easier.

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The psychiatrist did not argue with or amplify the patient’s main barrier, which was limited time, and offered a low-effort solution. However, repeatedly re-offering the script and repeating safety warnings after she had clearly stated she needed to leave kept the conversation more focused on the clinician’s agenda than fully resolving her ambivalence.
