# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

---

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist maintained a respectful, nonjudgmental stance and validated the client’s ambivalence about smoking and social connection. Acceptance was strong, but it was somewhat implicit rather than deeply personal or explicitly affirming.

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Reflections accurately captured the client’s experience, including how cigarettes were tied to breaks, social presence, and feeling exposed. Empathy was consistent and skilled, though some responses were more conceptual than emotionally deep.

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The session stayed focused on reducing smoking in a way that fit the client’s dental concerns and daily routine. Direction was clear, but it ended without a fully consolidated, specific plan or follow-up step.

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist worked collaboratively by building on the client’s ideas about staying present during breaks and using a drink as a coping strategy. The collaboration was strong, though the psychiatrist still guided the flow of the conversation more than the client.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 5
**Comments:** The psychiatrist consistently emphasized the client’s choice, accepted reduction as a valid option, and avoided pressuring immediate cessation. Autonomy support was excellent, as the client generated her own self-directed language such as “I’m just resting.”

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist frequently drew out the client’s own motivations and solutions, especially around teeth, appearance, and social comfort. Evocation was effective, though some suggested options made the exploration less open-ended.

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist invited the client’s perspective and allowed the client’s concerns to shape the conversation. Partnership was strong, but the psychiatrist remained somewhat leading rather than fully co-directing the session.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The client generated useful change talk around protecting teeth, maintaining a smile, and trying a smaller change such as skipping after lunch. Change talk was frequent and meaningful, though confidence, readiness, and commitment were not fully explored.

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist handled sustain talk well by not arguing, dismissing, or pushing, and by normalizing the client’s concern about feeling odd or excluded. Sustain talk was softened, but the strongest resistance about feeling “anti-smoking” was not fully transformed into change talk.
