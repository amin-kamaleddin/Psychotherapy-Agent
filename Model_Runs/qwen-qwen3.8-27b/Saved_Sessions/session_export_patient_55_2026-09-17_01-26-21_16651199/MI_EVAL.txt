# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

---

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist used nonjudgmental, respectful language and avoided shaming or arguing. Acceptance was strong but could be more explicit in affirming the client’s worth beyond her achievements and coping.

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist offered strong reflective listening and captured the client’s identity concerns, pacing needs, and internal stress. Empathy remained largely cognitive and behavioral rather than deeply exploring underlying fear, shame, or ambivalence.

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The conversation remained coherent and client-led, but it drifted into general stress and inner noise without a clear alcohol-specific target. The session lacked a collaborative narrowing toward a specific behavior change goal.

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 5
**Comments:** The session was co-constructed, with the client shaping pacing, content, and direction. The psychiatrist worked as a partner rather than an authority delivering a fixed assessment.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 5
**Comments:** The psychiatrist consistently supported the client’s choice and freedom, including statements like “You don't owe me a fix tonight.” The client was treated as the decision-maker rather than being pressured or advised.

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist used evocative questions to elicit the client’s own account of stress, coping, and the function of drinking. Evocation was strong in process but less focused on developing explicit change motivation or confidence.

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 5
**Comments:** The psychiatrist framed the conversation as a shared exploration and repeatedly invited the client to guide the process. The client’s preferences and ideas genuinely influenced the direction of the session.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The psychiatrist asked useful questions to evoke alternatives and some change talk emerged, such as wanting a way to decompress without alcohol. Change talk was not consistently amplified, deepened, or summarized.

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** The psychiatrist avoided arguing and reflected the practical function of drinking, but sustain talk remained dominant. The response did not effectively soften, redirect, or balance the client’s reasons for continuing drinking.
