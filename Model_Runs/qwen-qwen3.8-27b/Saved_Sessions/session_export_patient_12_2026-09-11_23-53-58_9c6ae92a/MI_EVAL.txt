# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

---

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The psychiatrist is generally nonjudgmental and avoids blaming or shaming, with statements like “I’m here and not judging.” However, the stance remains somewhat clinical and safety-focused, with repeated directives that limit deeper warmth and unconditional positive regard.

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Empathic responses are present but mostly surface-level and repetitive, such as “that sounds really heavy.” Deeper reflections of Amira’s shame, isolation, ambivalence, and fear of helplessness are often missed as the psychiatrist shifts quickly to safety instructions.

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The session stays focused on the target issue of recent drug use and acute safety, which is clinically appropriate. However, the direction is mainly crisis-management rather than motivational, without clear movement toward a change goal.

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** There is partial collaboration, as the psychiatrist invites input and asks which small step feels doable. Overall, though, the psychiatrist remains the primary driver, offering clinician-generated options rather than co-creating a plan based on Amira’s values and priorities.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The psychiatrist acknowledges Amira’s choice in some moments, such as asking whether she wants to vent or make a plan. However, many repeated directives and safety instructions make the autonomy support uneven.

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Questions are largely factual and safety-focused, such as what was used, when, and whether she is physically unwell. There is little active evocation of Amira’s own reasons for change, values, hopes, or self-efficacy.

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** The psychiatrist invites some input but maintains an expert crisis-manager stance rather than a true partner stance. Amira’s contributions affect only narrow behaviors, while the psychiatrist continues to provide most of the options and direction.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** There is limited cultivation of change talk, although the psychiatrist positively acknowledges actions like putting the bag away and setting the alarm. These moments are not consistently explored or developed into stronger change talk.

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Amira’s sustain talk is prominent, including feelings of helplessness, hopelessness, and futility. The psychiatrist does not reinforce it, but the repeated focus on risk, consequences, and precautions leaves this sustain talk largely unsoftened or redirected.
