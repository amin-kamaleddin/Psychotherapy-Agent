# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

---

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Therapist maintains a respectful, nonjudgmental tone with statements like “That’s fine” and “It’s your call.” However, acceptance is limited by occasional worry/risk framing and limited validation of the client’s practical perspective.

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The therapist uses accurate surface-level reflections such as “I hear your confusion” and “It sounds like you’re surprised.” Deeper emotional undercurrents, including ambivalence and defensiveness, are not fully explored.

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The session initially focuses on the target behavior but shifts toward medical problem-solving and practical dental planning. Direction toward a clear motivational change goal becomes less consistent.

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The therapist invites choice, uses “we” language, and lets client preferences guide next steps. Collaboration remains strong, though the therapist still takes on a somewhat medical authority role.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Autonomy is supported through repeated affirmations of the client’s right to choose and lack of direct pressure to quit. Some concrete medical advice and follow-up instructions introduce a mildly directive element.

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** The therapist gathers information and offers options but does little to evoke the client’s own values, reasons for change, or internal motivation. There is limited exploration of what would matter to the client in reducing cigar use.

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The therapist demonstrates a collaborative stance by offering options, asking preferences, and avoiding a purely expert-driven approach. A mild expert-to-patient dynamic remains due to medical recommendations and symptom monitoring.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Change talk is minimally developed, and the therapist does not consistently reflect, amplify, or build on the client’s possible reasons for change. Few evocative questions explore the client’s health concerns, values, or confidence in changing cigar use.

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The therapist avoids arguing and uses acceptance to prevent escalation, such as “That’s fine” and “It’s your call.” However, sustain talk is not consistently redirected or explored with double-sided reflections.
