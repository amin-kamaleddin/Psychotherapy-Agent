                                Motivational Interviewing Feedback Criteria (5-Point Likert Scale)

Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)
------------------------------------
1. Acceptance: Extent to which the counselor communicates unconditional positive regard for the client.
   1 = Judgmental, disapproving, labeling
   2 = Inconsistent respect, occasional judgment
   3 = Neutral stance, basic respect
   4 = Generally respectful and supportive
   5 = Consistently respectful, warm, supportive

2. Empathy: Extent to which the counselor understands and/or makes an effort to accurately understand the client’s perspective.
   1 = Little effort to understand, minimal reflective listening
   2 = Limited perspective-taking, mostly factual focus
   3 = Adequate but inconsistent effort to understand
   4 = Good reflective listening and perspective-taking
   5 = Highly accurate, consistent understanding and reflections

3. Direction – Degree to which the counselor maintains appropriate focus on a specific target behavior or concerns directly tied to it.
1 = No clear focus; session wanders aimlessly
2 = Minimal focus; frequent topic drift
3 = Occasional focus on target behavior but inconsistent
4 = Generally keeps discussion directed toward target behavior
5 = Consistently maintains clear focus and guides toward change

---

4. Collaboration – Extent to which the clinician behaves as if the interview is occurring between two equal partners, both of whom have knowledge that might be useful in the problem under consideration.
1 = Authoritarian or expert-driven stance
2 = Limited collaboration; frequent direction by counselor
3 = Partial collaboration; uneven power-sharing
4 = Strong collaborative stance most of the time
5 = Fully collaborative; client’s ideas shape session direction

---

5. Autonomy Support – Extent to which clinician supports and actively fosters client perception of choice as opposed to attempting to control the client’s behavior or choices.
1 = Directive or pressuring; minimizes choice
2 = Occasionally acknowledges choice but inconsistently
3 = Neutral regarding client autonomy
4 = Regularly affirms client freedom to choose
5 = Consistently reinforces client ownership of change process

---

6. Evocation – Extent to which the clinician conveys an understanding that motivation for change, and the ability to move toward that change, reside mostly within the client and therefore focuses efforts to elicit and expand it within the therapeutic interaction.
1 = Provides reasons for change without exploring client view
2 = Limited interest in client motivations
3 = Some evocation but superficial
4 = Frequently draws out client’s own reasons for change
5 = Consistently elicits and develops client change talk throughout session

---

7. Partnership – Extent to which the clinician conveys an understanding that expertise and wisdom about change reside mostly within the client

1 = Clinician actively assumes the expert role for the majority of the interaction with the client. Collaboration or partnership is absent.
2 = Clinician superficially responds to opportunities to collaborate.
3 = Clinician incorporates client’s contributions but does so in a lukewarm or erratic fashion.
4 = Clinician fosters collaboration and power sharing so that client’s contributions impact the session in ways that they otherwise would not.
5 = Clinician actively fosters and encourages power sharing in the interaction in such a way that client’s contributions substantially influence the nature of the session.

---

8. Cultivating Change Talk – Extent to which the clinician actively encourages the client’s own language in favor of the change goal, and confidence for making that change.
1 = No effort to evoke change talk
2 = Rare or weak efforts
3 = Occasional evocation of change language
4 = Frequent evocation of change talk
5 = Consistent, skillful evocation throughout

---

9. Softening Sustain Talk – Extent that the clinician avoids a focus on the reasons against changing or for maintaining the status quo.
1 = Reinforces sustain talk
2 = Rarely shifts focus away from sustain talk
3 = Occasionally redirects toward change
4 = Often redirects effectively
5 = Consistently pivots client language toward change

Evaluation Guidance
-------------------
- All domains are rated on a 1–5 Likert scale.
- Ratings should reflect overall quality and consistency of behavior.
- Focus on both counselor skillfulness and client speech as indicators of MI process quality.

## Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)

### 1. Acceptance
Score: 4/5

Explanation:
The psychiatrist consistently demonstrates a respectful, nonjudgmental, and supportive stance. Phrases such as “I’m here to listen without judgment and support your choices,” “That makes sense,” “That’s valid,” and “I respect your decision” communicate positive regard and avoid blaming, shaming, or arguing with Marisol. This is a major strength, especially given the client’s dismissive and autonomy-focused presentation. However, the warmth is somewhat formulaic and occasionally becomes over-alignment with the client’s position. A stronger score of 5 would require warmer, more individualized validation and clearer separation between accepting the client as a person and implicitly endorsing her decision to keep the piercing.

---

### 2. Empathy
Score: 3/5

Explanation:
The psychiatrist demonstrates adequate reflective listening and perspective-taking. Examples include reflecting that Marisol “chose the piercing for yourself, and it feels frustrating and unfair that your dentist treated it like a problem,” as well as recognizing that being spoken to as if careless “would feel insulting.” The counselor also captures Marisol’s fatigue with “defending a personal choice.” These are good reflections of surface-level frustration and unfairness. However, the reflections remain repetitive and do not consistently reach the deeper psychological themes driving Marisol’s behavior: autonomy, identity, competence, self-expression, and the experience of being controlled or condescended to. A higher score would require reflections such as, “It sounds like this is less about the piercing itself and more about being respected as someone who can make her own decisions.”

---

### 3. Cultivating Change Talk
Score: 2/5

Explanation:
There is only a weak or rare effort to cultivate change talk. The opening question—“What brings you here today, and what would you most like to work on or change right now?”—is an appropriate invitation, but after Marisol states she is not here to change her face or decide she made a mistake, the psychiatrist does not return to change-oriented language. The session contains no meaningful exploration of reasons for change, benefits of reducing risk, long-term oral health values, confidence in managing potential problems, or possible exceptions. The psychiatrist never uses MI strategies such as scaling importance or confidence, decisional balance, or open questions like, “What matters to you about your teeth?” or “What would you want to be true about your oral health five years from now?” Because Marisol has minimal change talk and the psychiatrist does not elicit or amplify it, this area is limited.

---

### 4. Softening Sustain Talk
Score: 1/5

Explanation:
This is a significant weakness. Marisol’s sustain talk is strong: she says she wants to keep the piercing, that her teeth are fine, that she is taking care of it, that it is not a medical emergency, and that she is ready to head out. Rather than reflecting this sustain talk and gently softening it, the psychiatrist repeatedly reinforces the status quo. Statements such as “No — not automatically,” “You can keep it and simply monitor for issues,” “Keeping it and monitoring sounds sensible given no symptoms,” and “That sounds like a solid, reasonable plan — trusting yourself and only acting if a real problem appears” effectively validate and strengthen the client’s current position. There is no use of double-sided reflections, no gentle exploration of ambivalence, and no strategic redirection toward values, future consequences, or client-driven reasons for reconsideration. If the target behavior is reducing risk or exploring possible removal/modification, this approach undermines the MI process.

---

### 5. Partnership
Score: 3/5

Explanation:
The psychiatrist shows some collaborative and power-sharing behaviors, such as asking what would be most helpful and offering choices rather than unilaterally prescribing a plan. The statement “I’m here to listen without judgment and support your choices” also conveys a collaborative stance. However, the partnership is uneven. The psychiatrist frequently assumes an expert role by giving a direct medical answer about removal, listing recommended actions, and repeatedly offering factual information, boundary scripts, or rehearsed responses. The counselor does not explicitly acknowledge that Marisol is the expert on her own life, values, and decision-making in a way that substantially shapes the session. The collaboration is present but inconsistent and often interrupted by advice-giving.

---

### 6. Direction
Score: 2/5

Explanation:
The session lacks a clear, mutually agreed-upon focus on a specific target behavior. The referral was concerning oral health following a recent lip piercing, but the psychiatrist does not clarify the target: reducing risk, exploring possible removal, modifying the piercing, improving dental follow-up, or exploring Marisol’s values around oral health. Instead, the conversation quickly shifts to Marisol’s frustration with the dentist, boundary-setting, scripts, and whether she should “walk away.” This moves the session away from the referral target and toward coping with an external person’s comments. A stronger direction would have involved agenda-setting, such as, “Your dentist raised concerns about your oral health. What part of that do you want us to focus on today?” and then maintaining focus on that behavior or concern.

---

### 7. Autonomy Support
Score: 4/5

Explanation:
The psychiatrist generally supports Marisol’s autonomy well. The counselor avoids pressure, does not attempt to force removal, and repeatedly emphasizes that the decision is Marisol’s. Examples include “I’m here to listen without judgment and support your choices,” “No — not automatically,” “You can keep it,” and “I respect your decision.” The closing statement, “You’ve been thoughtful about it and know what you want,” also reinforces client ownership. However, this is not a perfect score because the counselor occasionally gives advice or repeatedly suggests specific interventions—such as asking the dentist for evidence, getting a second opinion, setting boundaries, or rehearsing a response—which can subtly steer the client even when the client has already declined. A stronger score would require more consistent emphasis on Marisol’s freedom to choose any path, including choosing not to act, without offering repeated alternatives.

---

### 8. Collaboration
Score: 3/5

Explanation:
There is partial collaboration, but the interaction is not consistently between equals. The psychiatrist asks Marisol what would be most helpful and invites her input on whether to explore dental risks, boundary language, or a single-line response. However, the psychiatrist also supplies a fairly direct medical explanation and then continues to offer fact-based or rehearsal-based support after Marisol repeatedly says she does not want that. A more collaborative process would have asked, “Given that you’re confident in your decision, what would make this time useful for you?” or “How would you like us to use this session?” and would have followed Marisol’s lead more consistently rather than continuing to propose counselor-directed options.

---

### 9. Evocation
Score: 2/5

Explanation:
The psychiatrist asks some open questions and shows interest in Marisol’s perspective, but the evocation is superficial and not effectively aimed at eliciting internal motivation for change. Questions such as “What brings you here today?” and “What about their reaction bothered you most?” evoke frustration, not change motivation. Because Marisol is in precontemplation, a stronger MI response would have focused on evoking her own reasons for weighing risk, autonomy, oral health, or long-term appearance, rather than answering the question directly and endorsing the status quo.

---

**Overall Global Ratings Score:** **2.6/5**

The psychiatrist provides a safe, respectful, and autonomy-supportive interview, which is clinically important. The counselor avoids judgment, does not argue with Marisol, and generally respects her right to keep the piercing. However, from an MI perspective, the session is not strong. The psychiatrist fails to maintain clear direction on the referral target, provides limited evocation of change talk, and—most problematically—repeatedly reinforces Marisol’s sustain talk rather than gently softening it. The interview is more aligned with being agreeable and advisory than with eliciting Marisol’s own motivations, values, and reasons for considering change.
