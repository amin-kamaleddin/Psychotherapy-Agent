# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

---

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The psychiatrist is consistently nonjudgmental and validates Tyler’s frustration, safety, and right to refuse details. However, the warmth becomes formulaic and is often redirected into problem-solving, which slightly weakens the sense of being accepted as a person.

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Several reflections accurately capture surface-level feelings such as frustration, humiliation, and being unfairly punished. The empathy remains limited because deeper themes like shame, loss of privacy, and the need to be respected are not explored or reflected back in a more complex way.

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** The session drifts away from the target behavior of substance use and becomes focused primarily on conflict with the mother. There is little sustained attention to Tyler’s actual choices, use patterns, risks, or motivation related to the original concern.

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** The psychiatrist offers options and invites input, which gives some appearance of collaboration. In practice, the clinician remains the expert who supplies scripts, explanations, and techniques rather than co-constructing an approach with Tyler.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The psychiatrist regularly affirms choice, accepts refusal without punishment, and offers the client control over the topic. This is partially undercut by frequent advice-giving, scripts, and prescriptive suggestions that can feel controlling for a resistant teen.

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** The clinician frequently supplies explanations and solutions rather than drawing out Tyler’s own values, motivations, or ambivalence. There is minimal use of evocative questions, scaling, or expansion of client-generated change-relevant language.

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** The psychiatrist offers menus of choices, but the underlying stance is expert-driven. Tyler’s own ideas and knowledge about his life are acknowledged but not used to meaningfully shape the direction of the session.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** The psychiatrist does not effectively evoke or amplify Tyler’s own statements about maturity, independence, or wanting to be respected. Change talk is rare, and the session is not structured to strengthen the client’s own reasons or desire for change.

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** The psychiatrist avoids arguing with sustain talk, which is appropriate, but does not effectively soften it or hold the ambivalence. By validating the idea that the problem is mainly the mother’s overreaction, the response reinforces the client’s disengagement from the core issue.
