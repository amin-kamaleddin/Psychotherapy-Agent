# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

---

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist maintains a respectful, nonjudgmental stance and validates the client’s efforts without blaming smoking, drinking, or partial change. It is not a 5 because the warmth remains somewhat procedural and could more explicitly affirm the client’s difficulty, courage, or health values.

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist uses accurate reflections of the client’s frustration, ambivalence, and practical burdens, helping keep the session grounded in the client’s lived reality. It is not a 5 because the empathy remains more behavioral and practical than emotional, with missed opportunities to reflect deeper worry about wasted medicine, helplessness, or fear of uncontrolled asthma.

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The session stays clearly focused on asthma control, medication use, inhaler technique, smoke reduction, and follow-up. It could be stronger by more consistently connecting the immediate plan to broader referral concerns and the client’s stated goal of better lung control.

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist frames the work as joint exploration and lets the client’s preferences shape the final plan. It falls short of a 5 because the clinician still provides much of the medical content, structure, and problem-solving before fully eliciting the client’s own ideas.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist frequently supports autonomy through choice-based language, avoids pressure, and accepts the client’s condition for change. It is not a 5 because the autonomy support becomes less explicit when giving direct medical instructions, safety guidance, and structured recommendations.

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The psychiatrist elicits some practical commitments by asking which options feel most doable. Evocation is limited because the session gives much information and problem-solving before drawing out deeper values, confidence, reasons for change, or amplifying the client’s own change talk.

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist invites client input and allows preferences to shape the session, including inhaler practice, smoke change, and reminder plan. It is not a 5 because the interaction still carries a clinician-led, medical-management flavor rather than fully positioning the client as the expert on his own life and constraints.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The psychiatrist evokes some change-oriented statements when the client chooses a smoke reduction step and reminder. These change statements are not consistently explored, amplified, or linked to deeper benefits, values, or confidence in change.

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The psychiatrist appropriately accepts sustain talk without arguing or criticizing the client’s reasons for maintaining smoking. However, the response often simply accommodates the sustain talk rather than using double-sided reflections or linking the small step to the client’s larger health goals.
