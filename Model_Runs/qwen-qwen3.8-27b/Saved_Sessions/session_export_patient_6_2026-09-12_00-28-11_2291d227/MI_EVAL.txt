# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

---

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist maintained a respectful, nonjudgmental tone and avoided lecturing or blaming. However, acceptance was more task-focused and did not consistently affirm Alexei as a person, including his deeper conflicts around pressure, fear, and values.

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The psychiatrist offered some accurate reflections but often moved quickly into advice, scripts, or practical steps. There was limited deeper exploration of Alexei’s emotions, such as fear of losing parental trust, shame, or worry about being the “odd one out.”

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The session stayed focused on the target issue of alcohol-related risk, peer pressure, and avoiding a repeat incident. It lacked stronger MI direction by not consistently linking the practical coping plan to Alexei’s own values, goals, and motivation for change.

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The psychiatrist collaborated well on creating a natural exit line, such as “My mom’s texting. Gotta go.” However, much of the session was counselor-led, with the psychiatrist providing lists of steps, scripts, and rehearsal recommendations rather than letting Alexei drive the plan.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The psychiatrist included some autonomy-supportive questions and respected Alexei’s chosen exit line. Yet the session also contained many directives and recommendations, which undermined the message that Alexei could identify his own plan.

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** The psychiatrist asked a few opening questions but did not consistently draw out Alexei’s own motivations, values, or confidence for change. Alexei’s change talk, such as caring about soccer and parental trust, was not reflected, amplified, or used to build motivation.

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The psychiatrist demonstrated moments of partnership, especially when co-creating a short, natural exit line. Overall, however, the interaction remained largely expert-driven, with the psychiatrist acting more as a teacher or advisor than a collaborative partner.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Alexei offered meaningful change talk, including not wanting to do it again and caring about soccer, but the psychiatrist did not consistently recognize or expand on these statements. The session shifted toward practical advice and behavioral rehearsal instead of eliciting Alexei’s own reasons for change.

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** The psychiatrist did not argue with Alexei’s minimization of risk, which was appropriate, but also did not effectively soften or redirect sustain talk. The repeated focus on harm-reduction and managing a drinking situation may have inadvertently framed alcohol use as acceptable to control rather than something to avoid in order to protect his goals.
