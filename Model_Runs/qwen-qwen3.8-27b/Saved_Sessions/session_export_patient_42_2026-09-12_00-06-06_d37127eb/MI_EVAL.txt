# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

---

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist consistently used respectful, validating, and nonjudgmental language that reduced shame around Elise’s ambivalence and prior relapse. Acceptance was not scored higher because warmth was sometimes delivered alongside frequent advice-giving, which slightly diluted unconditional positive regard.

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist demonstrated good reflective listening by accurately identifying the conflict between quitting for health and smoking for social connection. However, empathy remained somewhat surface-level, with missed opportunities to explore deeper feelings such as fear of rejection, loneliness, or loss of identity.

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The session maintained a clear focus on smoking cessation and the specific target behavior of smoking during social work breaks. Direction was not rated higher because the session often shifted into detailed skill-building and practical planning rather than staying focused on exploring ambivalence and motivation.

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Elise influenced the plan by choosing coffee, revising the refusal phrase, and expressing concern about losing friendship, showing some true collaboration. However, the psychiatrist frequently led with extensive advice, scripts, and options, making the interaction feel less like an equal partnership and more like expert-led coaching.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The psychiatrist supported autonomy by offering choices and asking Elise to select a doable approach. Autonomy support was weakened by repeated detailed advice, scripts, and specific plans, which may have reduced space for Elise to generate her own solutions and decisions.

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The psychiatrist made several evocative attempts, especially by opening with open-ended questions and asking which option felt doable. Evocation was limited in depth and frequency, as the session often shifted into advisory mode rather than continuing to draw out Elise’s deeper motivations, values, and reasons for change.

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The psychiatrist maintained some partnership by inviting input, asking Elise to choose an approach, and responding to her revisions. The partnership was uneven because the psychiatrist frequently took an expert role by providing multiple options, scripts, and detailed guidance, which could have felt more collaborative.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Elise generated some change talk, such as choosing coffee, trying a one-week plan, and asking if it was a good start. However, the psychiatrist did not strategically use open-ended questions or reflections to strengthen Elise’s own desire, ability, need, and commitment language.

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The psychiatrist acknowledged Elise’s sustain talk about belonging, friendship, and social pressure without dismissing it. Softening was inconsistent because the session relied more on practical redirection than MI strategies such as double-sided reflections, rolling with resistance, or evocative questions to explore the costs of continuing to smoke.
