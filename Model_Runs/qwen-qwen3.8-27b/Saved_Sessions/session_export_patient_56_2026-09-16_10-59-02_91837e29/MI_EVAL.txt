# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

---

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist maintains a respectful, nonjudgmental stance with frequent affirming and validating statements. Acceptance is slightly limited by continued use of measurement or checklist language, which keeps the conversation partly oriented toward performance rather than fully accepting the client's discomfort with being evaluated.

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist demonstrates good reflective listening and perspective-taking, accurately reflecting the client's quiet pang, task-mode conflict, and dislike of performative presence. Empathy falls short of a 5 because deeper issues around self-worth, inbox identity, and fear of performing are not fully explored emotionally.

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The session maintains a clear focus on aligning the client's behavior with her core value of family happiness and presence at home. Direction is slightly uneven because the conversation occasionally slows when it circles implementation details and measurement language.

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist collaborates well, and the client's preferences substantially shape the final plan, including leaving the phone in the car and avoiding formal tracking. Collaboration is not a 5 because the clinician still supplies many structured options before the client fully leads from her own values.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The psychiatrist includes several autonomy-supportive behaviors, such as inviting choices and asking what feels doable. Autonomy support is inconsistent because repeated use of language such as 'try,' 'test,' and 'notice' subtly pressures performance despite the client's clear preference not to be evaluated.

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The psychiatrist makes some evocative attempts by asking about recent values-based moments and what would make change feel natural. Evocation remains superficial because the client's strong intrinsic motivation and rich values are not fully drawn out or expanded before moving to practical steps.

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist generally adopts a collaborative, partnership-oriented stance and responds to the client's choices rather than overriding them. Partnership is not a 5 because the clinician frequently offers specific options and structures before the client has fully led the process.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The session elicits clear change talk, including the client's desire to be home and statements such as 'It's my family, not a job.' However, the psychiatrist does not consistently amplify or deepen this change talk before shifting to practical implementation and measurement language.

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist avoids arguing with the client's resistance and validates her concerns about quizzes, tests, and monitoring. The score is limited by repeated use of 'try,' 'notice,' and measurement language, which can subtly maintain the evaluation frame the client is trying to avoid.
