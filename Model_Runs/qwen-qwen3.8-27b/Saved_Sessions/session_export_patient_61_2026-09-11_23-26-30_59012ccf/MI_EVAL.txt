# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

---

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist is respectful, validating, and nonjudgmental, often normalizing the client’s overwhelm and refusing behavior. However, the acceptance feels somewhat repetitive and lacks stronger, individualized affirmations of the client’s strengths or effort.

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist uses strong reflective listening to capture feelings such as exhaustion, unfairness, pressure, and being overwhelmed. However, the reflections remain somewhat surface-level and do not fully explore deeper emotional themes such as fear of failure, loss of control, resentment, or identity as a provider.

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The session stays loosely connected to the referral issue of diabetes-related overwhelm but drifts across multiple topics such as sleep, work, family, grounding, and safety. It lacks a clear, collaboratively chosen target behavior or focused MI direction on one manageable diabetes-management step.

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** There is partial collaboration through choice questions and invited client input, preventing a purely authoritarian stance. However, the psychiatrist remains the main agenda-setter and solution-generator, with little evidence that the client’s own ideas are shaping the session.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The client’s freedom to choose is consistently honored, with frequent low-pressure options and acceptance of the client’s decision to leave for work. Some directive pressure remains because the counselor repeatedly proposes specific actions, scripts, and exercises.

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Early open questions invite some self-expression, but the session does not consistently draw out the client’s internal motivations, fears, values, or reasons for change. The psychiatrist repeatedly offers external solutions instead of using evocative questions to build the client’s own change language.

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The psychiatrist invites input with choice questions and avoids an authoritarian stance, which supports some partnership. However, the client’s role remains limited to accepting, rejecting, or clarifying constraints while the counselor supplies most of the agenda and intervention options.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** This is a major weakness, as the client offers very little change talk and the psychiatrist does not strategically build on weak statements such as being “looking into it.” The counselor instead shifts quickly to practical options rather than eliciting the client’s own motivations, values, or reasons for change.

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The psychiatrist avoids arguing with or dismissing the client’s resistance and validates the desire to put the problem aside. However, the session remains saturated with sustain talk, and the counselor does not use double-sided reflections, rolling-with-resistance, or ambivalence-focused questions to help the client move toward change.
