# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

---

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist maintained a respectful, nonjudgmental stance and affirmed the client’s concerns without shaming. Acceptance was present but somewhat procedural, with fewer emotionally attuned affirmations of the client’s effort and vulnerability.

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Reflections were accurate on practical issues and normalized some client experiences, but they often missed the deeper emotional and relational meaning behind the client’s statements. There were missed opportunities to reflect fear of judgment, prior failure, ambivalence, and work-related stress more fully.

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The session stayed clearly focused on smoking cessation, coping with urges, and medication planning. Direction was maintained, though the pacing was sometimes clinician-led rather than fully client-directed.

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist responded to the client’s preferences, incorporated the client’s ideas, and adjusted strategies to fit the client’s concerns. Collaboration could be stronger by inviting the client to generate more solutions before providing options.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The client was given choices and the psychiatrist generally avoided pressure or rigid direction. Autonomy support could be strengthened by more explicitly emphasizing the client’s ownership of decisions, timing, and method.

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** The psychiatrist provided useful information and practical plans but did not consistently draw out the client’s own motivations, values, reasons for change, or confidence. More open-ended, evocative questions would have strengthened change talk and commitment.

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The psychiatrist invited some client input and followed the client’s direction at times, but the interaction often shifted into an expert-driven, instructional style. Partnership would be stronger with more consistent positioning of the client as the authority on his own life.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The client expressed meaningful change talk, and the psychiatrist created some opportunities for the client to articulate preferences and next steps. However, the psychiatrist did not consistently amplify or deepen this change talk through reflections or MI-style questions.

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The psychiatrist avoided reinforcing sustain talk and normalized lapses in a generally supportive way. Softening was not consistently strategic, with missed opportunities to use double-sided reflections or gently reframe ambivalence before moving into planning.
