# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

---

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist maintains a respectful, nonjudgmental stance and affirms the client’s parenting efforts. Acceptance is strong, though it is not a 5 because validation is sometimes tied to her coping or protective behaviors rather than unconditional regard.

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 5
**Comments:** The psychiatrist demonstrates accurate and nuanced empathy by reflecting the client’s pride, frustration, guilt, and need for a private pause. The reflections capture the emotional meaning of smoking without oversimplifying the client’s experience.

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The session generally stays focused on smoking, the parking-lot pause, and the client’s worry about her son. Direction becomes slightly diffuse as the conversation moves into general coping options, so the connection to a specific smoking-reduction goal could be sharper.

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist uses collaborative language and allows the client’s ideas to shape the session, especially around the parking lot, phone, and need for personal time. Collaboration is strong, though the clinician occasionally introduces options that are more clinician-generated than fully client-led.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 5
**Comments:** The psychiatrist consistently supports the client’s autonomy by emphasizing her choice and avoiding pressure or mandatory plans. Statements such as “I don’t have the answer—you do” clearly reinforce the client’s ownership of the process.

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist effectively evokes the client’s own needs, concerns, and internal resources through open-ended questions. The score is not a 5 because evocation does not consistently deepen the client’s change talk specifically related to smoking.

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 5
**Comments:** The psychiatrist positions the client as the expert on her own experience and uses collaborative, non-prescriptive language. The client actively shapes the direction of the session, which reflects strong partnership.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The psychiatrist makes some effective attempts to elicit change-relevant material, but the response to client change talk is not consistently skillful. The client’s statements about wanting to quit and being a better example could have been reflected, amplified, and expanded more.

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist handles sustain talk well by validating the function of smoking rather than arguing or challenging the client. The conversation still remains heavily focused on the sustaining role of cigarettes, so the pivot toward change could be more explicit.
