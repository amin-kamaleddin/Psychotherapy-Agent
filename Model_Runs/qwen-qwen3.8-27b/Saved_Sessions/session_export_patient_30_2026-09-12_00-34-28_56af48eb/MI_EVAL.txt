# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

---

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist maintained a respectful, nonjudgmental stance and reduced shame around the client’s hesitation. However, the acceptance felt somewhat generic and frequently shifted into medical advice, limiting a more individualized, affirming connection.

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** There were some effective early reflections, particularly around muscle side-effect worries. However, empathy became inconsistent and more plan-focused, missing opportunities to explore deeper emotions such as fear, livelihood, and feeling stuck.

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The session remained focused on cholesterol management, medication concerns, muscle side effects, and follow-up planning. However, the direction was more medical problem-solving than MI-guided focus on the client’s own goals and values.

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** The interaction was limited in true collaboration because the psychiatrist unilaterally created questions, a plan, scripts, and checklists. The client’s preference for a simpler approach was not fully honored, making the session feel more expert-driven than co-created.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The psychiatrist validated the client’s right not to start medication and respected his boundary. However, many specific directives were given in a way that could feel controlling rather than fully autonomy-supportive.

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** The psychiatrist supplied many reasons, practical steps, and medical information rather than consistently eliciting the client’s own motivations. Client values such as work, family, and maintaining physical function were not deeply explored or reflected.

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Although the psychiatrist occasionally invited input, the interaction quickly became expert-driven with detailed plans, scripts, and checklists. This limited power sharing and made the client feel more like a recipient of advice than an equal partner.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** The client offered some change talk, such as cutting back on fried food, walking more, and being willing to talk later. The psychiatrist did not actively cultivate this language with open questions that evoke desire, ability, reasons, or commitment.

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** The client’s sustain talk was acknowledged, but it was not effectively softened or pivoted. Phrases like “not crazy” were respectful but reinforced the status quo rather than exploring both sides of ambivalence.
