# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

---

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist maintains a respectful, nonjudgmental tone and validates Eric’s minimization and boundary about not quitting. Acceptance is strong but somewhat procedural, with limited deeper affirmation of Eric as a person or the difficulty of external pressure.

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist uses accurate reflections that capture Eric’s ambivalence and work-related stress. Empathy could be deeper by naming underlying feelings such as resentment, fear of being labeled sick, or pride in physical capability.

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The session stays focused on smoking, stress, and practical harm reduction without drifting off topic. Direction becomes overly focused on implementation details rather than maintaining MI momentum toward motivation and discrepancy.

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist invites Eric’s input and co-creates a practical plan around spacing out smokes. Collaboration is improved by reducing early therapist-generated options and allowing Eric’s own ideas to emerge first.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist clearly supports Eric’s autonomy by emphasizing choice, no pressure, and respect for his limits. Autonomy support could be strengthened by offering fewer structured suggestions after Eric resists experiments or cues.

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The psychiatrist asks useful open questions and invites Eric to identify realistic options. Evocation is limited because the session shifts quickly into suggestions instead of drawing out Eric’s deeper reasons, values, confidence, or discrepancy.

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist works collaboratively and lets Eric shape the final plan around the water bottle and back-door break. Partnership is strong but could be more client-led by waiting longer and asking more open questions before presenting options.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Some change talk is elicited around reducing smoking frequency rather than quitting. More evocative questions about importance, confidence, values, or desired outcomes would help develop change talk more fully.

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Sustain talk is acknowledged without argument, which is appropriate and low-pressure. It could be softened more effectively by using double-sided reflections that balance smoking’s short-term benefit with Eric’s longer-term costs or reasons for change.
