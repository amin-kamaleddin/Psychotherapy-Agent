# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

---

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist maintains a respectful, nonjudgmental, and supportive stance while normalizing the client’s fear and hesitation. Acceptance is solid, though it can feel slightly formulaic and does not consistently reach deeper unconditional positive regard.

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Reflective listening is generally strong and validates the client’s ambivalence and social discomfort. Empathy could be deeper by exploring underlying feelings such as loneliness, shame, fear of rejection, or the cost of continued isolation.

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The session stays focused on the target behavior and narrows the issue to concrete, manageable actions. It becomes somewhat repetitive and procedural, with limited collaborative summary or clear consolidation of a single agreed-upon step.

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist invites client input and adjusts the plan based on her preferences, showing a collaborative tone. However, the counselor still supplies many options and practical solutions, which can reduce the sense of fully equal partnership.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist regularly offers choices and respects the client’s decision to keep the message saved rather than forced. Autonomy support is weakened by frequent prescriptive suggestions and externally driven micro-strategies.

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Early open questions help the client identify the social fear and some hesitant willingness to try. The session shifts too quickly into practical advice, limiting deeper exploration of values, internal motivation, and stronger change talk.

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The psychiatrist invites input and offers choices, but often takes a more active problem-solving role by providing specific tips, scripts, and plans. The client’s expertise and ownership of the process could be more clearly centered.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The client offers brief, hesitant change talk, and the psychiatrist responds with some affirmations. There are missed opportunities to deepen desire, ability, reasons, or need statements through more evocative questions.

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The psychiatrist does not argue with the client’s sustain talk and normalizes many concerns. However, it does not sufficiently reduce the influence of avoidance or use strategies such as double-sided reflections or exploring the cost of staying the same.
