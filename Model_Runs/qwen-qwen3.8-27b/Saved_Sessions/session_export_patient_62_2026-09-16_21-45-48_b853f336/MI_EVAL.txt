# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

---

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist uses a respectful, nonjudgmental, validating tone throughout, accepting Jordan’s emotional state without criticism or moralizing. Acceptance is strong but somewhat formulaic and could more explicitly affirm Jordan as a person beyond the difficulty.

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist demonstrates strong reflective listening by accurately capturing Jordan’s mixed feelings, ambivalence, and practical conflicts around the car and work. Empathy is solid, but reflections become repetitive and do not consistently deepen into the emotional core of Jordan’s repeated “I can’t get a break.”

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The session stays focused on a concrete and relevant target: finding a small, realistic step to address the car issue affecting work and finances. Direction is clear, but the work could be better consolidated toward a specific change goal and confirmed as Jordan’s priority.

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist invites Jordan’s perspective and builds the conversation around Jordan’s own ideas, such as calling the shop or needing a firm yes or no. Collaboration is strong, though it remains a guided partnership rather than fully client-led co-creation.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist avoids pressure, directives, and prescriptive advice, consistently reinforcing that Jordan knows his situation and choices best. Autonomy support is strong, though it could be more explicit when Jordan expresses self-doubt and uncertainty.

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist uses open-ended, action-focused questions to elicit Jordan’s own goals, concerns, and possible steps rather than giving reasons for change. Evocation is a strength, but it does not fully develop Jordan’s deeper motivation, confidence, or values.

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist conveys a collaborative stance by stating the intention to work with Jordan rather than for him and by positioning Jordan as the expert on his life. Partnership is strong, though the psychiatrist still guides the session quite systematically without fully negotiating the agenda.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The psychiatrist evokes useful change talk, including Jordan’s desire to stop feeling stuck and possible actions like calling the shop. However, the psychiatrist does not consistently reflect, amplify, or consolidate these emerging change statements into stronger client ownership.

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The psychiatrist avoids arguing with or dismissing Jordan’s sustain talk and uses double-sided reflections to leave room for change. Softening is reasonable, but the most powerful hopeless statements, especially “I can’t get a break,” are not fully reframed or resolved, leaving sustain talk dominant.
