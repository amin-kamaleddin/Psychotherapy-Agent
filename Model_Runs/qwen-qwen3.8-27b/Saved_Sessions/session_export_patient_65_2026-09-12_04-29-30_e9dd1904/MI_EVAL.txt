# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

---

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist maintains a respectful, nonjudgmental, and supportive stance with normalizing language. However, occasional evaluative phrases such as “good move” and “doing the right thing” reduce the consistency of unconditional positive regard.

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Reflections accurately capture the client’s practical frustration and need for clarity. The psychiatrist does not fully explore deeper ambivalence, avoidance, shame, or the emotional weight of external pressure.

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 5
**Comments:** The session remains tightly focused on the target behavior of completing court-ordered community service hours. Each question, script, checklist, and follow-up plan directly supports clarifying hours, locations, proof, and deadlines.

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The psychiatrist invites input, adapts to the client’s preferences, and uses joint problem-solving language. However, the counselor often drives the process by generating most of the scripts, options, and planning content.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The psychiatrist offers choices, avoids direct pressure, and respects the client’s reluctance to share personal information. Autonomy is weakened by frequent problem-solving and subtle normative language that positions the counselor as the evaluator.

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** The session begins with an MI-style question but quickly shifts to logistics and task completion. There is limited effort to elicit the client’s own motivations, values, importance, readiness, or confidence.

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The psychiatrist invites client choice and incorporates preferences such as texting, reminders, and a shorter checklist. Nevertheless, the interaction becomes expert-driven, with the psychiatrist doing much of the cognitive and planning work.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** There is an initial attempt to open discussion, but change talk is not consistently elicited or expanded. The conversation relies more on brief affirmations than on questions that deepen the client’s own reasons for change.

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The psychiatrist avoids arguing against sustain talk and offers low-effort alternatives that respect the client’s reluctance. More advanced MI techniques, such as double-sided reflections, are not consistently used to resolve ambivalence.
