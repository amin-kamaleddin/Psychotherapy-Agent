# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

---

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist consistently shows warmth, respect, and nonjudgment while validating Isolde’s hesitation and fear of self-monitoring. Acceptance is somewhat procedural and task-oriented, and could be deepened by affirming Isolde’s self-awareness, values, and capacity beyond completing an exercise.

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist accurately reflects Isolde’s ambivalence between being useful and truly present, capturing the meaning behind her worry about performance. The main limitation is moving too quickly from emotional exploration to problem-solving and technique, which can limit deeper felt sense.

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The session stays focused on the target concern of aligning behavior with the value of family happiness, especially presence rather than performance during dinner. The pacing is strategic and purposeful, with only slight reduction in advisory scaffolding needed to further support client-led direction.

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** There is strong joint problem-solving, with the sound anchor, plate-pickup cue, and definition of success co-created with the client. It is not a 5 because the psychiatrist still leads with multiple generated options and interventions before fully inviting Isolde to originate her own plan.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist regularly supports choice by offering options, asking what feels doable or kinder, and accepting rejections without pressure. A stronger score would require more explicit affirmation of Isolde’s right to choose or not choose and less implicit expectation to complete the planned exercise.

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist frequently draws out the client’s own motivations and language, such as “coming home” and “silence is still part of the connection.” Evocation is sometimes weakened by advisory content and could be strengthened with more value-based open questions.

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist collaborates well by asking what Isolde wants to explore and which step feels most doable, allowing her preferences to shape the plan. Partnership is strong but not fully equal because the psychiatrist still supplies much of the structure, cues, and experiment framework.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist elicits useful change talk, such as choosing the sound anchor because it keeps her present and viewing silence as connection rather than failure. To reach a higher level, the psychiatrist could more deliberately expand change talk by linking these choices to values, relationships, and self-efficacy.

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist effectively softens concerns about “work,” “performing mindfulness,” and “policing thoughts” by reframing them as practice rather than performance. A higher score would require less initial reinforcement of the task frame and a faster pivot toward the client’s own values and language.
