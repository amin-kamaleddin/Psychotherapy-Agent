# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

---

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist generally shows respect, patience, and nonjudgment through validation and acceptance of limits. However, some evaluative or medically alarming language slightly reduces unconditional positive regard.

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Empathy is present but mostly surface-level and content-focused. It misses deeper ambivalence about being seen as overreacting or losing control.

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The session stays focused on alcohol use, safety, and contacting the physician. It would be stronger if more explicitly tied to Kenji's personal reduction goals.

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** There is collaboration in tailoring the doctor message, but the psychiatrist remains the primary source of content and plan. More co-creation of the agenda and goals would strengthen the MI process.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist offers choices, flexibility, and avoids forcing immediate decisions. Some directive medical urgency and prescriptive language slightly undercut client-led autonomy.

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Questions are mostly information-gathering and safety-oriented rather than drawing out Kenji's own motivation. There is limited exploration of his values, concerns, or personal reasons for change.

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The psychiatrist asks for input and adjusts to preferences, but often assumes the expert role. A more equal partnership would invite Kenji to set the agenda and define success.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Kenji shows small willingness to take a step, but the psychiatrist rarely reflects, amplifies, or expands this change talk. More MI-consistent follow-up would strengthen emerging motivation.

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Sustain talk is met without argument, but not consistently softened with MI strategies such as double-sided reflections. Repeated focus on withdrawal danger keeps the conversation medical rather than ambivalence-focused.
