# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

---

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist consistently uses respectful, nonjudgmental language and affirms the patient’s effort without labeling or shame. Acceptance is strong, though it occasionally feels formulaic and could include deeper validation of shame and relapse fear.

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The clinician provides accurate reflective listening and captures key concerns about stress, blood pressure, and fear of failing. Empathy could be stronger by reflecting more of the emotional nuance behind statements like “I know it sounds weak” and “I don’t want to fail again.”

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 5
**Comments:** The session remains strongly focused on smoking cessation, medication, stress-related triggers, quit timing, and practical coping strategies. The conversation follows a clear logical progression toward concrete change planning with little irrelevant drift.

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist invites input, offers options, and collaborates on the prescriber sentence and coping plan. Collaboration could be deeper by using more co-construction language so the patient more visibly shapes the overall approach, not just details.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The clinician supports patient choice by inviting input, offering options, and allowing the patient to select a quit date and coping strategies. Autonomy support would be stronger with more explicit reinforcement that the patient is ultimately in charge of the decision.

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The psychiatrist elicits some patient-generated change talk, especially around readiness and choosing a quit date. However, the session relies more on providing information and practical options than drawing out deeper values, confidence, or commitment.

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The session maintains a collaborative stance and invites the patient’s priorities to guide the conversation. Partnership could be more explicit through sustained power-sharing language and regular checks about what the patient wants to focus on next.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The psychiatrist encourages some change talk and affirms the patient’s plan, but does not consistently amplify desire, ability, reasons, need, or commitment. More evocative questions would help consolidate the patient’s own motivations for quitting.

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The clinician generally avoids arguing and acknowledges barriers such as stress, time pressure, and fear of relapse. The session would benefit from more strategic double-sided reflections to normalize ambivalence and address the patient’s final concern about slipping.
