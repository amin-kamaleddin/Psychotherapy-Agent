# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

---

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The psychiatrist uses respectful, nonjudgmental, and normalizing language. However, acceptance is weakened by frequent shifts into advice and a lack of stronger, individualized affirmations.

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The psychiatrist offers useful reflections on ambivalence and loneliness early in the session. Later empathy becomes shallower, often moving quickly from reassurance to strategy rather than holding the client's deeper fears.

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The session stays generally focused on peer pressure, party behavior, and identity. However, the target behavior is not consistently defined in motivational terms or tied tightly to a client-chosen change goal.

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** The interaction is often consultant-like, with the psychiatrist supplying scripts, lists, and experiments. The client's concerns and preferences do not consistently shape the work in a genuinely joint way.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** There are some choices offered, but the psychiatrist repeatedly assigns experiments, scripts, and boundary tasks. This makes the client feel managed rather than fully in charge of deciding whether, when, and how to change.

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** The psychiatrist asks some open questions but tends to supply solutions rather than draw out the client's own values, motivations, and reasons for change. Important change talk emerges but is not consistently amplified or developed.

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Although some input is requested, the overall stance is expert-led and coach-like. The client's role in deciding direction and method is limited, reducing true power sharing.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** The client produces meaningful change talk, such as wanting to be a normal kid and not wanting to keep drinking. The psychiatrist does not consistently evoke, affirm, or deepen this talk with exploratory questions or reflections.

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The psychiatrist is generally non-arguing and acknowledges that both sides can be true. However, sustain talk is often met with tasks and strategies before being fully softened through deeper ambivalence-focused reflections.
