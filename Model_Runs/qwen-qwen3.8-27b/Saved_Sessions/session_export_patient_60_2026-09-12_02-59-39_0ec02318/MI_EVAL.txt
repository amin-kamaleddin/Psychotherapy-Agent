# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

---

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist remains respectful, nonjudgmental, and avoids blaming or shaming Jason for his alcohol use. However, the acceptance stays somewhat procedural and does not fully connect to Jason’s deeper frustrations, identity concerns, or ambivalence.

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The psychiatrist offers accurate reflections of Jason’s annoyance and desire to end the process quickly. These reflections are helpful but remain surface-level and miss the emotional and identity-based meaning behind statements such as “I’m twenty, not fifteen.”

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** The session remains centered on the incident, statement drafting, and procedural next steps rather than maintaining clear focus on alcohol-related behavior or change. As a result, the conversation drifts toward administrative problem-solving instead of building momentum toward behavior change.

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** There is some collaboration, as the psychiatrist offers options and adjusts the statement based on Jason’s preferences. However, the psychiatrist remains the primary problem-solver, making the interaction more expert-driven than fully equal-partnered.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist consistently supports Jason’s choice and control over administrative decisions, such as wording, signing, and process. This support is strong but limited mostly to the statement and process, rather than Jason’s future decisions about alcohol use.

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** The psychiatrist begins with a useful open question but quickly shifts to advising, drafting, and procedural guidance. There is limited evocation of Jason’s internal motivations, values, or discrepancies related to his alcohol use.

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The psychiatrist asks for input and adapts to Jason’s preferences, showing some partnership. However, the overall stance remains fairly expert-driven, with the psychiatrist leading on strategy, legal considerations, and problem-solving.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** There are only rare and weak efforts to elicit Jason’s own reasons for change, such as wanting to study, avoid a record, or be seen as responsible. The session does not meaningfully develop change talk through follow-up questions, scaling, or reflection of his goals.

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** The psychiatrist avoids direct arguing, but does not effectively soften or redirect Jason’s sustain talk, such as minimizing the incident or framing it as normal. The focus on legal details and the written statement may reinforce defensiveness rather than redirect attention to future behavior.
