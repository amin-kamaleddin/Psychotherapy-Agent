# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

---

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist maintained a respectful, nonjudgmental tone and avoided arguing with the client’s ambivalence. Acceptance was somewhat procedural, however, and occasionally narrowed the goal to avoiding trouble.

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist used accurate reflections of the client’s ambivalence and practical concerns. Empathy was strong but remained mostly cognitive and did not fully explore deeper emotions such as shame, fear, or value conflict.

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The session stayed relevant to the alcohol-related incident and did not wander off topic. Direction became too narrowly focused on avoidance strategies and did not consistently connect the incident to broader values, risks, or longer-term change.

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The clinician collaborated well by accepting the client’s preferences and adapting the plan accordingly. The process was strong but still somewhat clinician-led, with the psychiatrist supplying structured options rather than fully client-led exploration.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist repeatedly invited the client’s choice and respected his preferences, showing good autonomy support. Some prompts still gently pushed toward adopting a plan, which kept autonomy support below the highest level.

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The clinician evoked several client-owned practical strategies, but the motivation discussed was mostly about avoiding consequences. There were missed opportunities to evoke deeper intrinsic values such as education, safety, health, or long-term goals.

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist treated the client as the expert on his own life and invited his input throughout the session. Partnership was strong, but the clinician still acted somewhat like a coach or consultant by offering structured options.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The psychiatrist elicited some practical change talk and invited the client to identify doable steps. The change talk was not deeply amplified and remained focused on avoiding consequences rather than intrinsic motivation.

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The psychiatrist did not argue with or challenge the client’s sustain talk. Softening was limited, as the clinician did not consistently use double-sided reflections or discrepancy-based questions to gently shift the conversation toward change.
