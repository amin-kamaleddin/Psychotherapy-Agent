# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

---

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist maintained a respectful, nonjudgmental stance and used reflections that validated the client's perspective. It fell short of a 5 due to mildly evaluative language and limited explicit normalization of ambivalence and repeated struggle.

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Reflective listening accurately captured worry, guilt, pressure, overwhelm, and the meaning of smoking breaks. Empathy remained somewhat surface-level, missing deeper exploration of guilt, identity, and the final concern about possibly smoking in the car.

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The session began with a clear smoking-related focus but shifted toward stress management and immediate coping skills. Direction was coherent around stress reduction but did not consistently guide toward a clear smoking reduction or cessation goal.

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The psychiatrist honored client preferences and adapted to choices such as breathing, going outside, and doing the exercise alone. The session remained largely counselor-led, with the psychiatrist supplying options and scripts rather than co-constructing a client-driven plan.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Autonomy support was strong, with repeated invitations, choices, and acceptance of the client's desire to proceed independently. It was limited by prescriptive scripts and mildly normative statements that introduced some direction.

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The psychiatrist used some open and experiential questions to elicit preferences and feedback. Evocation was limited because the session did not consistently draw out the client's own values, reasons for change, confidence, or motivations for reducing smoking.

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The psychiatrist invited input and adapted to the client's preferred pace and coping options. However, the client was not fully positioned as the expert, and the psychiatrist remained the primary agenda-setter and content provider.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** The client offered meaningful change talk, but the psychiatrist did not effectively reflect, amplify, or build on it. There was little use of change-focused questions, importance/confidence scaling, or future-self exploration to strengthen motivation.

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Sustain talk was acknowledged, but the session remained saturated with reasons for maintaining smoking and stress-focused coping. The psychiatrist mostly offered additional options or education rather than using double-sided reflections, reframing, or strategic pivots toward change.
