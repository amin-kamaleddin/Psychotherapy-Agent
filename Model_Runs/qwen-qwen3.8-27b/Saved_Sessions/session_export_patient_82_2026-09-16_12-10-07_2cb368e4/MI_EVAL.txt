# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

---

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Uses respectful, nonjudgmental language and normalizes Lukas’s ambivalence without shaming or moralizing. Acceptance is strong, though some statements feel mildly assumptive and less deeply individualized than a 5 would require.

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Demonstrates good reflective listening to Lukas’s fear of appearing weak, concern for Tyler, and discomfort with the incident. Moves quickly into options and problem-solving, missing opportunities to explore deeper emotions such as shame, embarrassment, or identity concerns.

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Maintains clear focus on early alcohol use, peer pressure, safety, and the upcoming high-risk situation. The session becomes somewhat task-oriented and directive, prioritizing scripts and a checklist over broader motivational direction.

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Co-constructs the plan with Lukas and builds directly on his ideas, such as the early-practice excuse and mom pickup text. Collaboration is strong but not fully equal because the psychiatrist still supplies much of the plan structure, scripts, and tactics.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Supports autonomy by asking permission, offering options, respecting Lukas’s preference not to involve his father or coach, and inviting him to choose. Some mildly directive moves, such as the final checklist and prompt to send the text now, reduce the emphasis on Lukas being fully in charge.

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Elicits some change talk around responsibility, getting home safely, and feeling better with a plan. Evocation is limited because the psychiatrist often provides reasons, scripts, and options rather than consistently drawing out Lukas’s own values, confidence, and motivation.

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Maintains a collaborative stance by asking permission, inviting Lukas’s views, and using shared language such as “we can.” The psychiatrist still supplies much of the tactical content, which keeps the interaction somewhat expert-driven rather than fully power-shared.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Some change talk emerges, including wanting to get home safely, being responsible, and viewing the plan as a “win.” The session shifts too quickly into script-building and safety planning, with limited reflection, amplification, or consolidation of Lukas’s own reasons for change.

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Avoids arguing with Lukas and validates concerns about looking weak or using the coach as a shield. Softening is inconsistent because there is limited use of double-sided reflections or deeper exploration of the ambivalence behind the sustain talk.
