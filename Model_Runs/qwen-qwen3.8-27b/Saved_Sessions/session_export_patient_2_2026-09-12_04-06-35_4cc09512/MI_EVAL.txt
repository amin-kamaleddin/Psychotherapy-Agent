# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

---

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist maintains a respectful, nonjudgmental stance and accepts Aisha’s boundaries, including her refusal of a patch and preference not to discuss alcohol. Acceptance is generally strong but can feel formulaic, with repeated phrases like “that makes sense” and less explicit affirmation of her value as a mother/person.

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist uses accurate reflections that capture Aisha’s ambivalence and practical worries, such as being caught between protecting her health and needing an evening break. Empathy is solid but does not always go deeply enough into underlying emotions such as fear, grief, or worry about being a bad mother.

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The session stays fairly focused on reducing evening smoking and building a concrete, doable plan. Direction is purposeful, though introducing medication and NRT options briefly broadens the focus and slightly shifts attention away from Aisha’s self-selected target.

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist invites Aisha to choose among options and co-constructs a plan based on her preferences, including tea, delay, backup gum, and a fridge note. Collaboration is good but becomes less equal-partner at times when the psychiatrist shifts into an educational or provider-led mode.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist supports Aisha’s choice by respecting her preferences and avoiding pressure to accept gum or patches. Autonomy support is frequent and respectful, though it could be stronger by more explicitly affirming that Aisha is in full control of her decisions.

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The psychiatrist asks scaling and planning questions that draw out Aisha’s reasons for change, such as protecting her health and being present for her son. Evocation is present but diluted by frequent advice-giving and clinical education, limiting deeper elicitation of Aisha’s own motivations.

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist positions Aisha as a collaborator by asking what she wants to focus on, which options feel doable, and what would increase her confidence. Partnership is strong, though it becomes less balanced when the psychiatrist provides more expert-led information about treatment options.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The psychiatrist helps Aisha articulate change talk around health, cancer concerns, and wanting to be there for her son. The main limitation is that change talk is not consistently amplified, deepened, or connected to broader values before moving into practical planning.

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The psychiatrist avoids arguing with Aisha’s sustain talk, such as comparisons to coworkers or resistance to drastic interventions. However, the session lacks more active MI-consistent softening, such as double-sided reflections or exploration of the function behind Aisha’s ambivalence.
