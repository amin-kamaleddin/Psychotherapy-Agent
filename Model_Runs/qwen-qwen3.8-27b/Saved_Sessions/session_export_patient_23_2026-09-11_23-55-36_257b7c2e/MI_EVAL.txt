# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

---

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist consistently conveys respect, nonjudgment, and support, including clear normalization of setbacks. Warmth is strong but somewhat procedural, with less explicit affirmation of the patient’s deeper experience or inherent worth.

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Reflections are accurate and helpful for practical concerns, such as time pressure and worry about overdoing it. However, empathy remains mostly surface-level and does not fully explore the emotional meaning of drinking as reward or relief.

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The session stays clearly focused on reducing evening alcohol use and builds a concrete, behavior-specific plan. Direction is strong, though the focus becomes somewhat checklist-like and task-driven.

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The patient contributes meaningful ideas, and the final plan incorporates her choices. However, the therapist supplies most of the structure, details, and instructions, so collaboration is present but uneven.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The therapist regularly uses choice-based language, offers options, and supports the patient’s agency around slips. A highly structured and prescriptive plan somewhat limits the sense that the patient is the primary expert on her own change.

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** The approach is largely directive, with many practical strategies and reasons for change offered by the therapist. There are few attempts to elicit the patient’s own values, motivations, confidence, or change talk.

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The therapist invites input and asks which options feel doable, and the patient’s ideas shape the plan. The session remains more expert-led than truly power-sharing, limiting the MI spirit of partnership.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** The patient offers some change-related language, such as choosing two glasses and framing one glass as progress. The therapist rarely reflects, amplifies, or expands this talk, and evocative change talk is limited.

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The therapist avoids arguing with sustain talk and validates concerns about wine as a reward and the adequacy of two glasses. These statements are often acknowledged but quickly redirected to action rather than explored to resolve ambivalence.
