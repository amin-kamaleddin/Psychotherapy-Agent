# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

---

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist maintained a respectful, nonjudgmental tone and acknowledged the patient’s time constraint and plan. Acceptance was generally supportive but remained somewhat generic and did not fully validate the underlying safety anxiety.

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Reflections accurately captured that the patient was rushed, worried, and prepared to contact her doctor. However, empathy was surface-level and missed deeper emotional concerns about not making a dangerous medication error.

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The session stayed focused on the target issue of medication safety and contacting the prescriber. However, repeated safety reminders and offers to review the email created a mild loop and reduced forward momentum toward a clear change process.

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** There was partial collaboration, as the psychiatrist invited input and responded to the patient’s preference to handle the email herself. The interaction remained largely one-directional, with the clinician providing the answer, script, and repeated offers of help.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The psychiatrist generally respected the patient’s need to leave and did not pressure her to stay or accept help. Autonomy support was inconsistent, as repeated offers and safety reminders kept the clinician in a somewhat directing role rather than explicitly reinforcing client ownership.

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** The opening question was evocative, but the psychiatrist quickly shifted into direct information-giving mode. There were few efforts to draw out the patient’s own reasons, values, concerns, or confidence in handling the medication decision.

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** The psychiatrist assumed an expert-driven, instructive role by providing the clinical answer, script, and repeated offers to draft or review the message. There was limited co-creation, negotiation of agenda, or explicit acknowledgment that the patient was the ultimate decision-maker.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** The psychiatrist affirmed the patient’s safety-oriented statements but did not actively evoke or expand change talk. Questions were not used to explore reasons, values, ability, confidence, or next steps in a way that strengthened motivation.

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The psychiatrist acknowledged the patient’s need to leave without arguing or becoming defensive. However, sustain talk related to time pressure was not strategically redirected toward a brief, client-driven change step.
