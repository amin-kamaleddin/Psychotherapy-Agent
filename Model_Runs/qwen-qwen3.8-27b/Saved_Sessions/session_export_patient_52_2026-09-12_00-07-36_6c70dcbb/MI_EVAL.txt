# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

---

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist uses a respectful, nonjudgmental tone and validates exhaustion, guilt, and burden without shaming drinking. Acceptance is strong but could be warmer by more explicitly affirming the client's caregiving efforts and effort to seek support.

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** There are accurate reflections about exhaustion, guilt, and the difficulty of asking for help, but the counselor often moves quickly into problem-solving. Deeper empathy would better reflect the ambivalence, such as needing a reward while fearing harm to time with her son.

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The session stays connected to alcohol use, stress, and a concrete target around asking her sister for help. However, the focus is scattered across many topics, which weakens the clarity and focus of the session.

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The psychiatrist adapts to the client's preferences, such as avoiding beach imagery and choosing one small step, which supports moderate collaboration. However, much of the plan is counselor-driven rather than fully co-created with the client.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The psychiatrist offers choices and asks which option feels most doable, which supports client choice. Autonomy support is limited by directive language, multiple added suggestions, and a drafted message that may reduce the sense of client ownership.

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** A few evocative questions are used, but the session is dominated by practical suggestions and structured tools. More evocation would be needed to draw out the client's own values, reasons for change, and preferred next step.

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** The counselor frequently takes an expert, problem-solving role by offering scripts, breathing exercises, journaling formats, and backup options. Partnership is limited because the client is not consistently positioned as the primary expert on her own life.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** The client offers useful change talk, such as trying one drink and asking her sister for childcare, but the psychiatrist does not consistently develop or amplify it. More questions and reflections could have strengthened the client's own motivation for change.

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Sustain talk is prominent, including the need for a reward, worry about resting without alcohol, and distrust of a paid sitter. The psychiatrist responds nonjudgmentally but does not consistently soften or reframe these statements toward change.
