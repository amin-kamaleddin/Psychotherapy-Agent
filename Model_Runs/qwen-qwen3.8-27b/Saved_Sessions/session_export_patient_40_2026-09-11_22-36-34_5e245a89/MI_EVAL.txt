# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

---

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist consistently demonstrates respect, nonjudgment, and validation without labeling or moralizing the relapse. However, the acceptance is somewhat formulaic and quickly shifts into safety screening and planning, limiting deeper individualized affirmation.

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The psychiatrist uses some reflective statements and normalizes ambivalence, but the empathy is often brief and cognitive. There are missed opportunities to more fully reflect shame, fear, vulnerability, and the emotional complexity behind the client’s statements.

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The session maintains a strong focus on relapse prevention, triggers, coping steps, supports, and follow-up. However, the direction becomes procedural and directive at times, with less explicit connection to the client’s own stated values and goals.

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** There is shared problem-solving, particularly in developing the relapse-prevention plan and meeting intro. However, the psychiatrist often acts as the primary problem-solver by providing the plan, scripts, and coping steps, creating a less equal collaborative dynamic.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The counselor frequently supports Elias’s autonomy by offering choices, reducing pressure, and emphasizing that the plan can be adapted. However, some recommendations remain directive, such as setting a specific goal to attend a meeting this month.

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The counselor asks useful questions about what has kept Elias sober, triggers, and support needs, eliciting useful motivations. However, evocation is limited because the conversation often moves into advising and structuring before fully developing the client’s own reasons for change.

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Elias is invited to make choices and contribute, such as selecting phone meetings and identifying supports. However, the session often becomes expert-driven, with the psychiatrist presenting a detailed plan before fully inviting Elias to co-create his own approach.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The psychiatrist elicits meaningful change talk, including the client’s children, avoidance of shame, and value of a written plan. However, there are fewer opportunities to deepen, amplify, or strategically reinforce these statements through reflective questions.

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The psychiatrist generally responds to sustain talk in a non-adversarial way and normalizes ambivalence. However, the responses are often procedural and advice-based, with limited use of double-sided reflections to hold both sides of the ambivalence.
