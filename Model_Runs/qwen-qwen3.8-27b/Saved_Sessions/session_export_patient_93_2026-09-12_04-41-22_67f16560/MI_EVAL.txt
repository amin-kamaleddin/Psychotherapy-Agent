# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

---

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist maintained a respectful, nonjudgmental stance and avoided criticizing Margaret’s drinking or choices. Acceptance was strong but somewhat procedural, with limited explicit affirmation of her strengths, values, or resilience.

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Reflections accurately captured Margaret’s ambivalence, practical concerns, and motivational shifts. The main limitation was that empathy remained mostly cognitive and did not fully validate deeper emotions such as fear, loneliness, or irritation.

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The session stayed clearly focused on reducing alcohol use, especially the 6 p.m. drinking cue and fall prevention. Direction was strong, though it focused more on immediate implementation than consistently linking behavior change to deeper values or long-term goals.

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Margaret meaningfully contributed to the plan, including walking, timing, fallback options, and reminders. However, the psychiatrist remained the primary architect of the conversation, giving the session a somewhat expert-driven rather than fully client-led quality.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist consistently reinforced Margaret’s right to choose and respected her preference against abstinence or monitoring. There was still a mild push for follow-up after Tuesday, despite her expressed preference to keep the process private and low-pressure.

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The psychiatrist used open questions and importance/confidence rulers to evoke motivation and client-generated ideas. Evocation was present but limited because the conversation shifted toward structured planning without deeper exploration of Margaret’s values, identity, or personal strengths.

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The psychiatrist invited input and offered choices, showing some partnership. However, the session was often driven by the psychiatrist’s agenda and structured options, limiting genuine co-direction.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Margaret expressed useful change talk about staying safe, remaining independent, and improving balance, and the psychiatrist acknowledged some of it. The evocation was not consistently deepened, with missed opportunities to amplify her own reasons for change.

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The psychiatrist generally avoided arguing and accepted Margaret’s “cut back rather than quit” frame without becoming defensive. Sustain talk was mostly bypassed or neutrally reflected rather than strategically redirected toward resolving ambivalence.
