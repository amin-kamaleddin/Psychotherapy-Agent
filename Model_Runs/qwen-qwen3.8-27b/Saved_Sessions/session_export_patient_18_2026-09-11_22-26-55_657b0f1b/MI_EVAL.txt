# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

---

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist maintained a respectful, nonjudgmental tone and acknowledged the client’s frustration without shaming her. It fell slightly short of a 5 because the supportive warmth was occasionally interrupted by a practical, task-focused stance.

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The psychiatrist offered brief reflections and recognized the client’s annoyance and sense of unfairness. However, the empathy remained surface-level, with limited exploration of the client’s deeper concerns about being flagged, privacy, and her mother’s reaction.

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The session stayed generally connected to the alcohol screening and check-in, providing enough structure to avoid aimlessness. It lacked a stronger strategic focus on exploring alcohol use, risk, and the client’s own motivation for change.

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** There was partial collaboration, especially when co-creating a text for the client’s mother and offering options. However, the psychiatrist often acted as the primary source of solutions, making the collaboration uneven and more advisory than mutually exploratory.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The psychiatrist acknowledged choice by asking what the client wanted and offering options. This was undercut by the frequent use of scripts and prescriptive guidance that may have made the client feel directed rather than autonomous.

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Evocation was weak because the psychiatrist supplied much of the content, rationale, and language rather than eliciting the client’s own concerns and motivations. The client’s values, goals, and reasons for change were not deeply explored or expanded.

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The psychiatrist showed some partnership by asking for input and offering choices early in the session. Over time, the conversation became more counselor-led, with the psychiatrist providing scripts and guiding the client toward specific responses.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** The client spontaneously offered some change-relevant statements, such as not wanting to lie to a counselor. The psychiatrist did not consistently build on this language or use open questions to strengthen the client’s own reasons for change.

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** The client expressed several sustain themes, including minimizing the alcohol use and wanting to keep the matter low-profile. The psychiatrist responded mainly with practical scripts rather than using MI-consistent reflections or exploration to soften these statements.
