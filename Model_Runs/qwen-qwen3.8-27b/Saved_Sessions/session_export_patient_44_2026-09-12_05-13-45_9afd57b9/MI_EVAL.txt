# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

---

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist consistently uses warm, nonjudgmental language and avoids shaming the client for missed gym days or snacking. However, the acceptance is somewhat tied to the client's progress and could be deeper by affirming Erik's worth independent of outcomes and explicitly normalizing the discouragement of comparing himself to younger gym-goers.

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist demonstrates good reflective listening and accurately captures the client's ambivalence, such as summarizing that gym progress feels positive while comparisons and automatic snack moments create frustration and guilt. It falls short of a 5 because the reflections mostly paraphrase content rather than fully exploring the emotional nuance of feeling "like a kid," "behind," or afraid of failing after a missed day.

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The session maintains a clear focus on the target behavior of lifestyle change, especially the afternoon candy-bar urge and self-regulation. It begins broadly but quickly narrows to a specific, actionable target.

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist behaves as an equal partner by co-constructing the plan step by step and responding to the client's language, such as "data-focused" and the "receipt" metaphor. It is not a 5 because the psychiatrist still leads with a sequence of structured questions and provides the menu of possible prompts, which slightly limits the sense of fully mutual agenda-setting.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist supports choice by asking the client what he would like to change, what he is willing to try, and how he would handle a slip rather than issuing directives. It is not a 5 because there is less explicit language affirming the client's complete freedom and ownership, such as "This is your plan and you decide what is realistic for you."

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist effectively evokes the client's own motivation and strategies, using confidence scaling, obstacle planning, and questions about what would make follow-through easier. A 5 would require more consistent evocation of deeper personal motivation, such as why this change matters to Erik, what it represents for his health, self-image, or future, and more explicit reflection that amplifies his emerging confidence.

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The session is collaborative and largely driven by the client's own ideas. It is not a 5 because the psychiatrist still provides the structure and offers examples such as phone alarm, calendar alert, or leaving the bottle by the door, which makes the interaction more therapist-paced than fully client-directed.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist frequently elicits change talk through open questions such as "When things go well for you, what helps you stay on track?" and confidence scaling from 0–10. A 5 would require more consistently amplifying or deepening the client's own change talk, such as asking what this plan protects about his health, identity, or energy.

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The client offers sustain talk about slipping until Thursday, feeling discouraged by younger lifters, and reaching for snacks automatically, and the psychiatrist does not reinforce or argue with this talk. However, the responses are more neutral redirections than active softening of sustain talk, and stronger MI would use double-sided reflections and explore the function of the snack or comparison.
