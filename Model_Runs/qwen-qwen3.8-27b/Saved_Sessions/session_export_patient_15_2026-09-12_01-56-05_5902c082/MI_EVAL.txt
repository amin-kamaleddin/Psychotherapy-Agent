# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

---

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The psychiatrist maintained a respectful, non-judgmental tone and acknowledged the patient without criticizing missed doses. Affirmation was present but somewhat task-focused, with limited deeper recognition of the patient’s broader struggles and ambivalence.

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Reflective statements tracked the patient’s plan and preferences accurately, showing adequate understanding. However, reflections remained mostly factual and did not deeply explore emotional meaning, ambivalence, or the significance of keeping things simple.

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The session stayed consistently focused on medication adherence and a concrete routine, which was a clear strength. Direction was limited because the broader case target, including alcohol use and its relation to blood pressure, was not addressed.

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The psychiatrist invited input and incorporated some patient preferences, such as using coffee as a cue and avoiding noisy alarms. Collaboration was uneven because the psychiatrist supplied most of the ideas while the patient often responded passively.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist regularly offered options, respected the patient’s preference for a simple plan, and avoided pressuring larger commitments. Autonomy was well supported, though active steering through repeated suggestions and confidence ratings slightly limited full patient-led choice.

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The use of importance and confidence rulers elicited some movement and helped the patient articulate a small step. Evocation remained surface-level, with limited exploration of personal values, deeper reasons for change, or intrinsic motivation.

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The patient was asked for input and preferences, creating a collaborative feel. However, the psychiatrist carried much of the problem-solving by offering many specific solutions, reducing the patient’s role as the expert in his own routine.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The psychiatrist repeatedly elicited change talk by asking about importance, confidence, barriers, and small steps, and the patient expressed willingness to try a plan. The change talk was present but narrow, with missed opportunities to connect it to the patient’s values or life goals.

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The psychiatrist did not argue with or amplify the patient’s minimizing statements and instead framed the plan as practical and low-key. Softer responses could have more skillfully acknowledged sustain talk while preserving the motivation for change.
