# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

---

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist maintained a respectful, nonjudgmental stance and validated the patient’s concerns about school, parents, and social awkwardness. Some warmth was procedural, and frequent advice-giving slightly reduced the sense of unconditional positive regard.

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Reflections were generally accurate and helpful, especially in naming the patient’s desire to avoid embarrassment and protect important relationships. They remained mostly content-level and did not fully capture deeper social or emotional tensions around peer pressure.

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The session stayed clearly focused on the target behavior of refusing alcohol and exiting social situations without escalation. The direction was practical and helpful, though somewhat narrow and tactical rather than broadly linked to personal values or long-term goals.

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** There was collaboration in that the patient selected and practiced specific refusal strategies and exit options. However, the interaction was fairly counselor-led, with the psychiatrist generating most of the suggestions rather than co-creating them with the patient.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist supported choice by asking which options felt doable, most natural, or easiest to try, and affirmed the patient’s right to keep responses brief. The score was limited by the heavy reliance on prescriptive scripts and tactics rather than more client-generated solutions.

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** There was an early attempt to evoke the patient’s own priorities by asking what mattered most when deciding whether to drink. After that, the session shifted heavily into providing external scripts and strategies rather than continuing to elicit the patient’s own motivations, values, and confidence.

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The psychiatrist invited the patient to choose and practice options, which supported some partnership. The overall stance was still fairly directive, as the psychiatrist quickly moved into an expert coaching role and supplied a list of strategies rather than first exploring the patient’s own ideas.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** The patient expressed some change-related statements, such as feeling able to handle the refusal and recognizing that being a little awkward is better than drinking. The psychiatrist did not consistently reflect, deepen, or amplify this change talk, relying more on supplied scripts than on drawing out the patient’s own reasons for change.

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist handled sustain talk about social concerns by avoiding disagreement and reframing the patient’s worries in a way that preserved dignity and reduced pressure. The approach was effective but could have been stronger with more sophisticated ambivalence work or double-sided reflections.
