                                Motivational Interviewing Feedback Criteria (5-Point Likert Scale)

Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)
------------------------------------
1. Acceptance: Extent to which the counselor communicates unconditional positive regard for the client.
   1 = Judgmental, disapproving, labeling
   2 = Inconsistent respect, occasional judgment
   3 = Neutral stance, basic respect
   4 = Generally respectful and supportive
   5 = Consistently respectful, warm, supportive

2. Empathy: Extent to which the counselor understands and/or makes an effort to accurately understand the client’s perspective.
   1 = Little effort to understand, minimal reflective listening
   2 = Limited perspective-taking, mostly factual focus
   3 = Adequate but inconsistent effort to understand
   4 = Good reflective listening and perspective-taking
   5 = Highly accurate, consistent understanding and reflections

3. Direction – Degree to which the counselor maintains appropriate focus on a specific target behavior or concerns directly tied to it.
1 = No clear focus; session wanders aimlessly
2 = Minimal focus; frequent topic drift
3 = Occasional focus on target behavior but inconsistent
4 = Generally keeps discussion directed toward target behavior
5 = Consistently maintains clear focus and guides toward change

---

4. Collaboration – Extent to which the clinician behaves as if the interview is occurring between two equal partners, both of whom have knowledge that might be useful in the problem under consideration.
1 = Authoritarian or expert-driven stance
2 = Limited collaboration; frequent direction by counselor
3 = Partial collaboration; uneven power-sharing
4 = Strong collaborative stance most of the time
5 = Fully collaborative; client’s ideas shape session direction

---

5. Autonomy Support – Extent to which clinician supports and actively fosters client perception of choice as opposed to attempting to control the client’s behavior or choices.
1 = Directive or pressuring; minimizes choice
2 = Occasionally acknowledges choice but inconsistently
3 = Neutral regarding client autonomy
4 = Regularly affirms client freedom to choose
5 = Consistently reinforces client ownership of change process

---

6. Evocation – Extent to which the clinician conveys an understanding that motivation for change, and the ability to move toward that change, reside mostly within the client and therefore focuses efforts to elicit and expand it within the therapeutic interaction.
1 = Provides reasons for change without exploring client view
2 = Limited interest in client motivations
3 = Some evocation but superficial
4 = Frequently draws out client’s own reasons for change
5 = Consistently elicits and develops client change talk throughout session

---

7. Partnership – Extent to which the clinician conveys an understanding that expertise and wisdom about change reside mostly within the client

1 = Clinician actively assumes the expert role for the majority of the interaction with the client. Collaboration or partnership is absent.
2 = Clinician superficially responds to opportunities to collaborate.
3 = Clinician incorporates client’s contributions but does so in a lukewarm or erratic fashion.
4 = Clinician fosters collaboration and power sharing so that client’s contributions impact the session in ways that they otherwise would not.
5 = Clinician actively fosters and encourages power sharing in the interaction in such a way that client’s contributions substantially influence the nature of the session.

---

8. Cultivating Change Talk – Extent to which the clinician actively encourages the client’s own language in favor of the change goal, and confidence for making that change.
1 = No effort to evoke change talk
2 = Rare or weak efforts
3 = Occasional evocation of change language
4 = Frequent evocation of change talk
5 = Consistent, skillful evocation throughout

---

9. Softening Sustain Talk – Extent that the clinician avoids a focus on the reasons against changing or for maintaining the status quo.
1 = Reinforces sustain talk
2 = Rarely shifts focus away from sustain talk
3 = Occasionally redirects toward change
4 = Often redirects effectively
5 = Consistently pivots client language toward change

Evaluation Guidance
-------------------
- All domains are rated on a 1–5 Likert scale.
- Ratings should reflect overall quality and consistency of behavior.
- Focus on both counselor skillfulness and client speech as indicators of MI process quality.

 # Motivational Interviewing Feedback: Psychiatrist Evaluation

## Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)

### 1. Acceptance
Score: 4/5

Explanation:
The psychiatrist consistently demonstrates respect and non-judgment throughout the session. Phrases such as 'I respect that it was your choice,' 'I really hear you,' 'I want to honor that,' and 'I respect that completely' communicate clear positive regard. The psychiatrist never judges, labels, or disapproves of Marisol's decision. However, the acceptance, while consistent, is somewhat one-dimensional — it primarily manifests as validation of the status quo choice rather than a deeper, more individualized warmth. There is no acknowledgment of Marisol's broader self-worth, her values beyond the piercing, or any recognition of the complexity of her situation. A score of 5 would require more nuanced, person-centered acceptance that goes beyond 'your choice is valid' to a genuine connection with the whole person.

### 2. Empathy
Score: 3/5

Explanation:
The psychiatrist makes adequate efforts at reflective listening, with some accurate reflections such as 'It makes sense that this choice felt intentional and meaningful to you' and 'I can imagine it's a little frustrating when someone raises something you've already decided about — like they're questioning your judgment.' This last reflection shows genuine perspective-taking and emotional attunement. However, the overall empathy is limited in depth and consistency. The psychiatrist does not explore the emotional underpinnings of Marisol's identity, her values of autonomy and self-expression, or the deeper meaning the piercing holds for her. The reflections tend to be surface-level paraphrases rather than complex reflections that delve into underlying feelings or meanings. There are also missed opportunities — for example, when Marisol says 'I just wanted a change,' the psychiatrist does not explore what kind of change she was seeking or what was driving that desire. The empathy is functional but not deep.

---

### 3. Cultivating Change Talk
Score: 1/5

Explanation:
This is the most significant weakness of the session. The psychiatrist makes virtually no effort to evoke, elicit, or cultivate any change talk from Marisol. There are no questions that explore what might be different if she reconsidered, no scaling questions, no 'what if' explorations, and no reflections that might expand any nascent ambivalence. When Marisol says 'I don't see a reason to change my mind,' the psychiatrist simply validates this rather than gently exploring whether there is any part of her that is even slightly open to consideration. The psychiatrist does not ask about her confidence in the long-term outcome, does not explore what 'taking care of it' looks like, and does not probe the dentist's specific concerns in a way that might open a door. The entire session effectively reinforces the status quo without any attempt to elicit even a sliver of change talk. In MI with a pre-contemplation client, the clinician should still be looking for and nurturing any seeds of change, however small. None were sought here.

### 4. Softening Sustain Talk
Score: 2/5

Explanation:
Rather than softening or redirecting sustain talk, the psychiatrist largely reinforces it. When Marisol says 'I already knew what I was doing' and 'I'm not asking anyone else to make that call for me,' the psychiatrist responds with full validation: 'It makes complete sense that you'd want your decision to be respected rather than second-guessed' and 'You're the one who knows your body and your choices best.' While avoiding arguing with the client is important in MI, the psychiatrist goes further than merely not reinforcing sustain talk — she actively amplifies it. The final exchanges ('You made your choice and you're good with it — I respect that completely' and 'You're doing what feels right for you, and that's all that matters') essentially close the door on any further exploration. A more effective MI approach would have acknowledged the sustain talk while gently leaving room for the client to explore the other side, or at minimum not have so firmly sealed the conversation around the status quo position.

### 5. Partnership

The psychiatrist maintains a non-expert stance and does not position herself as having authority over Marisol's decision. The session direction is entirely set by the psychiatrist's agenda (validate her choice, leave the door open, close). The client's contributions do not substantially shape where the conversation goes. A true partnership would have involved negotiating the agenda and allowing Marisol's own curiosity or concerns to drive the exploration.

### 6. Direction
Score: 2/5

Explanation:
The session lacks clear direction toward any therapeutic goal. While the topic (the piercing) is maintained, there is no strategic progression or focus on a specific target behavior or concern. The session feels aimless in a therapeutic sense — it moves from validation to more validation to more validation without any movement toward exploration, insight, or action. Even for a pre-contemplation client, there should be a sense of gentle direction toward at least exploring the client's perspective more deeply or identifying what information might be useful to them. ### 7. Autonomy Support
Score: 4/5

Explanation:
The psychiatrist consistently affirms Marisol's autonomy and right to make her own choices. True autonomy support in MI would also affirm the client's capacity to change if she chose to, and would present autonomy as the freedom to explore any option, not just the freedom to maintain the current one.

### 9. Evocation

**Overall Global Ratings Score:** **2.8/5**

The session demonstrates a respectful, non-judgmental approach that avoids the common mistake of arguing with a resistant client. The psychiatrist appropriately honors Marisol's autonomy and does not pressure her. However, the session is fundamentally passive and lacks the active, strategic elements that define skilled motivational interviewing. The most critical gap is the complete absence of any effort to evoke change talk or explore the client's values and motivations in depth. The psychiatrist's approach of validating the status quo at every turn, without any gentle exploration of the other side or any evocative questions, effectively reinforces Marisol's pre-contemplation stance rather than creating the conditions for it to potentially shift. The session reads more as a supportive validation conversation than a motivational interview. For a client in pre-contemplation, the MI clinician's job is not to argue for change but to create the conditions for the client to explore it — through evocation, gentle exploration of values, and strategic reflections that might open a door. None of these were attempted here. The trainee should focus on: (1) developing evocative questions that draw out the client's own values and goals, (2) using strategic reflections (double-sided, paradoxical) that acknowledge sustain talk while leaving room for change, (3) establishing a clearer therapeutic direction, and (4) finding even the smallest seeds of ambivalence to nurture rather than reinforcing the 'I've decided' narrative uncritically.
