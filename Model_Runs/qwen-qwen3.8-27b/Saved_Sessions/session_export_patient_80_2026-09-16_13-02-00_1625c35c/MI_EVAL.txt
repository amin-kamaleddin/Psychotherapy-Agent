# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

---

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The psychiatrist is generally respectful and nonjudgmental, but uses clinical/risk-management language that can subtly pathologize the client. Affirmations often approve the plan rather than showing deeper acceptance of the person.

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The psychiatrist accurately reflects practical content but provides limited reflection of the client's emotions, ambivalence, and identity concerns. Deeper responses to statements like “I don’t want to be that guy” could strengthen the therapeutic alliance.

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 5
**Comments:** The session remains tightly focused on reducing alcohol use and managing aggression, especially around Friday nights and work stress. There is clear forward momentum and minimal drift from the target behaviors.

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist collaborates effectively by co-constructing a practical plan built on the client's ideas. The score is limited because the psychiatrist often leads the agenda and supplies detailed solutions, making the collaboration somewhat counselor-guided.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The client is offered choices and preferences are respected, but there are several directive moments, including ready-made scripts and specific instructions. Greater autonomy support would involve helping the client generate his own wording, timing, and safety plan.

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist uses open questions, confidence scaling, and prompts for doable steps to draw out the client's motivation. However, frequent movement into problem-solving and advice can reduce deeper evocation of the client's values and internal reasons for change.

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist frequently invites input, and the client's ideas clearly shape the plan. The expert role in supplying detailed scripts and steps slightly reduces the sense that the client is the primary expert on his own change.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist effectively evokes change talk by asking about helpful factors, triggers, doable steps, and confidence. The client generates strong statements such as “non-negotiable” and “one Friday at a time,” though values-based exploration could deepen this further.

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist avoids arguing and reframes sustain talk into progress and realistic planning, including the client's emphasis on pausing rather than promising. More active MI strategies such as double-sided reflections could further help resolve ambivalence.
