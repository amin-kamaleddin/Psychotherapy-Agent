# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

---

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist communicates consistent nonjudgmental respect and unconditional positive regard, helping the client feel safe and not overreacting. However, the acceptance sometimes feels formulaic and procedural rather than deeply individualized to her full experience.

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The psychiatrist offers adequate empathic reflections and normalizes the client’s overwhelm, but often shifts quickly into problem-solving. Deeper emotional layers, such as fear of judgment, shame, and worry about failing as a parent, are not consistently explored.

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The session maintains a general focus on medical follow-up and small next steps, but momentum is diluted by safety checks, grounding, childcare logistics, and message drafting. A clearer, more deliberate focus on the target behavior would strengthen the session.

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The psychiatrist invites the client’s input and adjusts to her preferences, such as choosing a saved message rather than sending it. However, much of the session content is clinician-generated, limiting truly collaborative, co-created problem-solving.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist consistently emphasizes the client’s freedom to choose, using noncoercive language such as “no pressure,” “you don’t have to decide now,” and “you’re allowed to stop.” This is a major strength, though repeated new options can still feel gently pressuring.

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** This is a major area for growth because the psychiatrist often moves quickly into advice, triage, and practical solutions. There are relatively few questions designed to draw out the client’s own values, motivations, concerns, or confidence.

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The psychiatrist avoids a purely authoritarian stance and honors the client’s preferences in several moments. However, the clinician still frequently drives the agenda by supplying structure, scripts, and plans, making the partnership uneven.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** The client expresses meaningful change talk, including recognition that she should do something and tentative willingness to consider small steps. The psychiatrist does not consistently notice, reflect, or amplify this change talk to strengthen the client’s own motivation.

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The psychiatrist generally avoids arguing with the client’s sustain talk and uses normalizing language to reduce defensiveness. However, some permissive statements may unintentionally reinforce avoidance, and there is limited strategic redirection toward change.
