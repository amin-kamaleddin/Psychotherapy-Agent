# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

---

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The psychiatrist generally used normalizing, nonjudgmental language but sometimes pushed additional steps after the patient set a boundary. A higher score would require more consistent affirmation of the patient's pace, identity, and autonomy.

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Early reflections showed understanding of the patient's ambivalence and fear, but empathy later became more surface-level and repetitive. Deeper emotions such as shame, fear of being seen, or feeling labeled were not fully explored.

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The session stayed focused on safer use, infection risk, and a concrete step with fentanyl test strips. The main limitation was that the conversation became somewhat logistics-driven and repetitive around testing, naloxone, and reminders.

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The psychiatrist offered options and the patient shaped parts of the plan, such as online ordering and a simple checklist. However, collaboration remained procedural rather than deeply egalitarian, as the psychiatrist continued to lead with structured recommendations.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** There were autonomy-supportive statements and respect for the patient's preference for a simple, private plan. Autonomy support was weakened by repeated naloxone suggestions and the reminder offer, which the patient experienced as intrusive.

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The psychiatrist used open questions that elicited some change talk about avoiding infection and knowing what was in the powder. The main limitation was frequent shifts toward supplying information rather than further evoking the patient's own values, confidence, and reasons for change.

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The psychiatrist invited input and used collaborative language, and the patient's preferences influenced parts of the plan. However, the psychiatrist maintained a strong expert/information-providing role rather than consistently acknowledging the patient as the expert on his own life.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The psychiatrist elicited meaningful change talk about not wanting to die from a bad batch and wanting to know what was in the powder. These statements were affirmed but not consistently deepened or expanded through further evocative follow-up.

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The psychiatrist generally avoided arguing with the patient's sustain talk and acknowledged his preference to keep things simple. However, softening was more passive than skillful, with limited double-sided reflections and continued introduction of naloxone after the patient declined.
