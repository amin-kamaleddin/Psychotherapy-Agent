# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

---

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist is respectful and nonjudgmental, honoring Aisha’s boundary around drinking. Acceptance is strong but sometimes procedural rather than deeply warm.

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** There are appropriate empathic reflections about tiredness and the pain of slipping. However, responses remain surface-level and miss deeper guilt, fear, and ambivalence.

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The session stays clearly focused on reducing evening cigarettes through a small, manageable plan. The main limitation is that it becomes somewhat repetitive with implementation details.

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Aisha’s preferences help shape the plan, such as the 10-minute kitchen pause and “not yet.” However, the psychiatrist often leads with a menu of techniques before fully exploring her preferences.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Aisha is invited to choose and may decline options, which supports her autonomy. Yet the volume of direct suggestions can make the plan feel clinician-driven.

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The psychiatrist successfully elicits some client-generated ideas, such as cutting down and using “not yet.” There are missed opportunities to draw out Aisha’s values, confidence, and deeper motivations.

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Aisha’s choices meaningfully shape parts of the session. The interaction remains partly expert-driven because of frequent introduction of multiple strategies.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The psychiatrist elicits useful change talk about cutting down and using “not yet.” However, there are relatively few attempts to deepen Aisha’s own reasons for change.

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Sustain talk is generally accepted without arguing or confrontation. Statements like “I can’t stop myself” are met more with reassurance or advice than with reflective softening.
