# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

---

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist generally communicates respect, nonjudgment, and support for the patient's choices. The score is limited because the therapist occasionally shifts into practical problem-solving in a way that can feel subtly persuasive rather than purely accepting.

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist demonstrates strong reflective listening and accurately captures the patient's burden, exhaustion, and confidence concerns. It does not reach a 5 because the therapist mostly paraphrases practical frustrations without consistently exploring deeper emotions such as resentment, loss of autonomy, or fear of failure.

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The session maintains a clear and MI-appropriate focus on a specific asthma-management target, moving from broad frustration to a concrete small step. It is limited by becoming somewhat narrowly focused on logistics and repeatedly steering toward a tiny step before enough change talk has been developed.

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** There is some collaboration, especially when the therapist builds on the patient's jacket-pocket idea and invites him to choose. However, much of the interaction is therapist-driven, with repeated lists of options that reduce true co-construction and can create an uneven power dynamic.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The therapist consistently supports autonomy by emphasizing that the patient has choice and does not force commitment. The score is limited because the repeated brainstorming and scaling questions still carry a subtle push toward change.

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The therapist uses open questions and confidence scaling to elicit motivation and intention. The evocation remains somewhat superficial because the therapist quickly shifts from the patient's own values and language to practical suggestions without deeply developing his motivations.

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The psychiatrist shows clear partnership behaviors by asking the patient what he would like to explore and following his lead at points. The partnership is uneven because the therapist repeatedly introduces practical suggestions and keeps pushing for the next tiny step, making the interaction feel more clinician-led.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The therapist elicits some change-relevant language through questions and scaling, but does not consistently deepen, amplify, or reflect that change talk. The performance would be stronger with more exploration of what the patient's own statements reveal about his values, abilities, and reasons for change.

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The therapist appropriately acknowledges sustain talk without arguing or reinforcing it. However, the therapist often responds by offering another practical option instead of using double-sided reflections or effectively pivoting the patient's language toward change.
