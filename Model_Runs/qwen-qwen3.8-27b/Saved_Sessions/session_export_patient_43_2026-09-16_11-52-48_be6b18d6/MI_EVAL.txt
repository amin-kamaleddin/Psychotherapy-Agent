# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

---

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist was consistently respectful, nonjudgmental, and accepting of the patient’s defensive stance, using affirming language that reduced threat. The acceptance was professional and safe, though somewhat procedural rather than deeply warm.

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist demonstrated good reflective listening and accurate summaries of the patient’s practical concerns, work stress, and desire for independence. However, empathy remained mainly at the surface level and did not deeply explore the emotional meaning behind the patient’s defensiveness, pride, or fear.

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The session generally stayed focused on the referred issue of smoking, work stress, and health concerns, with clear narrowing to a manageable threshold. Direction was strong, though it leaned more toward reassurance and monitoring than toward progressively clarifying smoking-related change.

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist collaborated by offering options, inviting input, and incorporating the patient’s own wording into a plan. However, the counselor still provided considerable structure, so collaboration was strong but not fully equal throughout.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 5
**Comments:** Autonomy support was a major strength, with repeated emphasis on the patient’s control, choice, and self-direction. The psychiatrist respected the patient’s lack of readiness to quit and avoided coercive commitments, leaving decisions with the patient.

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The psychiatrist made some effort to elicit the patient’s own priorities, readiness, threshold, and preferred language. However, evocation was relatively shallow and did not deeply explore values, fears, hopes, or perceived benefits of changing smoking behavior.

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist conveyed a collaborative, non-authoritarian stance and invited the patient to shape focus, wording, and small steps. The main limitation was that the counselor still drove parts of the agenda through repeated scripts, check-ins, and closing prompts.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** The patient offered only weak, monitoring-focused statements rather than strong change talk about reducing or quitting smoking. The psychiatrist did not consistently elicit, reinforce, or expand importance, confidence, desire, need, ability, or values related to change.

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** The psychiatrist appropriately avoided arguing but often accepted the patient’s status-quo statements without effectively redirecting them toward change. More double-sided reflections and exploratory questions were needed to widen ambivalence and keep the door open to change.
