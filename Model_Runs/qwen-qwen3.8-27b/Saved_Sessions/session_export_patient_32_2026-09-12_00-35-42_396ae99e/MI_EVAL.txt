# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

---

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist was consistently respectful, nonjudgmental, and affirming of Derrick’s honesty and difficulty. Acceptance was strong but somewhat procedural, with praise tied more to completing the plan than fully acknowledging his ambivalence.

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The psychiatrist offered basic reflections and validation, but empathy remained surface-level and task-focused. There were missed opportunities to reflect deeper values, independence, shame, fear, and ambivalence.

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 5
**Comments:** The session maintained a clear, consistent focus on avoiding future DUIs and driving after drinking. The psychiatrist stayed on target, avoided drift, and guided Derrick toward a concrete harm-reduction plan.

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Derrick’s choices clearly shaped the plan, and the psychiatrist built on his ideas rather than imposing a fixed approach. Collaboration was strong but slightly uneven because the psychiatrist led with prestructured options and added extras.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist supported choice by asking Derrick to select steps, respect his refusal to role-play, and avoid pressuring him into a larger commitment. Autonomy support could be stronger by reducing prescriptive suggestions and emphasizing Derrick as the expert.

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The psychiatrist successfully evoked practical harm-reduction steps, such as cab money, a two-beer limit, and a backup contact. However, evocation was limited in depth because it focused on action planning rather than Derrick’s own values, reasons, or confidence for change.

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** There was evidence of partnership because Derrick chose and contributed to the final plan. The psychiatrist’s directive early “quick plan” and multiple added suggestions reduced the sense of shared leadership.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The psychiatrist elicited useful client-generated change language around practical steps, such as keeping $30 for a cab and setting a clear limit. Change talk was not deeply cultivated through values, importance/confidence scaling, or broader reasons for avoiding another DUI.

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The psychiatrist avoided arguing with Derrick’s minimization and redirected the conversation toward practical safety steps. More sophisticated MI techniques, such as double-sided reflections or ambivalence-affirming responses, were not consistently used.
