# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

---

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The psychiatrist is generally respectful and nonjudgmental, but the acceptance remains surface-level and formulaic. It does not fully acknowledge the client’s mixed feelings, repeated apologies, or self-awareness without over-agreeing.

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The psychiatrist reflects frustration, unfairness, and exhaustion reasonably well. However, deeper emotions such as helplessness, dehumanization, isolation, and fear of being seen as the “angry guy” are not consistently captured.

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The session stays focused on the presenting concern without drifting into unrelated topics. However, the direction is more problem-solving than MI-informed, lacking a clearly co-created target behavior or change goal.

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** The psychiatrist behaves more like a problem-solver than an equal partner. The client’s values, concerns, and ideas do not meaningfully shape the session, which remains fixed on practical options.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Choices are offered, but repeated menus of tasks make the client feel pressured and burdened. Autonomy support is weakened by the expectation that the client must select one of the psychiatrist’s recommended options.

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Evocation is minimal; the psychiatrist mostly provides external reasons and specific strategies rather than eliciting the client’s own values, motivations, and confidence. The client’s strongest change talk is not explored or expanded.

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Input is invited, but the session remains clinician-led and advice-driven. The psychiatrist repeatedly returns to the same practical options even after the client rejects them.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** The client offers clear change talk, but the psychiatrist shifts quickly into problem-solving, scripts, and practical options. The client’s own motivations, values, and readiness for change are not evoked or amplified.

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** The client’s resistance to forms, scripts, complaints, and other suggestions is met with more practical alternatives rather than exploration of ambivalence. Double-sided reflections and gentle redirection toward change are limited.
