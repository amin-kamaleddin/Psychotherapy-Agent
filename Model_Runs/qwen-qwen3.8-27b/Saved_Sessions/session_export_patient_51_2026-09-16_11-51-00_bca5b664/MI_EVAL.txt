# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

---

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The psychiatrist is generally polite and nonjudgmental with appropriate acknowledgments of the client's time constraint. However, repeatedly returning to the same safety summary and offering a note/email after the client has stated she must leave makes the interaction feel subtly pressing rather than fully accepting.

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The psychiatrist offers surface-level reflections noting the client is balancing work and family, but these remain mostly factual. There is no deeper exploration of the client's emotional meaning, such as possible worry or urgency around the medication decision.

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The psychiatrist maintains a clear focus on the citalopram–phenelzine interaction with no major topic drift. However, the direction is mostly informational rather than motivational, looping through the same safety summary without consistently moving toward a specific change goal or concrete next step.

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** A few collaborative questions are asked, but the overall interaction is not equal-partner; the psychiatrist remains the main speaker and problem-solver. The client's repeated statements about needing to leave do not lead to a shorter, client-directed conversation or a co-created plan.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** There are some autonomy-supportive elements like acknowledging the client will raise this with her prescriber, but the psychiatrist repeatedly offers a note/email after the client has declined and stated she must leave. This inconsistency reduces the sense that her choice is fully honored.

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** The initial open question is evocative, but the session quickly shifts to information delivery with little subsequent eliciting of motivation. There is minimal use of scaling questions, values exploration, or reflections that would help the client articulate her own reasons for safe medication use.

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** The psychiatrist largely assumes the expert role by giving a complete medication safety answer and repeating it multiple times. The power balance is uneven and the session feels more like a scripted message being delivered than a collaborative exchange shaped by the client's contributions.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** A few brief affirmative labels are used, such as 'good call to raise this with your doctor,' but these are counselor-generated rather than evoked from the client. The psychiatrist does not systematically invite the client to articulate her own reasons, values, or confidence for safe medication use.

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** The client's main sustain talk is her time pressure, which is never effectively reflected or redirected. Instead, the psychiatrist continues to deliver repeated detailed safety summaries, prolonging the interaction despite the client's clear constraint, when a briefer reflection of the tension and a focused close would have been more effective.
