# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

---

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist maintains a respectful, nonjudgmental stance and validates Dylan’s frustration about being labeled while separating the incident from his identity. The main limitation is that some reflections over-endorse minimization rather than gently broadening focus to choices and consequences.

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist accurately captures multiple layers of Dylan’s experience, including fairness concerns, identity threat, worry about his record, and the importance of his social life. However, the reflections do not consistently deepen the stronger emotional undercurrent, such as embarrassment, fear, or defensiveness.

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The session remains connected to the referral concern, but the target behavior is not clearly specified or consistently pursued. The focus shifts between process fairness, record protection, and vague “staying out of trouble,” which can sound more like avoiding detection than changing risky drinking behavior.

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist uses Dylan’s own language and invites him to define fairness, plans, and priorities rather than imposing an agenda. However, the interaction remains partly clinician-led because the psychiatrist still shapes the sequence, framing, and reflective process.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist frequently highlights Dylan’s choice, agency, and self-defined version of “staying out of trouble” without directly pressuring him to change. Autonomy support is strong but somewhat tied to the clinician’s preferred direction, such as avoiding trouble and protecting the record.

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist uses open-ended questions to draw out Dylan’s own standards, plans, and barriers rather than giving reasons for change. The limitation is that evocation mainly supports maintaining the status quo or avoiding detection, rather than generating deeper motivation to change alcohol use or accept responsibility.

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist positions Dylan as the expert on his own experience and avoids a didactic, expert stance. The partnership is real but not fully equal because the clinician still sets much of the agenda and frames the conversation around his own therapeutic direction.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The psychiatrist evokes some client-driven language, including Dylan’s desire to keep his record clean, “use his head,” and choose when to leave. However, the counselor does not systematically develop DARN-C change talk, assess importance or confidence, or strengthen a concrete commitment to safer behavior.

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The psychiatrist avoids arguing and uses some reframing to redirect Dylan’s minimization and “just avoid getting caught” language. Nonetheless, repeated validation of the guest role and normalcy of the behavior allows sustain talk to remain dominant without stronger double-sided reflections or discrepancy exploration.
