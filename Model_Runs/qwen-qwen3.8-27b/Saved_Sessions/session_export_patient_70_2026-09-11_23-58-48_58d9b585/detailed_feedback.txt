                                Motivational Interviewing Feedback Criteria (5-Point Likert Scale)

Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)
------------------------------------
1. Acceptance: Extent to which the counselor communicates unconditional positive regard for the client.
   1 = Judgmental, disapproving, labeling
   2 = Inconsistent respect, occasional judgment
   3 = Neutral stance, basic respect
   4 = Generally respectful and supportive
   5 = Consistently respectful, warm, supportive

2. Empathy: Extent to which the counselor understands and/or makes an effort to accurately understand the client’s perspective.
   1 = Little effort to understand, minimal reflective listening
   2 = Limited perspective-taking, mostly factual focus
   3 = Adequate but inconsistent effort to understand
   4 = Good reflective listening and perspective-taking
   5 = Highly accurate, consistent understanding and reflections

3. Direction – Degree to which the counselor maintains appropriate focus on a specific target behavior or concerns directly tied to it.
1 = No clear focus; session wanders aimlessly
2 = Minimal focus; frequent topic drift
3 = Occasional focus on target behavior but inconsistent
4 = Generally keeps discussion directed toward target behavior
5 = Consistently maintains clear focus and guides toward change

---

4. Collaboration – Extent to which the clinician behaves as if the interview is occurring between two equal partners, both of whom have knowledge that might be useful in the problem under consideration.
1 = Authoritarian or expert-driven stance
2 = Limited collaboration; frequent direction by counselor
3 = Partial collaboration; uneven power-sharing
4 = Strong collaborative stance most of the time
5 = Fully collaborative; client’s ideas shape session direction

---

5. Autonomy Support – Extent to which clinician supports and actively fosters client perception of choice as opposed to attempting to control the client’s behavior or choices.
1 = Directive or pressuring; minimizes choice
2 = Occasionally acknowledges choice but inconsistently
3 = Neutral regarding client autonomy
4 = Regularly affirms client freedom to choose
5 = Consistently reinforces client ownership of change process

---

6. Evocation – Extent to which the clinician conveys an understanding that motivation for change, and the ability to move toward that change, reside mostly within the client and therefore focuses efforts to elicit and expand it within the therapeutic interaction.
1 = Provides reasons for change without exploring client view
2 = Limited interest in client motivations
3 = Some evocation but superficial
4 = Frequently draws out client’s own reasons for change
5 = Consistently elicits and develops client change talk throughout session

---

7. Partnership – Extent to which the clinician conveys an understanding that expertise and wisdom about change reside mostly within the client

1 = Clinician actively assumes the expert role for the majority of the interaction with the client. Collaboration or partnership is absent.
2 = Clinician superficially responds to opportunities to collaborate.
3 = Clinician incorporates client’s contributions but does so in a lukewarm or erratic fashion.
4 = Clinician fosters collaboration and power sharing so that client’s contributions impact the session in ways that they otherwise would not.
5 = Clinician actively fosters and encourages power sharing in the interaction in such a way that client’s contributions substantially influence the nature of the session.

---

8. Cultivating Change Talk – Extent to which the clinician actively encourages the client’s own language in favor of the change goal, and confidence for making that change.
1 = No effort to evoke change talk
2 = Rare or weak efforts
3 = Occasional evocation of change language
4 = Frequent evocation of change talk
5 = Consistent, skillful evocation throughout

---

9. Softening Sustain Talk – Extent that the clinician avoids a focus on the reasons against changing or for maintaining the status quo.
1 = Reinforces sustain talk
2 = Rarely shifts focus away from sustain talk
3 = Occasionally redirects toward change
4 = Often redirects effectively
5 = Consistently pivots client language toward change

- Ratings should reflect overall quality and consistency of behavior.

## Overall Global Ratings Score: **2.8/5**

### Summary Interpretation

The psychiatrist demonstrates a respectful, safe, and practical style. The session is helpful for a concrete re-entry task: completing employment-related forms and preparing for a job interview. The psychiatrist avoids judgment, normalizes embarrassment, and provides useful structure.

However, from a motivational interviewing perspective, the session is more job-application coaching than MI. The psychiatrist is often directive, provides exact scripts, and focuses heavily on wording rather than exploring Lars’s underlying ambivalence, values, risk factors, and change talk. The psychiatrist does not consistently evoke Lars’s own motivation, amplify change talk, or help him resolve the tension between independence, embarrassment, peer pressure, alcohol use, and his future with Donna.

The client has clear motivation and protective factors, but the counselor does not fully harness them.

## Priority Areas for Improvement

### 1. Use more evocative questions

Instead of only giving scripts, ask Lars to generate his own reasons and wording.

Examples:

- “What does this job mean to your future?”
- “What would staying out of trouble look like six months from now?”
- “What part of your old life do you most want to leave behind?”
- “How would a steady schedule change your weekends?”
- “What would you want to be true about yourself after getting this job?”

---

### 2. Amplify Lars’s change talk

When Lars says something motivational, reflect it back and deepen it.

Example from transcript:

> Lars: “I want that car job so I can stop leaning on those mates. If I’ve got a schedule, maybe I stop drinking as much.”

Stronger MI response:

> “It sounds like a steady car job could be a way to create distance from the old patterns — less time with mates when you’re bored, less alcohol, more structure. How strong is that part of you right now?”

Or:

> “You’re not just talking about a job. You’re talking about protecting your future, your relationship with Donna, and maybe becoming the kind of dad you want to be.”

---

### 3. Reflect ambivalence more explicitly

Lars is torn between several things:

- Independence vs. needing help.
- Honesty vs. fear of being judged.
- Desire for steady work vs. fear of appearing flaky.
- Wanting distance from mates vs. still being pulled toward them.
- Wanting to do it himself vs. recognizing Donna helps him stay safe.

A stronger MI response would name both sides without arguing:

- “On one hand, you want to be able to handle this yourself. On the other hand, you know getting help might actually make it easier.”
- “You want to be honest, but you also don’t want to open a door that could make you look unreliable.”
- “Part of you wants to prove you can stand on your own, and another part knows Donna’s presence keeps you safer.”

---

### 4. Use importance and confidence rulers

These would help evocation.

Examples:

- “On a scale of 0 to 10, how important is it for you to stay out of trouble?”
- “What makes it that number and not a lower one?”
- “How confident are you that a steady job could help with the boredom?”
- “If you take one small step this week, what would it be?”

---

### 5. Connect the job goal to the broader re-entry targets

The job is not just employment. It is connected to:

- Reducing boredom.
- Reducing alcohol use.
- Creating distance from risky peers.
- Strengthening the relationship with Donna.
- Building a stable identity.
- Reducing recidivism risk.
- Becoming a better father.

The psychiatrist could have made these connections more explicit:

- “How would having actual hours change the moments you usually get into trouble?”
- “How would this job support the life you want with Donna?”
- “What would this job say about the person you’re becoming?”

---

### 6. Support autonomy while giving practical help

The psychiatrist can still help with forms and interview scripts, but in a more autonomy-supportive way.

Instead of:

> “Copy this exact line.”

Try:

> “Here’s one way people phrase it. What would make it sound more like you and still honest?”

Instead of:

> “Use short, truthful lines.”

Try:

> “What do you think an employer needs to hear from you, without giving away things that feel risky?”

This keeps the counselor collaborative rather than directive.

## Final Comment

This session is clinically reasonable and practically useful, but it is not yet strong motivational interviewing. The psychiatrist is safe, respectful, and task-focused, but the MI process is underused. The most important next step for the trainee is to move from “I will help you fix the form” to “Let’s understand what this form, this job, and this next step mean for the life you want to build.”
