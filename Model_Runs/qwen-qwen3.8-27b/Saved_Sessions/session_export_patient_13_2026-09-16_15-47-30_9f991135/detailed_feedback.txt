                                Motivational Interviewing Feedback Criteria (5-Point Likert Scale)

Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)
------------------------------------
1. Acceptance: Extent to which the counselor communicates unconditional positive regard for the client.
   1 = Judgmental, disapproving, labeling
   2 = Inconsistent respect, occasional judgment
   3 = Neutral stance, basic respect
   4 = Generally respectful and supportive
   5 = Consistently respectful, warm, supportive

2. Empathy: Extent to which the counselor understands and/or makes an effort to accurately understand the client’s perspective.
   1 = Little effort to understand, minimal reflective listening
   2 = Limited perspective-taking, mostly factual focus
   3 = Adequate but inconsistent effort to understand
   4 = Good reflective listening and perspective-taking
   5 = Highly accurate, consistent understanding and reflections

3. Direction – Degree to which the counselor maintains appropriate focus on a specific target behavior or concerns directly tied to it.
1 = No clear focus; session wanders aimlessly
2 = Minimal focus; frequent topic drift
3 = Occasional focus on target behavior but inconsistent
4 = Generally keeps discussion directed toward target behavior
5 = Consistently maintains clear focus and guides toward change

---

4. Collaboration – Extent to which the clinician behaves as if the interview is occurring between two equal partners, both of whom have knowledge that might be useful in the problem under consideration.
1 = Authoritarian or expert-driven stance
2 = Limited collaboration; frequent direction by counselor
3 = Partial collaboration; uneven power-sharing
4 = Strong collaborative stance most of the time
5 = Fully collaborative; client’s ideas shape session direction

---

5. Autonomy Support – Extent to which clinician supports and actively fosters client perception of choice as opposed to attempting to control the client’s behavior or choices.
1 = Directive or pressuring; minimizes choice
2 = Occasionally acknowledges choice but inconsistently
3 = Neutral regarding client autonomy
4 = Regularly affirms client freedom to choose
5 = Consistently reinforces client ownership of change process

---

6. Evocation – Extent to which the clinician conveys an understanding that motivation for change, and the ability to move toward that change, reside mostly within the client and therefore focuses efforts to elicit and expand it within the therapeutic interaction.
1 = Provides reasons for change without exploring client view
2 = Limited interest in client motivations
3 = Some evocation but superficial
4 = Frequently draws out client’s own reasons for change
5 = Consistently elicits and develops client change talk throughout session

---

7. Partnership – Extent to which the clinician conveys an understanding that expertise and wisdom about change reside mostly within the client

1 = Clinician actively assumes the expert role for the majority of the interaction with the client. Collaboration or partnership is absent.
2 = Clinician superficially responds to opportunities to collaborate.
3 = Clinician incorporates client’s contributions but does so in a lukewarm or erratic fashion.
4 = Clinician fosters collaboration and power sharing so that client’s contributions impact the session in ways that they otherwise would not.
5 = Clinician actively fosters and encourages power sharing in the interaction in such a way that client’s contributions substantially influence the nature of the session.

---

8. Cultivating Change Talk – Extent to which the clinician actively encourages the client’s own language in favor of the change goal, and confidence for making that change.
1 = No effort to evoke change talk
2 = Rare or weak efforts
3 = Occasional evocation of change language
4 = Frequent evocation of change talk
5 = Consistent, skillful evocation throughout

---

9. Softening Sustain Talk – Extent that the clinician avoids a focus on the reasons against changing or for maintaining the status quo.
1 = Reinforces sustain talk
2 = Rarely shifts focus away from sustain talk
3 = Occasionally redirects toward change
4 = Often redirects effectively
5 = Consistently pivots client language toward change

Evaluation Guidance
-------------------
- All domains are rated on a 1–5 Likert scale.
- Ratings should reflect overall quality and consistency of behavior.
- Focus on both counselor skillfulness and client speech as indicators of MI process quality.

 # Motivational Interviewing Feedback: Psychiatrist Evaluation

## Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)

### 1. Acceptance
Score: 4/5

Explanation:
The psychiatrist maintains a respectful, nonjudgmental tone throughout. Statements such as “I hear both fear about the weight gain or discomfort, and the part of you that isn’t ready to see a doctor yet” and repeated “It makes sense…” responses show acceptance of Anara’s ambivalence rather than criticism for avoiding care. The use of her name also personalizes the interaction. However, the warmth sometimes feels formulaic because of repeated templates (“It sounds like…” / “It makes sense…”), and there is less explicit, individualized positive regard such as affirming her courage, effort, or worth as a person. A 5 would include more distinctive, emotionally resonant acceptance, especially around shame about body size and fear of medical evaluation.

---

### 2. Empathy
Score: 4/5

Explanation:
The psychiatrist demonstrates good reflective listening and perspective-taking. Examples include accurately capturing the ambivalence: “part of you is ready for a small step toward care, while another part wants to keep it from becoming too real or too heavy,” and later, “part of you wants to face the door, and part needs to protect yourself if your stomach turns.” These are strong complex reflections that honor the client’s conflicting feelings. The reflection about the exit plan also tracks her relief: “you’re not sure you could really do it, yet the idea eases the weight for those first ten minutes.” The main limitation is that the reflections can be somewhat repetitive and content-focused; they do not consistently deepen the underlying emotion of shame, fear of being seen, or loss of control. A 5 would show more precise emotional nuance, for example reflecting the shame of being “looked at,” the fear of becoming “real,” or the need to protect herself from overwhelm without being labeled as avoiding.

---

### 3. Cultivating Change Talk
Score: 4/5

Explanation:
The psychiatrist frequently elicits change talk by asking about small steps and safety. Anara produces useful change talk: “If it’s just… getting through the door, maybe I can do that,” “If I can do that, I can do the rest,” “It takes the weight off my shoulders,” and “I think morning helps.” The questions are appropriately open and invite her to design her own plan. However, the psychiatrist does not consistently reinforce, amplify, or consolidate that change talk. There are missed opportunities to reflect the meaning of her statements, ask confidence questions, or explore values, such as: “What does your ‘I can do the rest’ tell you about yourself?” or “What would getting through the door say about how you’re caring for your child and yourself?” A 5 would include more skillful follow-up after change talk, including confidence scaling, importance scaling, and summarizing emerging reasons for change.

---

### 4. Softening Sustain Talk
Score: 4/5

Explanation:
The psychiatrist handles sustain talk well by accepting it without arguing. When Anara says she “don’t want it to be real yet,” worries about being a burden, or says she “don’t know if I can really do it,” the psychiatrist does not counter with persuasion or urgency. A strong example is: “Sleeping on it could be part of the plan, not a detour.” This softens sustain talk while preserving the direction toward change. The psychiatrist also redirects from barriers into smaller, manageable actions: companion, exit plan, quiet signal, text, morning timing. The limitation is that the session remains heavily focused on the reasons for not acting, and the softening is mostly through acceptance rather than active double-sided reflection that links the sustain talk to values and possible movement, for example: “You can protect yourself from being overwhelmed and still make the first step feel smaller.” A 5 would show more explicit, strategic pivots from sustain talk toward change while continuing to honor the concern.

### 5. Partnership
Score: 4/5

Explanation:
The psychiatrist generally conveys that Anara is the expert on what would work. Questions such as “What would a low-pressure way of getting that companion feel like?” and “What would you want ‘leaving’ to look like in that moment?” invite her to shape the plan. However, the psychiatrist also supplies several examples and maintains a clear agenda: phone call, information, companion, text, note, practice at home, “first small step,” and “first visit.” This is acceptable in MI, but it can feel slightly leading compared with a fully client-directed partnership. A 5 would show more explicit power-sharing, such as asking the client to set the agenda, define what counts as success, or choose the next focus without the counselor offering menu-like options first.

### 6. Direction
Score: 5/5

Explanation:
The psychiatrist maintains a clear and consistent focus on the target behavior: taking a small step toward seeing a doctor or attending an appointment. The session does not drift into unrelated topics. ### 7. Autonomy Support
Score: 4/5

Explanation:
The psychiatrist supports autonomy by avoiding pressure, advice, or urgency. The client is repeatedly positioned as the one deciding: “the part of you that isn’t ready,” “you’re not there yet,” and “Sleeping on it could be part of the plan.” There is no telling her she must go to the doctor or invalidating her fear. ### 8. Collaboration

### 9. Evocation
Score: 4/5

Explanation:
The psychiatrist mostly evokes motivation from within the client rather than providing reasons for change. A 5 would include more explicit evocation of change talk, confidence, and personal meaning throughout the session.

**Overall Global Ratings Score:** **3.8/5**

Overall, the psychiatrist demonstrates a strong MI style: respectful, nonjudgmental, focused, and collaborative. The session is especially effective in maintaining direction toward a specific target behavior and in co-creating a very small, manageable step with Anara. The main areas for improvement are increasing the depth and individualization of empathy and acceptance, more explicitly affirming autonomy, and more skillfully amplifying and consolidating change talk. With slightly less reliance on repeated reflective templates and more targeted follow-up to the client’s emerging confidence and values, the performance could move from strong to excellent.
