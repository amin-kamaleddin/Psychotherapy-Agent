# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

---

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 5
**Comments:** The psychiatrist consistently demonstrates warmth, respect, and nonjudgmental regard while validating the client’s values and concerns. Affirmations such as recognizing the client’s self-awareness and protective intentions reinforce a supportive, dignified stance.

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** There is solid reflective listening and accurate recognition of the client’s tension between caring for students and protecting her own energy. However, some reflections remain plan-focused and miss opportunities to deepen emotional resonance around guilt, exhaustion, and the desire to feel lighter.

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 5
**Comments:** The session is clearly focused on a specific, behaviorally concrete target: using a brief pause or boundary statement to protect self-respect and energy. The conversation moves strategically from values clarification to a small experiment, confidence check, supports, and follow-up with minimal drift.

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The plan is largely co-constructed, with the client’s ideas shaping the pause strategy, reminder, follow-up time, and check-in format. However, the psychiatrist occasionally shifts into a more advising role by offering specific scripts and wording alternatives before the client fully generates her own options.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 5
**Comments:** The psychiatrist consistently reinforces the client’s ownership of decisions through optional, client-centered language. When the client prefers her own Maria text, the psychiatrist accepts the choice without pushing, arguing, or implying that the therapist’s version is superior.

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist effectively elicits client-generated motivation, confidence, and self-efficacy through strong questions about small signs, ease of practice, and confidence level. The evocative stance is sometimes weakened by offering specific scripts and wording before the client has fully explored her own language.

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The interaction is generally collaborative, with repeated invitations for the client’s input and respect for her right to reject suggestions. The stance occasionally tilts toward adviser-like problem-solving when the psychiatrist introduces specific reminder wording, boundary statements, and text options.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist skillfully evokes change-relevant language around confidence, intention, and ability, especially through the confidence ruler and questions about supports. Some opportunities to amplify change talk are reduced when the psychiatrist offers concrete scripts instead of continuing to draw out the client’s own motivational language.

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 5
**Comments:** The client’s sustain talk—worry about seeming flaky, anticipated guilt, and concern that the Maria text feels too much—is handled with validation and reframing rather than argument or dismissal. The psychiatrist normalizes these concerns while helping the client connect them to self-respect, such as “Resting isn’t failing my students.”
