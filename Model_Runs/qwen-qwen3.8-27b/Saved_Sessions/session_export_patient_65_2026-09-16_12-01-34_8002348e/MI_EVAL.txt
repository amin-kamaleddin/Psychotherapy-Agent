# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

---

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The psychiatrist remained neutral, respectful, and nonjudgmental while acknowledging the client’s guardedness and practical concerns. However, the tone stayed more procedural than deeply affirming, with limited explicit validation of the client’s stress or broader emotional experience.

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The psychiatrist accurately reflected the client’s logistical worries, fear of being “stuck,” and discomfort with making calls. These reflections were helpful but remained mostly cognitive and surface-level, missing deeper emotional undertones such as shame, anger, or feelings of control.

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** The session stayed consistently focused on completing the court-ordered community service by the deadline, covering site selection, documentation, and follow-up steps. The direction was clear and efficient, but it was overly narrow in its emphasis on procedural compliance rather than broader motivational change.

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist worked collaboratively with the client to co-create emails, scripts, and a practical plan, inviting input on details like availability, location, and format. The partnership was strong, though the psychiatrist still performed much of the drafting and organizational labor, which slightly limited full co-construction.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist repeatedly offered choices and asked for preferences before moving forward, supporting the client’s sense of control over the process. This was generally strong, though it could have been strengthened by more explicitly affirming the client’s ownership of decisions and next steps.

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** The psychiatrist effectively evoked the client’s practical preferences and constraints, such as weekend availability and proximity. However, there was little effort to evoke the client’s personal reasons, values, or internal motivation for change, leaving the “why” largely unexplored.

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist treated the client as an active participant by inviting input, offering options, and allowing the client’s preferences to shape the plan. The overall dynamic was collaborative rather than lecture-driven, with the client meaningfully contributing to the final approach.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** There was very limited effort to elicit the client’s own change talk or internal reasons for change. The session centered on task completion and compliance logistics, with little use of motivational questions or affirmations that would help the client articulate personal motivation.

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The psychiatrist acknowledged the client’s doubts and resistance without arguing or reinforcing them, using simple validation and pivoting toward solutions. This was acceptable but missed more advanced MI strategies, such as double-sided reflections, to help the client explore ambivalence more deeply.
