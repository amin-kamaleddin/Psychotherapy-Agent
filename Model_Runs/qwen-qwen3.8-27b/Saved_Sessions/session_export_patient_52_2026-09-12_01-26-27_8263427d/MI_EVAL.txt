# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

---

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Maintains a respectful, nonjudgmental stance and normalizes the client's harm-reduction goal without moralizing. Warmth is sometimes procedural rather than deeply affirming when the client shares guilt, exhaustion, and parenting stress.

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Reflects key stress and parental concerns early, but often stays at the content or problem-solving level. Important emotional cues around alcohol as relief, guilt, fear of failure, and exhaustion are not consistently reflected or explored.

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Keeps a clear focus on alcohol use, stress, and harm-reduction goals throughout the session. Focus narrows into concrete scripts and practical problem-solving rather than motivating the client's own values and reasons for change.

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** The psychiatrist supplies most of the agenda, options, timers, scripts, and plan, so the interaction does not fully feel equal. Client input is incorporated, but the counselor often continues advising rather than building from the client's own ideas.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Acknowledges the client's freedom to choose and avoids shaming or forced quit framing. Frequent directive instructions, scripts, and structured steps weaken the sense that the client is fully owning the plan.

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Questions mainly address doability and format rather than drawing out the client's intrinsic reasons, values, concerns, or confidence. Missed opportunities to explore her guilt, parental presence, ambivalence about alcohol, and what change would help her do.

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Interaction is largely counselor-driven, with the psychiatrist supplying the agenda, coping options, and detailed plan. The client is invited to choose, but there is limited evidence of genuine co-creation or repeated client-led decision-making.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Client produces several change statements, and the psychiatrist reinforces them as progress and realistic harm reduction. Could be stronger with more evocative follow-ups that deepen the client's desire, reasons, need, and confidence.

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Avoids arguing or moralizing and keeps the conversation open to the client's evening ritual and limited drinking. However, sustain talk is often acknowledged rather than strategically reframed or used to resolve ambivalence.
