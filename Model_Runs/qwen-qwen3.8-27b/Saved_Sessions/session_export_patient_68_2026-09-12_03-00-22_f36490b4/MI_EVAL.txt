# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

---

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Maintains a respectful, nonjudgmental, and validating tone while normalizing the client’s ambivalence and embarrassment. The warmth is strong but somewhat procedural, with fewer deeply individualized affirmations of the client as a person.

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Accurately reflects the client’s ambivalence, practical concerns, and social fear of being awkward. However, reflections remain relatively brief and quickly move into problem-solving before deeper emotional meaning is fully explored.

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Maintains a clear focus on reducing injury risk and creating a safer plan for social evenings. The focus is appropriate but becomes somewhat mechanical and lacks deeper linkage to values, plus a concise summary or closure.

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The session feels collaborative, with the client’s ideas shaping the final plan and implementation details. A limitation is that the psychiatrist often presents structured options first, making the interaction somewhat counselor-led rather than fully co-created.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Frequently uses choice language, avoids pressure to stop drinking, and frames the plan as the client’s own. Autonomy support is slightly weakened by repeated provision of detailed options and scripts, which can steer the client toward counselor-generated solutions.

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Uses open-ended questions, confidence scaling, and implementation prompts to draw out concrete change talk and increased confidence. The evocation would be stronger with more values-based questions that connect the behavior to deeper personal meaning.

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The psychiatrist creates a cooperative session, but frequently supplies lists of options, exit strategies, and text-message drafts. This reduces the extent to which the client is positioned as the primary expert on his own life, friendships, and preferences.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Successfully elicits substantial, concrete change talk and helps the client commit to a realistic low-risk plan. A minor limitation is that the psychiatrist sometimes offers a menu of options before allowing more space for client-generated ideas.

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Handles sustain talk well by avoiding confrontation and reframing the client’s attachment to fun, socializing, and identity in a change-compatible way. The response is constructive, though it could use more explicit double-sided reflections to deepen validation before redirecting.
