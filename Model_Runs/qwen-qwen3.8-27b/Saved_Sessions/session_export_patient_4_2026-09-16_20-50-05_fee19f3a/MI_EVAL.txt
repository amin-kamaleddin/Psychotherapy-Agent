# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

---

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist consistently communicates respect, validation, and nonjudgment toward Aizada's pain and concerns. Affirmations are supportive but remain somewhat general rather than deeply individualized.

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Reflective listening captures ambivalence between the need for medical testing and emerging awareness of stress-related factors. The responses sometimes summarize or plan instead of fully deepening into the emotional core of her fear, loneliness, and frustration.

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The session stays focused on gastrointestinal symptoms, diagnostic concerns, and practical next steps. It becomes somewhat logistical and does not consistently guide the conversation toward broader change or symptom self-management.

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist co-constructs the plan with Aizada by incorporating her ideas about testing, a diary, support, and follow-up contact. Collaboration is strong, though the psychiatrist still takes a more organizing role in shaping the conversation.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 5
**Comments:** Aizada's sense of choice and control is consistently supported through direct invitations for her input and respect for her decisions. The psychiatrist avoids pressuring her to accept a psychological explanation or a particular course of action.

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Open-ended questions elicit useful client-generated ideas and some change talk. The evocation is more focused on problem-solving and logistics than on drawing out deeper values, fears, or intrinsic motivation for change.

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist positions Aizada as the expert on her own body and experience while inviting her lead in planning. Partnership is strong, though the psychiatrist occasionally positions herself as the facilitator of next steps rather than fully equal in decision-making.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Aizada expresses meaningful change talk, such as watching for patterns and managing the pain, but the psychiatrist does not consistently reflect, amplify, or develop it. Change talk is often used to build a medical plan rather than to strengthen motivation or confidence.

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** The psychiatrist respectfully avoids challenging Aizada's sustain talk about needing more tests and a physical explanation. However, she does not often use MI-style reflections or redirection to move the conversation toward emerging insight or change.
