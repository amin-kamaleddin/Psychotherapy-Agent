                                Motivational Interviewing Feedback Criteria (5-Point Likert Scale)

Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)
------------------------------------
1. Acceptance: Extent to which the counselor communicates unconditional positive regard for the client.
   1 = Judgmental, disapproving, labeling
   2 = Inconsistent respect, occasional judgment
   3 = Neutral stance, basic respect
   4 = Generally respectful and supportive
   5 = Consistently respectful, warm, supportive

2. Empathy: Extent to which the counselor understands and/or makes an effort to accurately understand the client’s perspective.
   1 = Little effort to understand, minimal reflective listening
   2 = Limited perspective-taking, mostly factual focus
   3 = Adequate but inconsistent effort to understand
   4 = Good reflective listening and perspective-taking
   5 = Highly accurate, consistent understanding and reflections

3. Direction – Degree to which the counselor maintains appropriate focus on a specific target behavior or concerns directly tied to it.
1 = No clear focus; session wanders aimlessly
2 = Minimal focus; frequent topic drift
3 = Occasional focus on target behavior but inconsistent
4 = Generally keeps discussion directed toward target behavior
5 = Consistently maintains clear focus and guides toward change

---

4. Collaboration – Extent to which the clinician behaves as if the interview is occurring between two equal partners, both of whom have knowledge that might be useful in the problem under consideration.
1 = Authoritarian or expert-driven stance
2 = Limited collaboration; frequent direction by counselor
3 = Partial collaboration; uneven power-sharing
4 = Strong collaborative stance most of the time
5 = Fully collaborative; client’s ideas shape session direction

---

5. Autonomy Support – Extent to which clinician supports and actively fosters client perception of choice as opposed to attempting to control the client’s behavior or choices.
1 = Directive or pressuring; minimizes choice
2 = Occasionally acknowledges choice but inconsistently
3 = Neutral regarding client autonomy
4 = Regularly affirms client freedom to choose
5 = Consistently reinforces client ownership of change process

---

6. Evocation – Extent to which the clinician conveys an understanding that motivation for change, and the ability to move toward that change, reside mostly within the client and therefore focuses efforts to elicit and expand it within the therapeutic interaction.
1 = Provides reasons for change without exploring client view
2 = Limited interest in client motivations
3 = Some evocation but superficial
4 = Frequently draws out client’s own reasons for change
5 = Consistently elicits and develops client change talk throughout session

---

7. Partnership – Extent to which the clinician conveys an understanding that expertise and wisdom about change reside mostly within the client

1 = Clinician actively assumes the expert role for the majority of the interaction with the client. Collaboration or partnership is absent.
2 = Clinician superficially responds to opportunities to collaborate.
3 = Clinician incorporates client’s contributions but does so in a lukewarm or erratic fashion.
4 = Clinician fosters collaboration and power sharing so that client’s contributions impact the session in ways that they otherwise would not.
5 = Clinician actively fosters and encourages power sharing in the interaction in such a way that client’s contributions substantially influence the nature of the session.

---

8. Cultivating Change Talk – Extent to which the clinician actively encourages the client’s own language in favor of the change goal, and confidence for making that change.
1 = No effort to evoke change talk
2 = Rare or weak efforts
3 = Occasional evocation of change language
4 = Frequent evocation of change talk
5 = Consistent, skillful evocation throughout

---

9. Softening Sustain Talk – Extent that the clinician avoids a focus on the reasons against changing or for maintaining the status quo.
1 = Reinforces sustain talk
2 = Rarely shifts focus away from sustain talk
3 = Occasionally redirects toward change
4 = Often redirects effectively
5 = Consistently pivots client language toward change

Evaluation Guidance
-------------------
- All domains are rated on a 1–5 Likert scale.
- Ratings should reflect overall quality and consistency of behavior.
- Focus on both counselor skillfulness and client speech as indicators of MI process quality.

 # Motivational Interviewing Feedback: Psychiatrist Evaluation

## Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)

### 1. Acceptance
Score: 4/5

Explanation:
The psychiatrist consistently maintains a nonjudgmental, respectful, and supportive tone. There is no disapproval, argument, or labeling of Marisol’s choice. Statements such as “I’m glad you reached out and I’m here to listen,” “You’re in charge of what we focus on,” “I respect that,” and “I respect your choice and independence” convey positive regard and acceptance of Marisol as the decision-maker. However, the acceptance is somewhat formulaic and overly agreeing in places. Phrases such as “I’m glad you’re happy” and “I’m glad it feels right for you” are respectful but risk sounding more like social reassurance than deep, individualized positive regard. A stronger session would have affirmed Marisol’s autonomy while also acknowledging the complexity of being referred for something she does not see as a problem, without overvalidating the status quo.

---

### 2. Empathy
Score: 4/5

Explanation:
The psychiatrist demonstrates good reflective listening and accurately captures several core parts of Marisol’s perspective: that the piercing was a deliberate choice, that she likes how it looks, that she is not feeling stuck, that she is attending because of a referral, and that her decision is tied to independence. Examples include “you chose the piercing because you wanted something different and you like how it looks” and “you chose it to show independence.” These are accurate and therapeutically appropriate. The main limitation is that the reflections remain relatively surface-level and repetitive. The psychiatrist does not deeply explore the emotional or identity meaning underneath Marisol’s independence talk, such as how it feels to be told to reconsider something that symbolizes self-determination, or whether the dentist’s reaction felt controlling, judgmental, or intrusive. With stronger empathy, the psychiatrist might have reflected something like, “It sounds like this is not just about the piercing—it’s about being able to make your own decisions without being managed.”

---

### 3. Cultivating Change Talk
Score: 2/5

Explanation:
There are only weak or rare attempts to cultivate change talk. The psychiatrist asks a few open-ended questions that could have opened the door to change, such as “What would be most helpful: talk through the dentist’s reaction, get info about piercing and oral health, or just have someone listen?” and “is there anything around it you’d like support with now or later (handling reactions, care, or just enjoying it)?” However, these questions do not actively elicit Marisol’s own reasons for change, confidence, values, or possible willingness to modify behavior. When Marisol says, “I keep it clean, so I don’t get the worry,” this is a small opening to evoke self-efficacy and care-related change talk, but the psychiatrist responds by reinforcing confidence rather than exploring Marisol’s own standards for care or what “doing what’s right” might look like. The psychiatrist does not use importance/confidence questions, does not ask what would be good about protecting oral health, and does not build on the small change-relevant language Marisol offers. As a result, Marisol is never encouraged to voice motivation for change.

---

### 4. Softening Sustain Talk
Score: 2/5

Explanation:
The psychiatrist avoids directly arguing with Marisol and does not challenge her refusal to change, which is consistent with good MI spirit. However, the session does not effectively soften or redirect sustain talk; in several places it inadvertently strengthens it. Marisol’s sustain talk is strong: “I’m not worried,” “I’m not changing anything,” “It’s my choice,” and “I’ve got it under control.” The psychiatrist repeatedly reflects this back in a positive or validating way: “You sound confident,” “you’re taking steps to care for it—so the dentist’s concern doesn’t shake you,” “that sounds confident,” “I’m glad you’re happy,” and “you’re confident and in control of your choice.” This can reinforce the client’s belief that there is no reason to reconsider. A stronger MI response would have used double-sided reflections or gentle pivots, such as, “You really value making your own choices, and at the same time your dentist raised a concern about oral health. What part of that, if any, feels worth noticing?” The psychiatrist mostly allowed sustain talk to remain unchallenged and untransformed.

---

### 5. Partnership
Score: 4/5

Explanation:
The psychiatrist conveys a collaborative partnership stance and repeatedly communicates that Marisol is the expert on her own life and choices. Language such as “You’re in charge of what we focus on,” “You decide,” and “What would you prefer now?” shows that the psychiatrist is not imposing an expert agenda. Marisol’s contributions do influence the session, especially when she indicates she does not want to discuss health, aftercare, or advice, and the psychiatrist follows her lead. However, the partnership is somewhat passive rather than actively co-creative. The psychiatrist largely reflects and defers, but does not explicitly invite Marisol to help shape a shared understanding of the referral concern, her values, or a small next step she might find meaningful. A stronger session would have said something like, “You know what matters to you. If you were willing, could we explore what this referral feels like to you?”

---

### 6. Direction
Score: 3/5

Explanation:
The session stays broadly connected to the referral concern—Marisol’s lip piercing and the dentist’s reaction—but it lacks clear strategic direction toward the target behavior or related oral health concern. The opening question is very broad: “What’s been on your mind lately, or where in your life do you feel stuck or want things to be different?” After Marisol states that she is not stuck and does not want to change anything, the psychiatrist offers general options but does not maintain a gentle, MI-consistent focus on the specific concern that brought her in. There is no clear movement toward clarifying the target issue, exploring the meaning of the dental concern, or identifying any client-centered step that would respect her autonomy while keeping the focus on the referral reason. The session also becomes somewhat directionless at the end, with repeated closing statements after Marisol has already indicated she is finished. A stronger session would have summarized the core issue and, while respecting Marisol’s resistance, kept a coherent focus on how the piercing relates to her values, health, or daily functioning.

### 7. Autonomy Support
Score: 5/5

Explanation:
This is one of the strongest domains in the session. The psychiatrist consistently supports Marisol’s perception of choice and avoids being directive, pressuring, or argumentative. Statements such as “You’re in charge of what we focus on,” “You decide,” “your choice,” “I respect that,” and “you don’t want advice right now... I’m here” clearly communicate that Marisol is responsible for her own decisions. The psychiatrist does not lecture, persuade, or impose an agenda, even when Marisol explicitly says she does not want advice. This is excellent MI-congruent autonomy support. The only minor caution is that the degree of agreement may become so strong that it stops short of creating any therapeutic tension or exploration, but the autonomy-supportive stance itself is consistently strong.

---

### 8. Collaboration
Score: 4/5

Explanation:
The psychiatrist behaves in a collaborative way by inviting Marisol’s input, offering choices, and following her lead. Questions such as “What would be most helpful?” and “What would you prefer now?” frame the session as a shared process rather than a unilateral intervention. Marisol’s statements do shape the direction of the session, particularly when she declines discussion of health, aftercare, or advice and asks to wrap up. However, the collaboration remains somewhat shallow because the psychiatrist does not actively co-create meaning or jointly explore the problem under consideration. There is limited use of “we” language or joint problem-solving, and once Marisol disengages, the interaction becomes more reflective and closing-focused than collaborative. A stronger session would have sought Marisol’s expertise in areas such as her values, what independence means to her, or what would make any future discussion feel acceptable to her.

---

### 9. Evocation
Score: 2/5

Explanation:
The psychiatrist asks some open-ended questions, but they do not effectively evoke Marisol’s own motivation for change. Questions such as “What’s been on your mind lately?” and “What would be most helpful?” are appropriate starters, but after Marisol states that she is not stuck and does not want to change anything, the psychiatrist does not use MI techniques to draw out client-centered reasons for change. There is no use of importance rulers, confidence questions, exploring values, eliciting exceptions, or asking what would be good about protecting her oral health. Instead, the psychiatrist mostly reflects Marisol’s current perspective back to her, which reinforces sustain talk rather than evoking change talk. For example, when Marisol says the piercing “shows I do what I want,” the psychiatrist could have evoked change by asking, “What else is important to you that your choices protect?” Rather than doing so, the psychiatrist largely affirms that she is confident and in control. As a result, there is minimal active evocation of motivation from within the client.

---

**Overall Global Ratings Score:** **3.3/5**

The psychiatrist demonstrates a strong MI spirit, especially in acceptance, empathy, autonomy support, and collaboration. The session is respectful, nonjudgmental, and consistent with Marisol’s expressed lack of readiness to change. However, it is limited in clinical effectiveness because it is too permissive and affirming of sustain talk, does not strategically soften the client’s reasons to maintain the status quo, and fails to evoke or cultivate change talk. The session also lacks sufficient direction toward the specific referral concern and allows the interaction to close too readily after the client disengages. With stronger MI skills, the psychiatrist could have maintained acceptance and autonomy support while using reflective listening, double-sided reflections, and evocative questions to help Marisol explore the meaning of her independence, the dentist’s concern, and any part of the issue that might matter to her.
