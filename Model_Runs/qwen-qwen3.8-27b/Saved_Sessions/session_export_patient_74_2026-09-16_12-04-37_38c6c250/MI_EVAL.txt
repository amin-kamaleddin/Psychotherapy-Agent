# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

---

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist shows respect, warmth, and nonjudgmental acceptance, even when the patient expresses sustain talk about enjoying smoking. It is not perfect because some comments are mildly interpretive or agenda-setting and can shift the tone toward directive problem-solving.

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist uses frequent, accurate reflections of ambivalence between the calming ritual of smoking and concern about modeling behavior for her children. Deeper emotional meaning behind statements, such as fear, guilt, or values, was not consistently explored.

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The session maintains a clear focus on smoking and moves toward a concrete, low-pressure after-dinner patio test tied to the presenting concern. It becomes slightly narrow on logistics before fully consolidating motivation, importance, and discrepancy.

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The psychiatrist uses collaborative language, offers options, and adjusts the plan based on the patient's preferences. However, the clinician often generates the main ideas and structures the experiment, limiting equal collaboration.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist strongly supports autonomy through choice language, no-judgment framing, flexibility, and permission to withdraw. It is not perfect because repeated proposals after hesitation can still feel mildly directive.

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The psychiatrist makes reasonable efforts to evoke motivation by asking about reasons for coming, preferences, confidence, and self-monitoring. The effect is limited because the clinician frequently interrupts with external suggestions rather than drawing out the patient's own values and reasons.

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The psychiatrist invites input and adapts to the patient's preferences, creating some collaborative momentum. The session remains unevenly shaped by the clinician's expert problem-solving agenda rather than the patient leading the process.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The psychiatrist elicits some change talk around concern for her children, willingness to try a small experiment, and attention to cravings. It does not consistently amplify or deepen the patient's own change language.

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The psychiatrist accepts sustain talk without arguing and uses some two-sided reflections. It does not consistently turn sustain talk into discrepancy or explore its emotional meaning before moving to new options.
