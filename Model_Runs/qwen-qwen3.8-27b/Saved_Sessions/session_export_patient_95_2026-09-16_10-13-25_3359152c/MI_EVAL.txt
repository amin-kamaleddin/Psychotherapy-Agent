# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The psychiatrist is respectful and shows concern, but directive and urgent safety language can undermine unconditional positive regard.

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Strong, accurate reflections capture Maria’s exhaustion and ambivalence, though repeated factual safety questions occasionally interrupt deeper emotional engagement.

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The session stays focused on immediate safety and the presenting risk, though the direction is more containment-oriented than MI-process-focused.

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** There is collaborative language and some responsiveness to the client’s preferences, but the psychiatrist often drives the conversation through safety questions and risk management.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Choices are offered and some boundaries are respected, yet repeated pressure around 988, contacting others, and waiting can reduce the client’s sense of ownership.

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The psychiatrist uses some evocative questions to elicit reasons to live, but many closed or factual questions and suggestions limit deeper client-led motivation.

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The interaction invites input and offers options, but the safety agenda and directive statements make the relationship feel less equal in high-risk moments.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Some change talk is elicited and acknowledged, such as staying for 20 minutes and staying for her mother, but it is not consistently expanded or deepened.

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** The psychiatrist appropriately avoids arguing with sustain talk, but there is limited active softening or strategic redirection toward ambivalence or change.
