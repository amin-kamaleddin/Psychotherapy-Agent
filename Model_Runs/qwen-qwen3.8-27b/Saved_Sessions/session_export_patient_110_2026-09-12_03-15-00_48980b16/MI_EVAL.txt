# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

---

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist is respectful, nonjudgmental, and supportive, validating the client’s prevention concerns and retirement goals. It is somewhat procedural and lacks deeper affirmation of the client’s ambivalence.

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Reflections accurately capture the client’s practical and identity-based concerns about medication. Emotional fear of losing enjoyment or independence is not fully explored.

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The session stays focused on deciding about cholesterol medication and creating a reversible trial plan. It is clear and serviceable but somewhat linear and task-driven.

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The psychiatrist asks for client input and co-creates a written request, but often defines the medical plan and solution. More client-led planning would strengthen collaboration.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Strong support for choice, reversibility, and an “off switch” helps the client feel in control. The provider still introduces a fairly specific medical plan, limiting full client-led decision-making.

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Open questions early on help the client articulate values, fears, and the idea of a test run. Later, the session shifts into information-giving and limits deeper evocation.

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The client contributes key ideas such as a test run and off switch, but the psychiatrist often leads content and structure. More explicit partner stance is needed.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The client expresses meaningful change talk, but the psychiatrist does not consistently reflect, amplify, or use scaling/values questions to deepen it.

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Sustain talk is acknowledged rather than challenged, and side-effect concerns are treated as legitimate. The psychiatrist does not consistently pivot those concerns toward valued goals or balance them with change.
