# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

---

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist remains nonjudgmental, respectful, and normalizing throughout. Acceptance is somewhat formulaic and efficiency-focused rather than deeply individualized.

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Reflections accurately capture frustration, distrust, safety concerns, and the desire for practical help. Empathy could be stronger in exploring deeper emotions, fear, and the unresolved “bones” question.

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The session stays focused on asthma management and narrows to a small, doable action. Some direction becomes problem-solving before motivation is fully established.

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist asks for input and adapts to the client’s constraints by co-creating a low-burden text message. The clinician still supplies most of the options and medical framing.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** There is strong emphasis on choice, no pressure to act immediately, and respect for the client’s decision. Some safety instructions and repeated options feel mildly directive.

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Some open questions are used, but the session shifts quickly to options and problem-solving. There are fewer evocative questions about the client’s own reasons, values, and confidence.

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The psychiatrist invites client input but frequently leads with clinical options and a structured plan. More explicit recognition of the client as the expert on her own life would strengthen partnership.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The clinician notices and reinforces small steps, such as carrying a rescue inhaler or sending a text later. Change talk is not consistently deepened or amplified.

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The psychiatrist avoids arguing and normalizes the client’s concerns. However, the conversation stays centered on risk and safety rather than strategically balancing concerns with values and willingness.
