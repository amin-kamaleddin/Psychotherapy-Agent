# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

---

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist maintains a respectful, nonjudgmental stance and avoids shaming the client for shoplifting, cravings, or fear. Acceptance is slightly procedural and sometimes tied to the client’s performance of plans rather than unconditional regard.

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Reflections of fed-upness, fear of getting caught, and worry about losing the group are accurate and supportive. However, empathy remains mostly surface-level and shifts quickly into advice without deeper exploration of shame, belonging, or identity.

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The session stays clearly focused on the target behavior, especially the high-risk moment of being handed an item around friends. Direction is appropriate but becomes repetitive and overly tactical rather than connecting the plan to broader reasons for change.

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The psychiatrist builds on the client’s responses and offers choices, showing moderate responsiveness. Collaboration is uneven because frequent structured lists feel more like guided selection than true co-creation.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The psychiatrist asks permission, invites choice, and avoids explicit coercion. The style remains advice-driven, with repeated scripts and step-by-step strategies that can subtly pressure the client toward preferred solutions.

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Evocation is weak, with questions mostly focused on selecting a behavior strategy rather than drawing out values, confidence, importance, or intrinsic motivation. The client offers change talk, but it is not consistently reflected, amplified, or expanded.

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** The psychiatrist invites input but often assumes the expert problem-solver role by repeatedly generating lists of options. Power sharing is uneven because the clinician drives the content and structure more than the client’s own experience.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** There are a few initial attempts to evoke change talk, and the client does offer reasons for stopping. The psychiatrist does not consistently reflect or amplify this talk, especially later when questions focus on tactics rather than desire, importance, or confidence.

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The psychiatrist acknowledges sustain talk without judgment, including concern about friends, wanting items, and not seeming broke or weird. However, the session mostly adds behavioral exits rather than using double-sided reflections or gentle pivots to work through ambivalence.
