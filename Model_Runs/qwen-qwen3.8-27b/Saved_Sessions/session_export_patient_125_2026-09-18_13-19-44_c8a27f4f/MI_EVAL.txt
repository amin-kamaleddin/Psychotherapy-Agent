# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

---

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Consistently nonjudgmental and validating throughout, using phrases like 'You don't have to fix anything here' and 'You don't owe anyone your optimism.' However, warmth is somewhat formulaic in places and lacks explicit affirmation of Tariq's worth independent of compliance, which would be especially important in a juvenile justice setting.

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 5
**Comments:** Reflections consistently capture the emotional core rather than merely paraphrasing, accurately tracking Tariq's arc from frustration to grief to exhaustion to self-protection. Particularly insightful in identifying Tariq's protective mechanism ('you're protecting yourself from hoping'), demonstrating genuinely skilled empathic listening.

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Session follows a clear logical progression from frustration through grief to self-protection and identity, with each question building on the last. However, it lacks a concrete target behavior or collaboratively identified goal by the end, ending on a reflective note without a clear sense of forward direction.

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Strong collaborative framing with language like 'From where you're sitting' and 'I'm here to listen,' consistently seeking Tariq's input before offering reflections. The collaboration is somewhat one-sided, with the psychiatrist doing most of the reflecting work while Tariq primarily responds, and lacks explicit acknowledgment of Tariq's expertise as co-author of the session's meaning.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Strong autonomy language throughout, clearly communicating that Tariq doesn't have to perform or be optimistic, and avoids imposing advice or directives. Could be strengthened by more explicitly affirming Tariq's ownership of both successes and setbacks, and by not framing his anger as something that needs to be managed or survived.

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Effective evocative questions ('What does your dad mean to you?', 'What would make these three weeks feel less like just waiting?') draw out Tariq's own values and motivations without imposing reasons for change. The psychiatrist misses opportunities to deepen key change talk, such as not following up on 'I'll make it up to him' or 'I don't wanna be that person who swallows everything' with questions that expand on those motivations.

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Maintains a collaborative stance and avoids an expert-driven approach, frequently inviting Tariq's perspective. However, the psychiatrist occasionally positions himself as the one who 'gets' Tariq (e.g., 'that one got me'), creating a slight hierarchy, and could strengthen partnership through more explicit agenda negotiation and acknowledgment of Tariq's expertise in his own life.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Some change talk emerges from Tariq's own momentum (desire to 'make it up to' his dad, not wanting to 'swallow everything'), but the psychiatrist doesn't consistently pick up, amplify, or strategically deepen these expressions. When Tariq offers meaningful change talk, the psychiatrist acknowledges it briefly without asking follow-up questions that would expand and solidify his commitment.

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Handles sustain talk effectively by reflecting the experience (e.g., 'the bar keeps moving') without reinforcing blame or arguing against it, while gradually shifting focus toward Tariq's internal experience. Could be more strategic in moments like when Tariq says he 'doesn't really trust that yet'—a double-sided reflection exploring what might make it different would have been a stronger intervention.
