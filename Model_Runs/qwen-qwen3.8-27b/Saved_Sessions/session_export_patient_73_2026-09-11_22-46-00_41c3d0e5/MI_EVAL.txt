# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

---

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The psychiatrist shows intermittent respect and validation by naming the client's fear, frustration, and concerns about money. However, acceptance is repeatedly undercut by an immediate shift into directive advice and plan-building rather than fully holding space for the client's experience.

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The psychiatrist makes consistent but surface-level reflections of the client's emotions, including fear, frustration, and worry about social judgment. Deeper feelings such as shame, identity fusion with smoking, and ambivalence about repeated failed attempts are not explored or complexly reflected.

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The session maintains a clear, unwavering focus on tobacco cessation and systematically narrows the discussion to a specific trigger, goal, and coping strategy. This direction is strategically effective, but it is achieved through prescriptive rather than collaborative MI-consistent methods.

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Although the psychiatrist uses collaborative-sounding language, the interaction is largely expert-driven with the counselor generating most of the plan. The client is positioned as a recipient of pre-constructed strategies rather than as a co-author of her own change process.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** The psychiatrist frequently provides explicit instructions, scripts, and specific plans, which undermines the client's sense of choice. Autonomy is occasionally acknowledged, but it is not consistently supported through open exploration or client-led decision-making.

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** The client's own motivation, values, and past successes are not effectively drawn out or amplified by the psychiatrist. Rather than eliciting change talk, the psychiatrist supplies an external framework of strategies, scripts, and a structured plan.

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** The psychiatrist frames the work as collaborative, but the actual interaction is dominated by counselor-driven planning and advice. The client's expertise in her own social context and preferences is underutilized, limiting the sense of working as equal partners.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** The client spontaneously generates some change talk, but the psychiatrist rarely reflects, amplifies, or builds on these statements. Emerging motivation is diluted when the psychiatrist quickly adds additional strategies and rehearsal prompts instead of exploring the client's reasons for change.

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** The client expresses substantial sustain talk related to fear of failure, social pressure, and appearing weird, but these concerns are only briefly acknowledged. The psychiatrist does not use double-sided reflections or strategic exploration to soften or work through the ambivalence.
