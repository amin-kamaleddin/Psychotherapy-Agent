# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

---

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist was respectful, nonjudgmental, and affirming of the patient’s progress without blaming him for relapse. Warmth was somewhat brief and procedural, with safety language occasionally making the tone feel more monitoring than fully accepting.

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** There were adequate reflections of fear, confusion, and difficulty, but the psychiatrist tended to move quickly toward solutions. Deeper emotional material such as shame, ambivalence, and the meaning of his children was not consistently reflected.

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The session stayed clearly focused on preventing nighttime relapse to alcohol and pills, including triggers, access, and coping. Direction sometimes narrowed into safety planning and logistics rather than the motivational core of ambivalence, shame, and recovery identity.

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The psychiatrist invited input and adjusted to patient preferences, such as avoiding written plans and using a simple text. However, repeated therapist-generated strategy lists made the interaction more expert-driven than fully collaborative.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist consistently asked for choice and permission, and he respected the patient’s simpler, lower-effort approach. Some summaries sounded directive, which could reduce the sense that the plan was fully client-owned.

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Some questions helped the patient voice strengths and what has been helping, but the psychiatrist did not consistently expand the patient’s own motivations. Stronger evocation would include asking how his children, progress, and values shape his reasons for change.

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The patient’s preferences influenced decisions, such as avoiding paperwork and choosing the slippers idea. Still, the psychiatrist often led the planning and generated multiple options rather than letting the patient’s nighttime experience drive the session.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** The patient offered meaningful change talk about his children, three months of progress, and wanting to avoid feeling ruined, but the psychiatrist did not consistently develop or amplify it. More reflection, scaling, and value-based questions are needed.

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The psychiatrist avoided arguing and used reassuring statements that reduced defensiveness, such as “you don’t need paperwork to stay safe.” However, the patient’s central ambivalence was acknowledged but not deeply explored or reflected in a two-sided way.
