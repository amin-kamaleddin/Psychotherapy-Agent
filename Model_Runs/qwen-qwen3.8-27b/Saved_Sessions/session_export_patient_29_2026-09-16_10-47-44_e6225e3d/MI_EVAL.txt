# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

---

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The psychiatrist is generally respectful and nonjudgmental, but acceptance is more task-oriented than consistently warm. There are fewer explicit affirmations of the client beyond progress, cooperation, and smoking-cessation goals.

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist accurately reflects practical concerns, ambivalence, and strengths related to stress, social smoking, and cravings. However, responses are more content-focused than emotion-focused, often missing the deeper emotional weight of the client's anxiety and self-criticism.

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The session stays clearly focused on smoking cessation, motivation, triggers, medication, and coping planning. At times the psychiatrist delivers large amounts of information, which can make the interaction feel more like a medical consultation than a client-led motivational interview.

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** There are useful collaborative moments, especially when selecting refusal strategies and role-playing. Overall, much of the session is driven by the psychiatrist's information delivery and prestructured plan rather than co-created with the client.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The psychiatrist acknowledges the client's choices and offers options at key moments. However, frequent direct instructions and recommendations reduce the emphasis on the client as the ultimate decision-maker.

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The psychiatrist asks good initial evocative questions and uses rulers to explore readiness and confidence. After the opening, the session shifts toward education and advice rather than continuing to develop the client's own change talk.

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The psychiatrist invites client input and shared decision-making in several places. The overall stance remains more expert-driven than collaborative, with the psychiatrist leading much of the session structure and content.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The psychiatrist elicits some useful change talk and asks questions that bring out the client's reasons for quitting. These statements are not consistently amplified, deepened, or consolidated into stronger commitment language.

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist generally avoids arguing with the client's concerns and normalizes worries about cravings, social pressure, and slips. However, sustain talk is not always skillfully redirected into change talk using advanced MI strategies.
