# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

---

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The psychiatrist is generally respectful and nonjudgmental, using basic validating statements without criticizing the client. However, the tone remains somewhat procedural and lacks deeper warmth or individualized attunement to Thomas’s emotional experience.

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Empathy is mostly factual and surface-level, with limited perspective-taking on the client’s feelings and values. Missed opportunities include reflecting concern about his child’s safety, parental responsibility, and work stress.

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The session stays focused on smoking, alcohol use, and safety, but the MI direction is weak. The conversation does not clearly guide Thomas toward a specific, personally meaningful change goal and often drifts into general safety instructions.

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Collaboration is limited because the session is largely clinician-led and driven by assessment, screening, and advice. The client’s preferences do not substantially shape a shared agenda or co-created plan.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The psychiatrist uses autonomy-supportive language such as “no pressure” and offers options, acknowledging the client’s right to choose. However, continued advice and repeated suggestions after Thomas signals he wants to leave make autonomy support inconsistent.

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** There are a few evocative attempts, such as an open question and readiness ruler, but the therapist largely provides advice instead of drawing out the client’s own motivations. Deeper values and concerns, such as being a reliable parent and worker, are not explored.

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** The psychiatrist makes superficial partnership moves by asking what Thomas wants and offering choices, but the overall stance remains directive and unequal. Thomas’s contributions do not meaningfully guide the session, and the clinician continues to lead with structured recommendations.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** The therapist makes weak attempts to elicit change talk, but the client’s brief change statements are not reinforced, expanded, or summarized. The session quickly shifts into advice-giving rather than developing the client’s own reasons for change.

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** The client presents sustain talk by minimizing use and expressing low concern, and the psychiatrist avoids arguing with him. However, responses often inadvertently validate the status quo without using double-sided reflections, discrepancy, or values-based exploration.
