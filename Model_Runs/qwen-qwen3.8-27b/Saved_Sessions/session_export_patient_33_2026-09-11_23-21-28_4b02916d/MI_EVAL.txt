# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

---

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist maintains a respectful, nonjudgmental stance and avoids pressuring the client to quit. Acceptance is slightly limited by warm, task-focused affirmations rather than deeper individualized statements of unconditional positive regard.

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The therapist shows adequate perspective-taking but often moves quickly from reflections to practical advice. Deeper emotional themes such as fear of judgment, shame, and the meaning of safety are not fully explored.

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The session remains clearly focused on safer use and immediate overdose-prevention planning with the housemate and Narcan. Direction is somewhat weakened by repeated addition of checklist-like safety elements, which can feel more structured than client-driven.

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The client’s preferences and chosen line influence the plan, but the therapist frequently provides structure, scripts, and backup options. The interaction feels more therapist-guided than a fully equal collaboration.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The therapist consistently offers options and respects the client’s decision to avoid quitting or rehab. Repeated suggestions and checklists subtly shift the session toward therapist-generated solutions rather than client-generated planning.

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The session begins with open, evocative questions but becomes more advisory and task-oriented. It does not consistently draw out the client’s deeper values, motivations, or confidence in staying safe.

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The therapist asks the client for input on goals, topics, and plan elements, showing some partnership. However, the therapist frequently adopts an expert problem-solving role rather than fully positioning the client as the primary source of solutions.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The client expresses useful change talk about safer use and not dying, and the therapist affirms this. However, the therapist does not consistently deepen or expand the client’s own change language with evocative questions.

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The therapist accepts the client’s sustain talk without argument, pressure, or shame, and redirects toward harm reduction and safety. It is slightly weaker because the therapist does not explicitly reflect the ambivalence behind both continued use and safety concerns.
