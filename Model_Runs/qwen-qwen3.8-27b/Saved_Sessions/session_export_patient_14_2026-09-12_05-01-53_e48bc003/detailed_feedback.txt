                                Motivational Interviewing Feedback Criteria (5-Point Likert Scale)

Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)
------------------------------------
1. Acceptance: Extent to which the counselor communicates unconditional positive regard for the client.
   1 = Judgmental, disapproving, labeling
   2 = Inconsistent respect, occasional judgment
   3 = Neutral stance, basic respect
   4 = Generally respectful and supportive
   5 = Consistently respectful, warm, supportive

2. Empathy: Extent to which the counselor understands and/or makes an effort to accurately understand the client’s perspective.
   1 = Little effort to understand, minimal reflective listening
   2 = Limited perspective-taking, mostly factual focus
   3 = Adequate but inconsistent effort to understand
   4 = Good reflective listening and perspective-taking
   5 = Highly accurate, consistent understanding and reflections

3. Direction – Degree to which the counselor maintains appropriate focus on a specific target behavior or concerns directly tied to it.
1 = No clear focus; session wanders aimlessly
2 = Minimal focus; frequent topic drift
3 = Occasional focus on target behavior but inconsistent
4 = Generally keeps discussion directed toward target behavior
5 = Consistently maintains clear focus and guides toward change

---

4. Collaboration – Extent to which the clinician behaves as if the interview is occurring between two equal partners, both of whom have knowledge that might be useful in the problem under consideration.
1 = Authoritarian or expert-driven stance
2 = Limited collaboration; frequent direction by counselor
3 = Partial collaboration; uneven power-sharing
4 = Strong collaborative stance most of the time
5 = Fully collaborative; client’s ideas shape session direction

---

5. Autonomy Support – Extent to which clinician supports and actively fosters client perception of choice as opposed to attempting to control the client’s behavior or choices.
1 = Directive or pressuring; minimizes choice
2 = Occasionally acknowledges choice but inconsistently
3 = Neutral regarding client autonomy
4 = Regularly affirms client freedom to choose
5 = Consistently reinforces client ownership of change process

---

6. Evocation – Extent to which the clinician conveys an understanding that motivation for change, and the ability to move toward that change, reside mostly within the client and therefore focuses efforts to elicit and expand it within the therapeutic interaction.
1 = Provides reasons for change without exploring client view
2 = Limited interest in client motivations
3 = Some evocation but superficial
4 = Frequently draws out client’s own reasons for change
5 = Consistently elicits and develops client change talk throughout session

---

7. Partnership – Extent to which the clinician conveys an understanding that expertise and wisdom about change reside mostly within the client

1 = Clinician actively assumes the expert role for the majority of the interaction with the client. Collaboration or partnership is absent.
2 = Clinician superficially responds to opportunities to collaborate.
3 = Clinician incorporates client’s contributions but does so in a lukewarm or erratic fashion.
4 = Clinician fosters collaboration and power sharing so that client’s contributions impact the session in ways that they otherwise would not.
5 = Clinician actively fosters and encourages power sharing in the interaction in such a way that client’s contributions substantially influence the nature of the session.

---

8. Cultivating Change Talk – Extent to which the clinician actively encourages the client’s own language in favor of the change goal, and confidence for making that change.
1 = No effort to evoke change talk
2 = Rare or weak efforts
3 = Occasional evocation of change language
4 = Frequent evocation of change talk
5 = Consistent, skillful evocation throughout

---

9. Softening Sustain Talk – Extent that the clinician avoids a focus on the reasons against changing or for maintaining the status quo.
1 = Reinforces sustain talk
2 = Rarely shifts focus away from sustain talk
3 = Occasionally redirects toward change
4 = Often redirects effectively
5 = Consistently pivots client language toward change

Evaluation Guidance
-------------------
- All domains are rated on a 1–5 Likert scale.
- Ratings should reflect overall quality and consistency of behavior.
- Focus on both counselor skillfulness and client speech as indicators of MI process quality.

 # Motivational Interviewing Feedback: Psychiatrist Evaluation

## Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)

### 1. Acceptance
Score: 4/5

Explanation:
The psychiatrist communicates respect and nonjudgment throughout. They acknowledge the client's frustration without criticism ("You sound frustrated and squeezed... That makes a lot of sense") and avoid moralizing about weight, food, or the client's resistance. Statements such as "I'm glad you came," "not punishing yourself if it's missed," and "I respect how you're keeping it practical" signal acceptance of the person and his pragmatic stance. However, the warmth is efficient rather than deeply relational. The counselor does not fully normalize ambivalence, does not explicitly apologize for the wait despite the client's opening complaint, and the frequent praise ("nice," "smart") is slightly evaluative rather than purely affirming. This is good but not yet exemplary unconditional positive regard.

---

### 2. Empathy
Score: 3/5

Explanation:
The psychiatrist shows a strong initial empathic reflection: "You sound frustrated and squeezed—angry about the time, wary of lectures, but also aware you're carrying too much and your doctor's concerned." This accurately captures the client's emotional state and ambivalence. Later, the counselor also reflects practical constraints, such as acknowledging that a confidence rating of six "makes sense given the rush and the forgetting risk."

The limitation is that much of the rest of the session reflects content and plan quality rather than deeper feeling or meaning. For example, the client's underlying stress about having no time, fear of another unmanageable prescription, frustration with being told what to do, or concern about blood pressure and independence are not explored. Phrases like "That's a smart, simple move" and repeated summaries of the plan are useful but more cognitive than empathic. The counselor misses opportunities to reflect the emotional burden of the client's schedule, the meaning of "getting hold of" his weight, or the relief of choosing a small test rather than a full lifestyle overhaul.

---

### 3. Cultivating Change Talk
Score: 3/5

Explanation:
The psychiatrist evokes some client-driven language by asking what would be useful, which option sounds best, which snack the client will try, and what would raise confidence from 6 to 8. These questions help the client move toward a small commitment and produce practical change-related language such as choosing cheese, clipping it to the keys, and trying the plan.

However, the session does not consistently cultivate change talk in the MI sense. The counselor rarely asks about the client's reasons, importance, desires, needs, or ability: for example, "Why is this worth trying?", "What would it mean to you if you started making small changes?", "What part of your health would this help?", or "What have you noticed when you make simpler choices?" The client's change-related statements, such as wanting to "stop the salty habit" or keeping it up if it makes a difference, are not actively reflected, amplified, or expanded. The session moves quickly from motivation to implementation, so change talk remains underdeveloped.

### 4. Softening Sustain Talk
Score: 4/5

Explanation:
The psychiatrist handles sustain talk quite well. When the client says he has no time, refuses breathing exercises, and says he will stop if it is too much bother, the counselor does not argue, debate, or reinforce the client's reasons for avoiding change. This effectively reduces the threat of change by making it a small experiment rather than a major lifestyle demand. A limitation is that the counselor does not use more explicit MI techniques such as double-sided reflections or rolling with resistance. For example, the counselor could have said, "On one hand, your schedule leaves no room for another big project, and on the other hand, you still want something practical that fits your life." That would have more deliberately softened sustain talk while preserving the client's autonomy.

### 5. Partnership

A stronger partnership stance would have invited the client to propose his own solution first and would have more explicitly positioned the client as the expert on his own schedule, car habits, and practical constraints.

### 6. Direction
Score: 4/5

Explanation:
The psychiatrist maintains a clear and practical direction focused on a behavior related to the client's weight and blood pressure concern: reducing salty car snacking. The focus on low-sodium choices, cravings, hunger, and possible blood pressure changes keeps the target behavior relevant.

The main limitation is that the direction becomes quite narrow and implementation-focused. The counselor could have more explicitly connected the snack swap to the broader goals of weight management, blood pressure, energy, or the client's stated desire to "get hold of" his weight. ### 7. Autonomy Support
Score: 3/5

Explanation:
The psychiatrist supports autonomy in several ways: offering choices, asking which option sounds best, accepting the client's refusal of breathing exercises, and emphasizing that missing the step is not a crisis or a reason for self-punishment. The counselor does not use explicit autonomy-supportive language such as "It's your choice," "You decide what fits," or "I'm here to support whatever makes sense to you." ### 8. Collaboration

Explanation:

### 9. Evocation
Score: 3/5

The counselor also provides reasons and options rather than waiting for the client to articulate them. This produces a practical plan but a relatively thin motivational process.

**Overall Global Ratings Score:** **3.0/5**

Overall, the session is respectful, efficient, and generally aligned with the client's pragmatic style. The counselor handles resistance well, avoids argument, and keeps the focus on a small, relevant behavior. However, the session relies too heavily on advice, specific recommendations, and implementation details, and it does not sufficiently evoke the client's own change talk, values, or deeper ambivalence. To improve, the psychiatrist should ask before advising, deepen empathic reflections of the client's stress and constraints, use more double-sided reflections, and deliberately elicit the client's reasons for change before narrowing to a plan.
