# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

---

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist uses a respectful, nonjudgmental stance with clear validation of the client’s concerns and goals. Some warmth feels formulaic and includes evaluative praise, plus a mild opening assumption before the client fully narrates the event.

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Reflections accurately capture the incident, fear of consequences, and connection to soccer and college goals. They often stay at the content level and miss deeper emotions such as guilt, fear of disappointing his mother, and the identity stakes involved.

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 5
**Comments:** The session stays tightly focused on alcohol-related risk and the client’s soccer and scholarship goals. It moves logically from concern to practical planning without meaningful topic drift.

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The client is treated as an active participant and meaningfully chooses the exit line, support person, timing, and preferred approach. The interaction is still somewhat therapist-directed because the psychiatrist supplies many concrete options and scripts.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist repeatedly affirms choice, ownership, and client-driven decisions without pressuring immediate disclosure or a specific action. Frequent therapist-provided scripts and options can slightly reduce emphasis on the client generating his own solutions.

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Scaling questions and targeted prompts effectively draw out some motivation and confidence. Evocation is limited when the conversation shifts too quickly into practical scripting and problem-solving rather than deeper client-centered reasons.

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist uses several collaborative invitations and allows client preferences to shape the session. Some structured guidance and option-giving make the process feel less fully client-led.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The client offers useful change talk about staying safe, protecting his future, and keeping his head straight. The psychiatrist sometimes moves quickly into practical options rather than amplifying, expanding, or deepening the client’s own change language.

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist handles minimization well without arguing or lecturing, keeping the tone nonjudgmental and reducing defensiveness. More double-sided reflections or explicit ambivalence reframing would strengthen this area.
