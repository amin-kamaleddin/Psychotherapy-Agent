# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

---

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist maintained a respectful, nonjudgmental stance and validated the patient’s fear and prior experiences. Acceptance was slightly limited by occasional language that subtly endorsed the testing agenda rather than affirming the patient unconditionally.

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Reflections were generally accurate and showed understanding of the patient’s physical symptoms, ambivalence, and fear of being dismissed. Deeper emotional themes, such as fear of death, exhaustion, and feeling invalidated, were not consistently explored.

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The session stayed organized around practical GI evaluation and preparing a GP request. However, the broader MI focus of exploring the stress-symptom connection and reducing rigid reliance on biomedical certainty was not consistently addressed.

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The psychiatrist invited input, adjusted the note, and incorporated the patient’s preferences, showing real collaboration. This was limited by an early, expert-driven focus on a detailed medical checklist and scripted testing agenda.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The therapist regularly supported choice by asking preferences, respecting the patient’s decisions, and avoiding overt pressure. Some wording still subtly pushed the testing agenda, slightly weakening autonomy support.

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The psychiatrist elicited some priorities, preferences, and reflections about when symptoms eased. However, the questions mostly remained practical and did not deeply evoke values, motivations, or readiness for change.

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The patient’s input shaped parts of the plan, such as sedation, written documentation, and note wording. The therapist also took an expert-driven role by quickly offering a clinical checklist and scripts without fully exploring the patient’s own understanding first.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** There were some initial change-oriented statements, and the therapist offered choices that could support ownership. Important change talk about symptoms improving with activity and willingness to discuss patterns was not sufficiently developed or evoked.

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** The therapist did not argue with the patient’s focus on testing and fear of missed cancer. Overall, however, the session largely reinforced the biomedical testing agenda rather than actively softening that dominant sustain talk.
