# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

---

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist remains respectful, nonjudgmental, and supportive despite the patient’s defensiveness and ambivalence. Some reflections add meanings beyond what the patient stated, such as “I hear hope” or protecting family, which makes the acceptance slightly less pure.

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist accurately reflects practical and emotional difficulties, including the patient’s exhaustion and the meaning of beer as a decompression ritual. A few reflections remain somewhat interpretive rather than fully grounded in the patient’s stated experience.

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The session stays focused on alcohol, diet, and diabetes management, moving toward concrete steps like meter use, doctor communication, and replacement routines. Diet is underexplored, and the focus on doctor wording slightly narrows the broader lifestyle direction.

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 5
**Comments:** The interaction is genuinely collaborative, with the patient’s lived experience shaping the plan and next steps. The psychiatrist co-creates options using the patient’s own language, such as weekend beer reduction, walking, sitting with his wife, and a short pause on exhausting days.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist consistently affirms the patient as the decision-maker and avoids pressuring him to commit. Repeated reliance on the doctor’s approval subtly weakens the emphasis on the patient’s internal agency.

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Open questions invite the patient to generate his own solutions, such as reducing beer to weekends and using a simple check with the meter. The evocation could be stronger by more consistently drawing out values, confidence, and intrinsic motivation beyond medical safety.

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist treats the patient as the expert on his life, schedule, and preferences. There are a few moments where the clinician suggests direction, such as treating walking or sitting with his wife as the first replacement, which reduces the partnership slightly.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The patient generates meaningful change language, including reducing beer to weekends, walking, sitting with his wife, and cooking instead of grabbing food. The psychiatrist could have more deeply explored the importance, values, and confidence behind these changes.

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist avoids arguing with the patient’s reasons for maintaining the beer, reframing his stance as careful and responsible. The final sustain talk about whether resting is “giving in” is not addressed, missing a chance to reframe self-compassion as part of change.
