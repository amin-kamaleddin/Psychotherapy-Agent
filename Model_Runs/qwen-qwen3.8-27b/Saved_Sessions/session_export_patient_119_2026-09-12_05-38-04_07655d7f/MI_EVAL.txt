# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

---

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 5
**Comments:** The psychiatrist consistently shows unconditional positive regard, validating the client’s minimization and lapses without judgment or frustration. Statements like “that makes sense,” “no logging, no guilt,” and “next one’s tomorrow” strongly reinforce a warm, non-shaming stance.

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist accurately reflects the client’s ambivalence, practical burden, and shame around missed doses. Empathy could deepen further by exploring the emotional meaning behind the client’s minimization and self-doubt.

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The session remains well focused on medication adherence and a practical plan for the midday barrier. Direction is slightly weakened when the conversation shifts into administrative check-in details and narrows away from broader diabetes management.

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist invites input, offers options, and co-constructs a plan that the client clearly shapes, including the sink location and no-log approach. Collaboration could improve by eliciting the client’s own ideas before presenting therapist-generated options.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 5
**Comments:** The psychiatrist strongly supports choice and ownership through phrases like “I’ll follow your lead,” “no logging, no guilt,” and flexible check-in options. The client is consistently positioned as the decision-maker with pressure and grading explicitly avoided.

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist frequently evokes the client’s own motivations, confidence, and preferences using scaling and “what would move you from a 5 to a 7?” questions. Evocation would be stronger with more exploration of the client’s values and longer-term meanings.

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist generally treats the client as the expert on her own life, schedule, and preferences. Partnership is slightly less than optimal because the therapist still offers structured suggestions before fully inviting the client’s own ideas.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist actively elicits change talk through confidence scaling, past-success questions, and “what would move you from a 5 to a 7?” The client produces useful change talk, but the therapist could amplify and deepen it more consistently.

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist handles sustain talk well by avoiding argument, reassurance, or persuasion and by acknowledging the client’s statements. It could improve by more consistently pivoting sustain talk back toward change talk.
