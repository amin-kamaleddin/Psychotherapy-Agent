# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

---

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist consistently communicates respect, nonjudgment, and unconditional positive regard, avoiding labels or moralizing about substance use. However, the warmth is somewhat procedural, and there is a missed opportunity to more explicitly affirm Tyler’s strengths and independence.

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The psychiatrist demonstrates adequate reflective listening and tracks Tyler’s priorities around friendship and fitting in, but the empathic work remains fairly surface-level. The clinician does not fully capture the emotional intensity under the resistance, and the exact repetition of a question undermines empathic attunement.

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The session maintains a general direction around health, friendships, and home conflict, but the focus on the specific target behavior is vague. The psychiatrist repeatedly uses the word "health" without clearly connecting it to the specific behavior or its consequences, allowing the conversation to drift toward the mother’s reaction.

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist behaves collaboratively by inviting Tyler’s perspective and following his lead on several topics, reinforcing a stance where Tyler is the expert on his own life. The collaboration is not perfect because the therapist occasionally imposes structure, such as "Maybe one boundary with your mom, one way to stay safe with your health," and the repeated question can make the interaction feel less mutually regulated.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist provides strong autonomy support, repeatedly affirming Tyler’s right to make his own choices and avoiding coercive or pressuring language. The score is not a 5 because there are moments where the therapist's repeated questioning may feel subtly pressuring, and the repeated emphasis on "health" and "staying safe" may begin to feel like a hidden agenda.

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The psychiatrist uses evocative questions and reflects the client's own language, inviting Tyler to articulate his own goals and strategies. However, the evocation is not consistently deep or strategic, and the therapist does not fully draw out Tyler's underlying values, fears, or reasons for change.

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist demonstrates a generally collaborative, partnership-oriented stance, conveying that the client is the expert on his own life and choices. The partnership could be stronger if the therapist more consistently negotiated the agenda and avoided repetitive questioning, which can feel less collaborative.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The psychiatrist evokes some change talk, especially ability talk, by reflecting back Tyler's statements that he can handle things and manage the health side. However, the cultivation of change talk is not consistently developed, and there are missed opportunities to deepen desire, ability, reasons, need, or commitment language.

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** This is the weakest domain; the psychiatrist acknowledges Tyler's sustain talk without judging it but does not effectively soften or redirect it. At times, the therapist inadvertently reinforces the sustain talk by accepting the framing that the main problem is the mother's reaction, rather than focusing on health risk, values, or future consequences.
