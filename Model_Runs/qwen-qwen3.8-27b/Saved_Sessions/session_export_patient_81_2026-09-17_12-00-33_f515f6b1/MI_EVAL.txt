# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

---

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Consistent nonjudgmental stance throughout, with a strong opening that sets a safe tone. Slightly dinged for occasional subtle editorializing (e.g., 'That's someone with a clear lens on themselves') that tells the client what they are rather than simply reflecting.

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Good reflective listening with accurate captures of the client's experience (e.g., the 'still frame' metaphor, the push-pull of defending and acknowledging). Limitations include repeated generic 'I understand. Can you tell me more?' responses and occasional over-interpretation layered on top of reflections.

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The session maintains a general focus on substance use and its consequences but lacks a clear strategic through-line toward a specific target behavior. The conversation wanders across multiple topics without collaboratively establishing an agenda or progressive narrowing.

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Generally collaborative stance with inclusive language and invitations for the client's perspective. However, the psychiatrist does the heavy lifting of framing and interpreting rather than co-constructing meaning, and some responses feel one-directional.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Strong autonomy support with consistent non-directive language ('This space is yours,' 'You don't owe me a thesis statement'). Could be strengthened by more explicitly affirming the client's right to make no changes at all.

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The final question about whether the cost is 'getting harder to ignore' is a skillful evocative move that draws out genuine change talk. However, the session lacks consistent evocation of values, future goals, or what change would look like for the client.

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Consistently collaborative stance that invites the client's input and avoids an expert-driven approach. Some formulaic 'I understand. Can you tell me more?' responses and a leading final question slightly undermine the partner stance.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The psychiatrist evokes some change talk through affirming the client's track record and the skillful closing question. However, there is no exploration of reasons for change, benefits of change, confidence in change, or values that might conflict with the current pattern.

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Sustain talk is handled well through gentle double-sided reflections and avoiding direct confrontation. The psychiatrist could more strategically use the client's sustain talk as a springboard for exploration rather than primarily just acknowledging it.
