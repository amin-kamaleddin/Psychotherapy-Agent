# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

---

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist consistently uses a nonjudgmental, validating stance that reduces client shame and disapproval. Acceptance is strong but somewhat formulaic, relying on repeated phrases rather than deeper individualized warmth.

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist accurately reflects the client’s ambivalence, fear, and shifting readiness with emotionally attuned statements. Reflections are generally strong but do not consistently explore deeper emotional themes such as self-worth or identity threat.

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The session maintains a clear focus on the target behavior and progressively narrows options toward a small, concrete step. The direction is purposeful but can feel mechanical rather than exploring deeper values or meaning.

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The session is framed as a joint process, with frequent invitations for input and adaptation to the client’s choices. Collaboration is strong, though the repeated menu-of-options approach makes the interaction feel somewhat clinician-structured.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The clinician consistently affirms the client’s right to choose and avoids pressuring her toward commitment. Autonomy support is clear, though it could be strengthened by more explicitly affirming the client’s capacity to own both successes and setbacks.

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Appropriate evocative questions are used to draw out the client’s perspective and motivations. However, the clinician often moves quickly to behavioral options before the client’s own change motivations are fully developed.

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The clinician invites input and follows some client choices, creating a sense of shared work. Partnership is present but somewhat limited by the frequent presentation of structured options, which makes the session feel less fully co-created.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Some change talk is elicited through questions about what change might feel like or what would make it worth it. The clinician does not consistently affirm, amplify, or expand upon the client’s own change statements.

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** The psychiatrist avoids arguing against sustain talk but largely validates it without effectively softening or exploring its costs. Sustain talk remains dominant, and the client’s rejected reframing is not followed by additional MI-consistent strategies.
