# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

---

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist maintains a warm, respectful, and nonjudgmental stance, affirming Elias’s dignity without labeling him as lazy, failing, or a burden. The acceptance is strong, though it sometimes feels slightly formulaic and does not fully express unconditional positive regard.

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Reflective listening is accurate and helpful, capturing Elias’s ambivalence, need for support without being handled, and the tension between presence and feeling tested. The empathy is strong, but it could explore deeper emotional themes such as loneliness, shame, and loss of independence more fully.

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The session remains focused on increasing physical activity through a small, realistic home-based action involving standing or walking with his spouse. Direction is clear, though it could be strengthened by explicitly connecting the micro-plan to broader goals such as independence, dignity, or quality of life.

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The conversation is collaborative, with Elias’s ideas driving the plan, including the kitchen setup, radio buffer, and language for asking for space. It could improve with more explicit invitations for Elias to choose the next step or adjust the focus of the session.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist avoids direct advice and pressure, inviting Elias to identify what would make a small activity feel safe and acceptable. Autonomy support is strong, but it would benefit from more explicit reinforcement of Elias’s right to choose and own the definition of success.

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The psychiatrist evokes some practical change talk by asking what has worked before and what would make a tiny active moment feel doable. However, evocation remains more procedural than motivational and does not consistently draw out deeper values, confidence, or stronger change language.

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist shares power with Elias by acknowledging his expertise about his body and relationship, allowing his preferences to shape the session. The partnership is generally strong, though repeated phrases such as “since you know your body best” can feel coached rather than fully collaborative.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The psychiatrist elicits some concrete change actions, such as standing at the sink, walking five steps, using the radio, and saying “we’re done” out loud. Change talk is present but not consistently amplified or deepened with stronger desire, ability, need, or commitment language.

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Elias’s barriers are acknowledged and gently reframed, such as “safe pause,” “stopping to be allowed,” and “company without surveillance.” The response is strong, though it could more consistently use double-sided reflections to pivot from sustain talk toward change.
