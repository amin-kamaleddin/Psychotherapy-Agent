# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

---

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist consistently maintains a respectful, nonjudgmental tone and validates the client's desire for control without imposing abstinence. However, the warmth remains professional rather than deeply affirming the emotional weight of her anxiety, repeated failed attempts, and loneliness.

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The reflections are accurate at the cognitive and behavioral level but remain largely on the surface of logic and behavior. More empathic depth is needed to reflect underlying emotions such as social anxiety, fear of judgment, exhaustion, and pain from using alcohol to cope.

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The session maintains clear focus on reducing alcohol use and progresses logically from assessment to exploration, planning, and confidence scaling. Direction could be strengthened by reducing question bundling and more explicitly connecting the plan to the client's stated values and vision of a better week.

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist invites client input and builds the plan around her preferences, creating a generally collaborative stance. However, the collaboration is somewhat asymmetric because the psychiatrist generates most of the strategic content while the client mainly selects from provided options.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist regularly affirms the client's freedom to choose, asks permission, presents options as choices, and respects her decisions without pushback. The large number of suggestions offered at times can subtly shift the dynamic toward the psychiatrist providing the solutions rather than the client generating her own.

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Some evocative techniques are used, such as the importance and confidence rulers and the question about a better week. However, evocation is often interrupted by structured practical suggestions, limiting deeper exploration of the client's own values, motivations, and self-efficacy.

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist consistently conveys a collaborative stance by asking permission, inviting input, and allowing the client's choices to shape the session. The partnership is limited by the structured, list-based approach, which can make the psychiatrist feel more like a consultant than a true equal partner.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The psychiatrist does elicit change talk through questions and confidence scaling, and the client expresses some motivation for change. However, the psychiatrist does not consistently amplify, deepen, or build on this change talk before moving to practical problem-solving.

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The psychiatrist acknowledges sustain talk without arguing or dismissing it, but does not actively soften or strategically redirect it. More use of double-sided reflections and direct engagement with underlying social fears would strengthen this area.
