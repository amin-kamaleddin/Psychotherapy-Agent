# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

---

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The psychiatrist is generally respectful and nonjudgmental, but evaluative comments such as “good call” and “smart plan” can feel conditional. Acceptance is further weakened by continuing to offer advice after the client signals she wants to end.

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The psychiatrist reflects the client’s stated priorities and surface content accurately, but misses emotional cues such as feeling “tested,” “overthinking,” and not seeing a big problem. Reflections are adequate but not consistently deep or emotionally attuned.

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The session stays focused on the relevant concern of handling alcohol in social situations and avoids over-pathologizing. Direction becomes slightly less effective at the end when the counselor repeatedly summarizes and advises after the client wants to leave.

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** There are collaborative moments, especially around the client choosing the nonalcoholic drink strategy, but the psychiatrist frequently takes a directive role by adding extras and refusal phrases. The repeated closing summaries make the interaction feel more counselor-driven than mutually constructed.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The psychiatrist supports autonomy by asking what feels doable and affirming the client’s preference to trust her gut. However, repeatedly adding strategies and summaries after the client asks to wrap up subtly undermines her freedom to choose.

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The opening questions elicit the client’s internal motivations, such as caring about school and not letting parents down. The counselor does not consistently deepen this change talk, moving toward advice instead of exploring ability, need, or commitment.

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The psychiatrist initially invites the client’s perspective and input, but the dynamic shifts toward an expert-led model when offering multiple strategies and repeated suggestions. The partner stance is weakened by not fully following the client’s cue to end the session.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The client expresses meaningful reasons for staying safe and maintaining her priorities, but the psychiatrist does not consistently build on or expand this change talk. The session moves quickly into advice-giving and planning instead of deepening client-driven motivation.

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Sustain talk is mild, and the psychiatrist appropriately avoids challenging the “not a big problem” statement directly. There are missed opportunities to use double-sided reflection or gentle discrepancy work to soften ambivalence and move toward personal values.
