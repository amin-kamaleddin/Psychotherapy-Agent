# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

---

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The psychiatrist offers respectful, nonjudgmental statements and allows the client to leave, but repeatedly adds safety checks and unsolicited advice. This protective, managerial tone reduces the warmth and unconditional regard experienced by the client.

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** There are adequate surface-level reflections of frustration and embarrassment, but the responses become repetitive and formulaic. The psychiatrist misses deeper themes about autonomy, the peer group, and the parent-child dynamic.

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** The session drifts away from the presenting issue of substance use and conflict toward crisis management and departure planning. The target behavior is not consistently explored or connected to the client's own goals.

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** The psychiatrist drives the agenda with practical options and advice rather than co-creating a shared direction. The client's preferences are repeatedly rejected and then met with more suggestions, creating an uneven power dynamic.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The psychiatrist acknowledges choice and says the client is in charge, but undermines this with repeated advice, safety checks, and logistical instructions. The client's comment about feeling lectured suggests the approach was not experienced as autonomy-supportive.

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** The psychiatrist makes minimal effort to elicit the client's own motivation, values, or reasons for change. Instead of exploring the meaning of the stash or peer group, the response focuses on external advice and safety planning.

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Although the session begins with a collaborative question, it quickly becomes expert-driven and managerially focused. The client's lived experience and perspective are not treated as central to guiding the conversation.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** The psychiatrist misses several opportunities to reflect, amplify, or build on the client's own language about not using, managing life, and wanting to avoid conflict. Change talk is not actively drawn out or strengthened.

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** The client's sustain talk is acknowledged but not meaningfully addressed with MI-consistent techniques such as double-sided reflections or gentle reframing. The therapist accepts the client's framing and then shifts to practical logistics, leaving ambivalence unexplored.
