# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

---

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The counselor is generally respectful and nonjudgmental, using language such as “I won’t judge” and “I respect your choice.” However, unconditional positive regard is weakened by normalizing avoidance and offering privacy tips to keep cannabis use hidden from the mother.

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The counselor reflects some of the client’s surface experience, such as feeling forced into the session and wanting to fit in with peers. Deeper emotions and meanings, including fear of losing peer belonging, resentment toward the mother, and discomfort with being labeled, are not fully explored.

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** The counselor initially gathers relevant information about use frequency, substance type, and potential consequences. After the client rejects help, the session loses focus on cannabis-related ambivalence and becomes centered on scripts, conflict management, and concealment strategies.

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** There are some collaborative attempts, such as asking the client to reword scripts in his own voice. Overall, the counselor remains more like an expert directing the session, limiting true co-creation of the agenda and treatment direction.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The counselor offers choices and includes autonomy-supportive language, such as “We can spend this time however you want.” However, repeated structured options and prescriptive suggestions, especially privacy tips, undermine genuine client ownership and values-based decision-making.

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** The counselor makes limited early efforts to evoke the client’s own motivations by asking about what is on his mind and what he likes about smoking. After that, the counselor shifts to advising and providing scripts rather than drawing out the client’s internal reasons for change.

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** The counselor tries to involve the client by offering choices and asking for preferences. The interaction remains counselor-driven, especially through repeated menus and scripts, rather than treating the client as the expert on his own life, values, and goals.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** There are only weak attempts to evoke change talk, mainly through a few early questions about use and possible problems. The counselor does not effectively explore values, future implications, or discrepancies that might motivate the client to consider change.

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** The counselor does not soften the client’s sustain talk and often reinforces it by agreeing with minimization and offering ways to avoid consequences. Suggesting privacy tips to keep the mother from finding out actively strengthens concealment and resistance rather than shifting focus toward ambivalence or change.
