# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

---

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist maintained a respectful, nonjudgmental stance and normalized the client’s worry and ambivalence. It fell short of 5 because the warmth was somewhat procedural and included a few inferences not fully grounded in the client’s own words.

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The clinician showed good reflective listening, especially in capturing the client’s concern about being seen as fragile or different. Deeper emotional themes and the final worry about an awkward pause were not fully explored.

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The session stayed focused on a clear target behavior: disclosing diabetes to a trusted peer in a brief, manageable way. Direction was strong but incomplete because the final concern about an awkward pause was left unresolved.

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist and client jointly refined wording, options, and the disclosure plan. Collaboration was limited by the clinician’s frequent provision of ready-made scripts before the client generated her own language.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The client was regularly offered choices, allowed to reject suggestions, and supported in deciding how much to disclose. Autonomy support could be stronger by inviting the client to create more of the wording herself.

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Some early questions invited the client’s own perspective and prevented a purely didactic session. Evocation was a key area for improvement because the session relied heavily on clinician-generated options rather than developing the client’s intrinsic motivation.

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist frequently invited input and respected the client’s preferences in choosing wording, timing, and a trusted friend. Partnership was not fully 5 because the clinician often took a coaching or script-providing role before fully waiting for the client’s own language.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The client produced some change-relevant language, including feeling that disclosure “didn’t feel as bad as I thought.” The clinician did not consistently amplify confidence or develop DARN-C change talk, especially desire, ability, reasons, need, or commitment.

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Sustain talk was generally handled without argument, including normalization of freezing and worry about follow-up questions. Some suggested strategies may have framed disclosure as high-stakes, and the final fear about an awkward pause was not fully addressed.
