# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

---

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist consistently avoids judgment, blame, or minimization while validating intense hopelessness and suicidality. However, the opening feels somewhat procedural, and there is less explicit affirmation of the client's inherent worth than would be needed for a perfect score.

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist uses accurate and therapeutically useful reflections of the client's ambivalence, pain, and conflicting motivations. Some reflections miss more specific emotions, such as relational anger, shame, fear, or guilt, preventing a top score.

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The session stays focused on suicidal urges, emotional overwhelm, and immediate safety, without drifting into unrelated topics. However, the opening is somewhat procedural, and the broader long-term direction is not fully connected to the immediate safety work.

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist uses collaborative language, follows the client's lead, and builds on client-generated ideas rather than imposing a standard safety plan. Some important agenda-setting and risk-clarification steps remain clinician-led, so collaboration is strong but not perfect.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist avoids commands and pressure while inviting the client to generate her own safety options. A few safety-related questions still carry a subtle expectation to cooperate, and explicit affirmation of the client's right to choose could strengthen the score.

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist skillfully elicits the client's own protective factors, motivations, and possible actions rather than supplying reasons to stay. Evocation is strong but does not fully deepen values, future orientation, confidence, or broader commitment.

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist maintains a generally collaborative stance and invites the client to contribute her perspective and pace. The partnership is strong, though some clinically necessary steps remain more clinician-led rather than fully co-developed with the client.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist effectively reflects and reinforces the client's own change talk and invites her to identify reasons and actions for staying safe. However, the work does not fully deepen change talk into stronger desire, ability, need, or commitment.

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist handles sustain talk well by avoiding argument and using double-sided reflections that acknowledge the client's pain while returning to reasons to stay. The final sustain talk remains strong and could be more actively softened by contrasting the consequences of suicide with relief without disappearing.
