# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

---

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist maintained a generally respectful, nonjudgmental, and supportive stance throughout the interaction. However, the acceptance was somewhat procedural and could have been warmer or more individualized.

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist demonstrated good reflective listening by accurately capturing the patient's practical concerns and frustrations. However, the empathy remained largely cognitive and logistical, missing an opportunity to reflect deeper emotional undercurrents such as irritation or fatigue.

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The session maintained an adequate focus on the patient's immediate presenting concern of getting the dental filling done. However, it lacked sustained strategic focus on the broader MI target behavior of smoking cessation.

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist behaved collaboratively by co-creating a session plan that visibly adopted the patient's shorter, less conversational style. However, the collaboration was mostly task-oriented and did not invite the patient to explore how his immediate goals might connect to a broader change process.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 5
**Comments:** This was a standout strength, with the psychiatrist consistently supporting the patient's sense of choice without any persuasive or coercive language. The psychiatrist respected his request for brevity, beautifully reinforcing his sense of control over his decisions and the session.

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The psychiatrist used evocative questions to reasonably draw out the patient's priorities regarding immediate dental logistics. However, the questions remained surface-level and were not consistently used to elicit deeper internal reasons, values, or confidence for smoking cessation.

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist maintained a strong partnership stance by frequently inviting the patient's own preferences and acknowledging his expertise on his own priorities. The main limitation was that the partnership remained mostly practical and task-focused rather than deeply collaborative around the larger smoking issue.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** There were only rare and weak efforts to evoke change talk relevant to smoking cessation. The psychiatrist missed opportunities to reflect or expand upon the patient's own acknowledgments of how smoking harms his mouth and causes staining.

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The psychiatrist appropriately tolerated and reflected the patient's sustain talk without arguing or increasing pressure. However, active MI strategies like double-sided reflections or reframing were not used to gently pivot the conversation toward change.
