# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

---

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist is respectful, nonjudgmental, and affirming, but can feel somewhat procedural and mildly pressuring when using words like “urgent” and “brave.”

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Reflections are accurate and perspective-taking, but remain more content-focused than emotionally deep. Deeper reflections of shame, self-criticism, or relapse fear would strengthen empathy.

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 5
**Comments:** The session stays strongly focused on the target behavior: reducing gambling-related financial risk through a specific, manageable first step.

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist co-constructs a practical plan with the client, though sometimes adds extra options after the client asks for simplicity.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 5
**Comments:** Autonomy is strongly supported through low-coercion language, clear choices, and respect for the client’s preference for a simple, low-stakes plan.

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Useful rulers and open questions are used, but the psychiatrist moves quickly into task planning without deeply eliciting internal motivation, values, or future vision.

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The client clearly shapes the session, but the psychiatrist still leads the structure and offers options rather than fully positioning the client as the expert.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The psychiatrist elicits concrete change talk and confidence, but does not consistently deepen or amplify the client’s own reasons for change.

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist avoids arguing or reinforcing sustain talk and effectively reframes concerns as overwhelm. More explicit double-sided reflections could further reduce ambivalence.
