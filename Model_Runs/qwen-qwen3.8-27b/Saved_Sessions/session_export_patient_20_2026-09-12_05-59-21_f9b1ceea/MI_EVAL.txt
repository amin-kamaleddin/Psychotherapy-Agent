# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

---

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist maintains a respectful, nonjudgmental stance without moralizing or shaming the client’s alcohol use. Acceptance is present but somewhat practical and transactional, not deeply affirming of the client’s vulnerability or fear of being labeled.

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The clinician offers accurate, practical reflections of the client’s preference for a low-drama, practical approach. However, empathy remains somewhat surface-level and does not fully address underlying emotions such as anxiety, guilt, or relationship strain.

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 5
**Comments:** The session stays strongly focused on the target behavior: alcohol use related to stress, unwinding, and weekend snapping. The psychiatrist keeps the conversation concrete, behavior-specific, and aligned with the referral concern.

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist incorporates the client’s preferences into the plan, creating a practical and collaborative working style. Collaboration is strong but not fully equal, because the clinician still drives much of the structure and proposed framework.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 5
**Comments:** The psychiatrist consistently supports the client’s sense of choice and control by offering options, avoiding pressure, and respecting informal or conditional follow-up. The client is positioned as the decision-maker throughout the session.

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** There is some effort to elicit the client’s own input and small commitments, but the exploration of internal motivations, values, and ambivalence is limited. The session moves into planning before deeply drawing out the client’s own reasons for change.

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The client’s contributions shape several details of the session and plan, showing some partnership. However, the psychiatrist remains the primary architect and does not fully position the client as the expert on the broader direction of change.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** The psychiatrist elicits some change-related language and willingness to try a small strategy, but does not consistently build on or amplify it. Change talk is treated more as logistical willingness than as motivation to be developed.

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** The clinician does not argue with or reinforce the client’s sustain talk, which is appropriate. However, the psychiatrist does not strategically soften or pivot sustain talk toward change, leaving ambivalent statements largely unaddressed.
