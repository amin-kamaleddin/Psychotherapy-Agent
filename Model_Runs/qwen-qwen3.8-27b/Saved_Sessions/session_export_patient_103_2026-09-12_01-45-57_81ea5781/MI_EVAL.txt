# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

---

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist consistently communicates respect, nonjudgment, and validation of the client’s frustration and need for fairness. The main limitation is that acceptance feels somewhat formulaic and reactive, with less affirmation of the client’s broader strengths, effort, or mixed feelings.

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The psychiatrist demonstrates adequate understanding of the client’s frustration, time pressure, and sense of being disrespected. Reflections remain mostly at the level of surface anger and do not consistently capture ambivalence, underlying hurt, fear of losing control, or the tension between cooperation and hostility.

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The session generally remains focused on the presenting concern of the appointment delay, job impact, and feeling disrespected. However, the direction is more incident-resolution than motivational interviewing, without a clear collaborative target such as communication under pressure, boundary setting, or handling time stress.

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** The psychiatrist uses some collaborative language, but supplies most of the content, options, and solutions. The client’s priorities and objections, such as not wanting scripts, emails, or role-play, do not meaningfully shape the conversation.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** The psychiatrist occasionally offers choices, but repeatedly provides scripts, templates, and instructions despite the client rejecting being managed. This directive approach undermines the client’s sense of ownership and reproduces the feeling of being controlled.

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** The psychiatrist asks a few open questions, but the session is dominated by advice, problem-solving, and action plans. There is insufficient use of evocative questions and reflections to draw out the client’s own motivations, values, and reasons for change.

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** The psychiatrist makes some superficial partnership moves, but the interaction quickly becomes expert-driven and directive. The psychiatrist functions more as a problem-solver than a partner who follows the client’s stated priorities and preferred next steps.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** The client offers language that could be expanded as change talk, but the psychiatrist does not systematically elicit, reflect, or amplify it. The conversation quickly moves into scripts, problem-solving, and action plans rather than building the client’s own change motivation.

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Sustain talk is present, including resistance to scripts, role-play, emails, and demands for confrontation. The psychiatrist often reinforces complaint, confrontation, or bureaucratic action rather than consistently reflecting and redirecting the client’s reasons against change.
