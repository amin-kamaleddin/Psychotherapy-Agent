# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

---

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist is respectful, validating, and nonjudgmental, including when the patient discloses prior attempts and current access to pills. However, repeated crisis-line warnings and conditional language make the acceptance feel partly procedural rather than fully individualized.

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** There are accurate reflections of pain and ambivalence, but empathy becomes inconsistent when the patient says she feels skipped over, tested, or treated as a problem. The psychiatrist does not consistently deepen reflections into underlying feelings, values, or the emotional meaning of safety questions.

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The session stays tightly focused on the acute concern, with clear movement from intake to safety assessment, means reduction, and short-term coping. The direction is strong clinically but is more safety-oriented than MI-directed, with limited connection to the patient’s own reasons for living or longer-term change goals.

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The psychiatrist offers some collaboration by asking what would help, offering to draft a message, and accepting the patient’s request for quiet music. However, most of the session is guided by the clinician’s safety agenda, so the patient’s preferences do not substantially shape the overall direction.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** There are moments of autonomy support, such as asking what would help and using permission-based language, but it is undermined by repeated directive safety questions, crisis instructions, and requests for promises not to use pills. The psychiatrist does not strongly affirm the patient’s ownership of choices or capacity to choose safety in her own way.

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The psychiatrist elicits some internal resources and change talk, especially around the cat, sister, and desire for the pain to stop rather than herself. However, evocation remains limited and is often interrupted by clinician-provided strategies, safety checks, and crisis directives.

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The psychiatrist communicates presence and invites some input, creating a sense of staying together with the patient. Nevertheless, the interaction remains substantially clinician-led through repeated safety questions, grounding instructions, and conditional escalation language.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Meaningful change talk is elicited, such as staying for the cat, concern for her sister, and wanting the pain to stop rather than herself. The psychiatrist rarely amplifies or strategically reflects this change talk to strengthen the patient’s sense of agency.

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The psychiatrist does not argue with the patient’s hopelessness or reinforce her sustain talk, and instead redirects to present-focused safety and concrete actions. Some softening is present, but the use of double-sided reflections is limited, and repeated focus on pills and promises keeps attention on risk rather than ambivalence.
