# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

---

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist maintained a respectful, nonjudgmental tone with supportive statements such as 'I hear you' and 'You're doing a lot.' Warmth was occasionally reduced by a quick shift toward practical next steps.

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The psychiatrist offered adequate reflections of the client's surface-level emotions, such as stress and feeling overwhelmed. Deeper empathic exploration was limited because reflections were often followed quickly by problem-solving.

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The session had clear focus and stayed centered on a specific target behavior: smoking in the car during the school run. The conversation moved logically from general smoking reduction to a narrow, actionable plan.

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The psychiatrist and client reached a joint agreement, such as keeping the lighter at home and storing biscuits in the pocket. However, collaboration was uneven because the psychiatrist generated most of the suggested options while the client selected from them.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Although the psychiatrist invited the client to choose, the choices were largely framed by the psychiatrist's recommendations. The client expressed resistance to 'homework,' 'programs,' and reminders, but continued to receive structured, clinician-led options.

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** The psychiatrist rarely elicited the client's own reasons for change, coping ideas, or internal motivations. Instead, the clinician provided multiple suggestions and asked the client to pick one, which limited self-generated change talk.

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** The psychiatrist adopted an expert-driven stance by offering the main solutions rather than exploring the client's existing resources or preferred strategies. This weakened the MI principle that the client is the expert on their own life and coping.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The psychiatrist acknowledged some change talk, especially around protecting the son in the car. However, these moments were not consistently deepened into stronger motivational statements or values-based reasons for change.

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The psychiatrist accepted sustain talk, such as relying on willpower or minimizing the severity of the problem, without arguing. The response remained more neutral than transformative, with limited use of double-sided reflection or redirection to resolve ambivalence.
