# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

---

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist is respectful and nonjudgmental, repeatedly affirming the client’s choices and normalizing her concern about seeming rude. Some statements, such as “You’re doing the right thing,” are mildly evaluative, preventing a top score.

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The psychiatrist offers accurate content reflections about the client’s practical goals and low-key approach. However, deeper emotions and ambivalence, such as fear of being seen as “the weird one” or wanting to fit in, are not fully reflected.

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The session stays focused on navigating alcohol-related social situations while staying lower-risk. The main limitation is repetitive tactical focus rather than consistently building toward broader motivation or a collaboratively chosen next step.

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The psychiatrist adapts to the client’s low-key style and incorporates her preferences, such as avoiding role-play and using the snack redirect. However, the counselor often leads by supplying multiple scripted options, so the plan is less fully co-constructed.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist repeatedly emphasizes the client’s choice and offers tactics as options rather than commands. Some recommendations are directive, but they are generally framed in a supportive, client-driven way.

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The psychiatrist asks helpful questions to elicit preferences and mild commitment, such as what feels most doable. The evocation remains superficial, missing deeper exploration of the client’s values, confidence, and meaning behind staying lower-risk.

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The psychiatrist invites client input and respects her boundaries around role-play and scripted exits. Nonetheless, the session becomes counselor-led, with the psychiatrist serving more as problem-solver than equal partner.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** The client offers some change-relevant language, but the psychiatrist does not systematically elicit, amplify, or summarize the client’s own reasons and confidence for change. The session relies more on external tactics than internal motivation.

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The psychiatrist responds to sustain talk with normalization and redirection rather than argument or judgment. However, there is limited use of double-sided reflections or deeper exploration of the conflict between fitting in and maintaining lower-risk choices.
