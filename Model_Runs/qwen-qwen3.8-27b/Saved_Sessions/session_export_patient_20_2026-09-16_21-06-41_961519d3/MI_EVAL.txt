# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

---

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist maintains a respectful, nonjudgmental stance and validates the client’s perspective without shaming or challenging minimization. It falls short of a 5 because the acceptance is somewhat task-focused and does not consistently deepen into explicit, individualized positive regard beyond the behavior being discussed.

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** There are strong reflections of the client’s ambivalence, values, and practical concerns, including use of the client’s own “red light” metaphor. A key miss is not exploring the client’s stated discomfort that “something feels a bit off,” and some reflections are more interpretive than emotionally attuned.

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 5
**Comments:** The psychiatrist maintains a clear, steady focus on alcohol use and guides the conversation from vague ambivalence to a concrete, manageable plan. The session avoids topic drift and effectively connects discussion to alcohol-related consequences and a doable next step.

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist builds the plan around the client’s own ideas, such as focusing on Wednesday, avoiding the six-pack, and using a phone reminder. It does not fully reach a 5 because the collaboration could be more explicit about shared decision-making and the client’s authority in shaping the agenda.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist consistently supports choice by asking what would feel possible and by respecting the client’s “not quitting” boundary. Some statements are slightly evaluative or guiding, such as “right automatic cue” and “clear compass,” which could more fully emphasize the client’s complete control.

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist uses open-ended questions that elicit the client’s practical preferences and self-generated solutions, and reflects key values like avoiding the Wednesday hangover. The evocation could be stronger by more consistently drawing out deeper change talk, confidence, and personal reasons for change.

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist shows a collaborative stance by inviting the client’s view and allowing the client’s ideas to drive the session. Some therapist-driven or evaluative language prevents a perfect score, as the client could be more explicitly positioned as the expert steering the conversation.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The psychiatrist elicits some change talk around the client’s desire to feel more like themselves and get through the week without being a mess. However, the session moves quickly into practical problem-solving and does not consistently develop or amplify deeper importance, confidence, ability, or future-focused change talk.

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist does not challenge the client’s sustain talk and uses ambivalence-aware framing that softens self-judgment. It could reach a 5 by more consistently using explicit double-sided reflections that name the pull of the status quo while turning toward change.
