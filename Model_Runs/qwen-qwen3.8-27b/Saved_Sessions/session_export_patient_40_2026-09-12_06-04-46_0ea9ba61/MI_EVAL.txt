# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

---

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist maintains a respectful, nonjudgmental, and generally supportive tone without moralizing relapse or pill use. The main limitation is that some affirmations are slightly generic and the opening summary may frame the client as more decided than he is at that moment.

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The psychiatrist demonstrates adequate reflective listening by tracking key themes such as concern for his children, shame, and fear of relapse. However, empathy is often brief and quickly shifts into planning, with missed opportunities to deepen understanding of fear, low confidence, or shame.

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The session maintains a clear and consistent focus on the target behavior, including alcohol use, pill use, and avoiding the Friday bar. The main limitation is that the discussion becomes quite procedural and action-oriented, potentially narrowing the process from motivation-building to task completion.

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist behaves largely as an equal partner by building on the client's ideas and following his lead in shaping a practical plan. However, the counselor still provides a substantial amount of content and structure, which can reduce the sense that the session is being shaped primarily by the client's own expertise.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist regularly supports choice and avoids pressure through invitations such as “which feels doable” and “pick one or tweak it.” The limitation is that autonomy support is not consistently maximal, especially when the counselor supplies many concrete options and scripts.

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The psychiatrist uses evocative questions to identify what matters most and a small realistic step, eliciting some client-generated motivation. The limitation is that the counselor often moves to options or scripts before fully drawing out and expanding the client's own change talk.

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The psychiatrist shows a collaborative stance by inviting input and allowing the client to select the text, backup plan, and practical supports. However, the partnership is uneven because the counselor frequently supplies structure, scripts, and options, which can feel less like co-creation and more like external planning.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The psychiatrist effectively asks what matters most and invites a small, realistic step, eliciting some client-generated reasons and commitment. The limitation is that change talk is not consistently amplified, with limited follow-up questions to explore the client's reasons, ability, commitment, or confidence.

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The psychiatrist handles sustain talk safely by normalizing ambivalence rather than arguing or reinforcing resistance. However, the counselor rarely uses more advanced MI strategies, such as double-sided reflections or evocative follow-ups, to help the client resolve the meaning of his ambivalence.
