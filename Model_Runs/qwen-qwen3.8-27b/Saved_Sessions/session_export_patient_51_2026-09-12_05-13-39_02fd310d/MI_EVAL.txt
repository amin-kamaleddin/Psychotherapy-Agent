# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

---

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist maintained a respectful, nonjudgmental, and supportive tone with affirmations that conveyed positive regard. Acceptance was somewhat procedural and repetitive, especially with repeated safety warnings and offers that felt formulaic.

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The psychiatrist made appropriate reflections, such as acknowledging surprise and the client's need to leave. However, empathy remained surface-level and did not fully explore the emotional texture of the client's rushed or anxious presentation.

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The session stayed focused on the medication safety concern and the need to confirm washout timing with the prescriber. Direction became inefficient near the end, with repeated offers of help and circular reiteration despite the client's clear urgency to leave.

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The psychiatrist used some collaborative language and invited client input at the beginning. However, after delivering the medical information, the interaction became more clinician-directed, with repeated offers and reminders that limited full co-construction.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist generally supported autonomy by avoiding pressure to make an immediate medication decision and framing offers as optional. Repeated re-engagement after the client signaled urgency slightly weakened the autonomy support.

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** The psychiatrist provided substantial clinical information about the contraindication, serotonin syndrome risk, and washout period. There was limited evocative MI practice, with few questions to draw out the client's own motivations, values, confidence, or deeper change talk.

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Partnership was present in the opening questions and offers of choice. After the initial information exchange, the psychiatrist became more directive, especially through repeated scripts and safety reminders, reducing a balanced power-sharing dynamic.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** The client offered change talk about avoiding unsafe interaction and planning to call the prescriber, and the psychiatrist affirmed these statements. The psychiatrist did not actively cultivate deeper change talk through questions about reasons, values, confidence, or commitment.

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The psychiatrist did not argue with the client's time pressure and sometimes acknowledged the need to leave. However, repeated re-offers of help after clear closure signals made the softening less strategic and risked prolonging the interaction.
