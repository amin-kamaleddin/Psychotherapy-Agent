# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

---

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The psychiatrist is generally respectful, nonjudgmental, and supportive, helping create a safe session. However, the acceptance is somewhat generic and occasionally reads as evaluative rather than deeply affirming.

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The counselor captures surface-level ambivalence and validates concerns about medication side effects. Empathy is limited in depth because stronger emotional and relational themes, such as staying active with his wife and not slowing her down, are not fully reflected.

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The session maintains a clear, consistent focus on cholesterol management, medication adherence, and walking as an actionable step. Direction is strong, though it leans somewhat prescriptive and task-oriented rather than fully client-led.

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** There is some collaboration, as the psychiatrist invites input and adapts to the patient's preferences for simpler questions and no role-playing. However, much of the plan is counselor-generated, so the collaboration feels uneven and not fully MI-consistent.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The psychiatrist offers choices and respects the patient's boundaries, which supports autonomy in a moderate way. More directive guidance, such as specific days, alarms, and strict monitoring instructions, can make the plan feel prescribed rather than self-chosen.

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The counselor uses some evocative prompts early on and elicits part of the patient's motivation to stay active. Overall, the approach is more informative than evocative, and the patient's deeper values-based reasons are not consistently drawn out and expanded.

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The psychiatrist asks for the patient's input and adjusts to his preferences, showing partial partnership. The balance remains uneven because the counselor frequently takes on a medical-adviser role rather than fully positioning the patient as the expert in his own life.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The patient produces meaningful change talk, including willingness to take the medicine and keep hiking. The counselor does not consistently reflect, amplify, or develop this change talk, missing opportunities to strengthen the patient's own motivation.

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The psychiatrist normalizes hesitation and redirects the patient toward action, which helps avoid reinforcing resistance. The responses would be stronger if they more consistently used double-sided reflections or strategic pivots to turn sustain talk toward change.
