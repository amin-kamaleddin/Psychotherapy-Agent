# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

---

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist maintained a nonjudgmental, respectful tone and normalized the patient's self-critical concerns about laziness and failure. Acceptance was strong but sometimes brief and quickly shifted into practical problem-solving, which limited deeper emotional attunement.

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist used accurate reflective listening and recognized barriers such as fatigue, meetings, and embarrassment. Empathy was generally effective, but it did not always stay long enough with the patient's underlying ambivalence and emotional meaning.

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The session stayed focused on increasing physical activity and moved clearly toward concrete implementation. Direction was clear, but it became somewhat logistics-heavy and did not consistently connect the plan back to the patient's values or reasons for change.

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The psychiatrist offered options and incorporated the patient's preferences, such as shifting from walking to desk stretches. However, the psychiatrist remained the primary source of ideas and plan structure rather than fully co-creating solutions with the patient.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The psychiatrist used permission-seeking language and offered choices, which supported autonomy. However, the repeated delivery of specific plans, scripts, and implementation steps made the session more directive than fully autonomy-supportive.

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Most questions were logistical rather than motivational, and the psychiatrist did not consistently elicit the patient's values, importance, confidence, or personal reasons for change. Evocation was limited by the amount of clinician-generated advice and planning.

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The psychiatrist invited input and allowed some patient choice, which showed partial partnership. The overall stance remained expert-driven, with the psychiatrist guiding the structure and solutions more than the patient.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The patient produced some change-relevant statements, and the psychiatrist asked for commitment-related choices. Change talk was not consistently reinforced, expanded, or connected to the patient's deeper motivations.

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The psychiatrist responded to barriers with normalizing and reframing statements without arguing. However, the conversation often stayed focused on the barriers themselves rather than consistently redirecting toward the patient's reasons and solutions for change.
