# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

---

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist is respectful, nonjudgmental, and validating without pressuring the client to quit. Warmth is generally supportive but could be more emotionally attuned during moments of self-doubt or fear of failure.

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** There are accurate reflections of the client’s ambivalence and the function of drinking, but the psychiatrist often moves to planning before fully exploring underlying loneliness, self-doubt, or fear of failure. Empathy is adequate but tends to remain surface-level.

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The session stays focused on alcohol reduction and develops a clear, concrete experiment around an alcohol-free evening. However, multiple questions and plan steps are sometimes bundled together, making the interaction feel somewhat agenda-driven.

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist invites client input and co-creates a realistic plan based on his preferences. The collaboration is strong but can become therapist-led when several options, scripts, and backups are offered before the client has fully explored his own ideas.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist supports choice by framing the goal as an optional experiment and asking the client to select preferences. There is still occasional therapist-driven planning, especially when specific actions are suggested before fully waiting for the client to lead.

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist effectively draws out the client’s own motivations, values, and confidence using rulers and exploratory questions. The main limitation is sometimes supplying meaning or concrete steps before the client has fully elaborated his own change talk.

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The client’s preferences clearly shape the session and the resulting plan. The partnership could be stronger by more consistently acknowledging the client as the expert and reducing the therapist’s menu of suggested solutions.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist frequently creates opportunities for the client to express desire, ability, and values related to change. When strong change talk appears, it could be deepened and amplified more before shifting to planning details.

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The psychiatrist does not reinforce sustain talk and helps reduce resistance by reframing the goal as small and optional. However, sustain talk and fear of failure are not consistently redirected or softened with deeper reflections or strategic reframing.
