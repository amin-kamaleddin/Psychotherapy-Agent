# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

---

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist is warm, respectful, and nonjudgmental, clearly affirming the client’s caution and safety-focused behavior. Some reflections add interpretations that feel slightly leading, preventing a top score.

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** There is strong reflective listening around the client’s time pressure and need for a quick safety check. Empathy is solid but not always deeply exploratory, and some reflections introduce meanings beyond the client’s stated words.

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 5
**Comments:** The session remains tightly focused on the immediate medication-safety question and a concrete next step. There is minimal drift, and the direction effectively guides the client toward a safe, actionable decision.

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The early interaction is collaborative, especially in co-constructing a brief message to the prescriber. However, repeated closure and reflections after the client clearly signals departure reduce the sense of equal partnership.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** There are good autonomy-supportive moments, such as asking permission before helping draft a message and positioning the client as the final decision-maker. The psychiatrist continues speaking after clear cues that the client must leave, which partially undermines autonomy.

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The client is asked to identify helpful next steps and produces a practical safety plan. Evocation is present but limited, with much of the session driven by clinician reassurance and interpretation rather than deeper client-generated motivation.

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist uses collaborative language and invites client input, avoiding a purely expert-driven stance. The interaction is still clinician-led in structure, with limited explicit acknowledgment of the client as the ultimate expert on her priorities.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The psychiatrist names and affirms change talk, such as checking before taking medication and preparing a message to the doctor. Deeper DARN-C evocation is limited, so the client’s own change language is not expanded as fully as possible.

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The client’s main sustain talk—needing brevity and a practical answer—is respected rather than challenged. The psychiatrist effectively redirects this constraint into a practical safety step, though strategic reframing is not explicit.
