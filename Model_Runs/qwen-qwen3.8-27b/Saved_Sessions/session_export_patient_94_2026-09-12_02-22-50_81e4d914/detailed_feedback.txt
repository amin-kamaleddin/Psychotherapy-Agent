                                Motivational Interviewing Feedback Criteria (5-Point Likert Scale)

Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)
------------------------------------
1. Acceptance: Extent to which the counselor communicates unconditional positive regard for the client.
   1 = Judgmental, disapproving, labeling
   2 = Inconsistent respect, occasional judgment
   3 = Neutral stance, basic respect
   4 = Generally respectful and supportive
   5 = Consistently respectful, warm, supportive

2. Empathy: Extent to which the counselor understands and/or makes an effort to accurately understand the client’s perspective.
   1 = Little effort to understand, minimal reflective listening
   2 = Limited perspective-taking, mostly factual focus
   3 = Adequate but inconsistent effort to understand
   4 = Good reflective listening and perspective-taking
   5 = Highly accurate, consistent understanding and reflections

3. Direction – Degree to which the counselor maintains appropriate focus on a specific target behavior or concerns directly tied to it.
1 = No clear focus; session wanders aimlessly
2 = Minimal focus; frequent topic drift
3 = Occasional focus on target behavior but inconsistent
4 = Generally keeps discussion directed toward target behavior
5 = Consistently maintains clear focus and guides toward change

---

4. Collaboration – Extent to which the clinician behaves as if the interview is occurring between two equal partners, both of whom have knowledge that might be useful in the problem under consideration.
1 = Authoritarian or expert-driven stance
2 = Limited collaboration; frequent direction by counselor
3 = Partial collaboration; uneven power-sharing
4 = Strong collaborative stance most of the time
5 = Fully collaborative; client’s ideas shape session direction

---

5. Autonomy Support – Extent to which clinician supports and actively fosters client perception of choice as opposed to attempting to control the client’s behavior or choices.
1 = Directive or pressuring; minimizes choice
2 = Occasionally acknowledges choice but inconsistently
3 = Neutral regarding client autonomy
4 = Regularly affirms client freedom to choose
5 = Consistently reinforces client ownership of change process

---

6. Evocation – Extent to which the clinician conveys an understanding that motivation for change, and the ability to move toward that change, reside mostly within the client and therefore focuses efforts to elicit and expand it within the therapeutic interaction.
1 = Provides reasons for change without exploring client view
2 = Limited interest in client motivations
3 = Some evocation but superficial
4 = Frequently draws out client’s own reasons for change
5 = Consistently elicits and develops client change talk throughout session

---

7. Partnership – Extent to which the clinician conveys an understanding that expertise and wisdom about change reside mostly within the client

1 = Clinician actively assumes the expert role for the majority of the interaction with the client. Collaboration or partnership is absent.
2 = Clinician superficially responds to opportunities to collaborate.
3 = Clinician incorporates client’s contributions but does so in a lukewarm or erratic fashion.
4 = Clinician fosters collaboration and power sharing so that client’s contributions impact the session in ways that they otherwise would not.
5 = Clinician actively fosters and encourages power sharing in the interaction in such a way that client’s contributions substantially influence the nature of the session.

---

8. Cultivating Change Talk – Extent to which the clinician actively encourages the client’s own language in favor of the change goal, and confidence for making that change.
1 = No effort to evoke change talk
2 = Rare or weak efforts
3 = Occasional evocation of change language
4 = Frequent evocation of change talk
5 = Consistent, skillful evocation throughout

---

9. Softening Sustain Talk – Extent that the clinician avoids a focus on the reasons against changing or for maintaining the status quo.
1 = Reinforces sustain talk
2 = Rarely shifts focus away from sustain talk
3 = Occasionally redirects toward change
4 = Often redirects effectively
5 = Consistently pivots client language toward change

Evaluation Guidance

- All domains are rated on a 1–5 Likert scale.
- Ratings should reflect overall quality and consistency of behavior.
- Focus on both counselor skillfulness and client speech as indicators of MI process quality.

 # Motivational Interviewing Feedback: Psychiatrist Evaluation

## Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)

### 1. Acceptance
Score: 3/5

Explanation:
The counselor generally avoids judgment, labeling, or overt disagreement. They repeatedly say things like “I respect that,” “I respect your choice,” “You’re in charge,” and apologize for repeating themselves, which supports a nonjudgmental stance. However, the overall acceptance is limited by a somewhat paternalistic undercurrent. Early on, the counselor gives direct medical advice (“Don’t stop suddenly—talk with your doctor”) and then repeatedly inserts safety reminders after the client repeatedly states she is leaving and has heard the safety information. Even if well intended, this can feel less like unconditional positive regard and more like the counselor does not fully trust the client’s judgment. There is also limited explicit affirmation of the client’s experience as a person, beyond surface-level respect for her choices.

### 2. Empathy
Score: 3/5

Explanation:
The counselor makes a consistent but somewhat surface-level effort to reflect the client’s perspective. Examples include reflecting that she feels pressured, is worried about long-term harms, wants a quick answer, prefers using the inhaler only when needed, and knows her own body. These reflections are often accurate at the cognitive level and help keep the session from becoming argumentative. However, the empathy frequently misses deeper emotional and relational themes. The client expresses frustration with being told what to do, a need for autonomy, and a sense of being treated as not competent (“I’m not a child”). The counselor does not adequately reflect the likely feeling of being controlled, disrespected, or medically overridden. There is also a missed opportunity to reflect the ambivalence underlying her behavior: she is skeptical and resistant, yet she researched inhalers and still attended the visit. The reflections are also repetitive and do not progressively deepen understanding.

---

### 3. Cultivating Change Talk
Score: 2/5

Explanation:
There are only minimal and weak attempts to cultivate change talk. The opening question (“What’s been on your mind lately, and what would you most like to talk about or change right now?”) is an appropriate MI-style invitation, but the counselor quickly shifts into providing medical information and scripts rather than exploring the client’s own motivations. The counselor does not use the client’s implicit change talk, such as the fact that she researched inhalers, came to the appointment despite her resistance, says she will come back if things get worse, or knows when to go to the ER. There is no follow-up that would help her articulate her own reasons, needs, or desires for managing asthma. Missed opportunities include asking what she wants to protect, what asthma symptoms worry her most, what a good day managing asthma would look like, or what would make using treatment feel more acceptable. The counselor rarely reflects, amplifies, or builds on statements that could move the client toward change.

---

### 4. Softening Sustain Talk
Score: 2/5

Explanation:
The client’s sustain talk is strong: she does not want daily chemicals, distrusts long-term side effects, prefers using the inhaler only when wheezing, and is resistant to changing her routine. The counselor does not become confrontational and generally avoids direct arguing, which is positive. However, the early response to her concerns is somewhat corrective: “benefits usually outweigh risks,” plus a list of side effects and risk-reduction strategies. This can be experienced as the righting reflex and may strengthen rather than soften her sustain talk. Later, the counselor accepts her position but does not use double-sided reflections, normalize her concerns while gently exploring the other side, or redirect in a way that helps her resolve ambivalence. The repeated safety reminders and offers of scripts for talking with the clinician keep the focus on clinical adherence and risk rather than helping the client turn her own concerns into motivation for a workable plan. There is little strategic softening of sustain talk.

### 5. Partnership
Score: 2/5

Explanation:
There is some language suggesting partnership, such as “You choose the focus” and offering options like a one-line script or signing off. However, the overall stance is more expert-driven than partnership-driven. The counselor quickly provides medical explanations, safety guidance, and scripts for what the client might say to her doctor. The client’s own expertise—her research, her lived experience with her body, her stated “own system”—is not actively used to shape the session. The counselor does not ask what the client’s system looks like, what she has already tried, what she thinks works, or what would need to be true for her to consider changing. The session is shaped more by the counselor’s clinical agenda than by a true co-created exploration. The power balance is uneven, with the counselor supplying information and the client largely rejecting it.

### 6. Direction
Score: 4/5

Explanation:
The session remains largely focused on the target behavior: inhaler use and asthma management. The counselor does not drift into unrelated topics and keeps returning to practical concerns about using the inhaler, safety signs, and communication with the clinician. This consistency is a strength. However, the direction is not strategically MI-aligned. The counselor repeatedly returns to safety reminders and script suggestions without creating momentum toward change. Because the client is precontemplative and resistant, a more skillful direction would have been to narrow the agenda with the client, perhaps to a small, acceptable step, and then gently explore the meaning and implications of her current plan. Instead, the session becomes repetitive around safety and compliance. It is focused on the target behavior, but it does not guide the client toward change effectively, so it is strong but not excellent.

### 7. Autonomy Support
Score: 4/5

Explanation:
The counselor frequently supports the client’s autonomy with explicit language such as “You choose the focus,” “I respect that,” “That’s your choice,” “You’re in charge,” and “I won’t keep you.” These statements are important, especially with a resistant client, and help reduce coercion. These repeated cautions can undermine the message that she is truly in charge. ### 8. Collaboration
Score: 2/5

Explanation:
The interaction is not very collaborative. Although the counselor uses some collaborative-sounding phrases, the actual process is largely counselor-driven. The counselor remains in the role of medical advisor, while the client remains resistant and time-pressed.

---

### 9. Evocation
Score: 2/5

Explanation:
Evocation is limited. The counselor asks an open question at the start and asks a factual question about which inhaler she uses, but most of the session consists of providing information, safety guidance, and suggested scripts rather than eliciting the client’s own motivations. There is little use of MI techniques to draw out the client’s values, concerns, hopes, or confidence. The counselor does not explore why she researched inhalers, what she has observed about her asthma, what she is afraid of, what would make treatment feel safer or more acceptable, or how she would evaluate whether her current plan is working. The client’s implicit motivation—she is worried enough about side effects and serious symptoms to mention them and attend the visit—is not effectively evoked. A stronger session would have used open questions, reflections, and strategic follow-ups to help the client articulate her own reasons for managing asthma in a way she could live with.

---

**Overall Global Ratings Score:** **2.7/5**

The counselor maintains a generally respectful, nonjudgmental stance and keeps the focus on asthma and inhaler use, but the session is too informational and insufficiently evocative. The counselor’s repeated safety reminders and early medical advice can feel paternalistic and may increase resistance. The strongest areas are autonomy support and target-behavior focus; the weakest areas are evocation, cultivating change talk, softening sustain talk, collaboration, and partnership. To improve, the counselor should slow down, ask permission before giving information, use the client’s own concerns and research as starting points, reflect the emotional undercurrent of frustration and autonomy, and avoid repeated cautions once the client has stated she is leaving.
