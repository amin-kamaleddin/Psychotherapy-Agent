# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

---

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist maintains a respectful, nonjudgmental stance with several affirmations and validations of the client's mixed feelings. Acceptance is generally strong, though it sometimes feels procedural and does not consistently deepen into more individualized positive regard.

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** There is good reflective listening and accurate tracking of the client's ambivalence, guilt, and concern about being the 'bad guy.' Empathy is strong but often remains more content-focused, without fully exploring the deeper emotional loss or identity conflict.

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 5
**Comments:** The session stays tightly focused on the target behavior of setting a firm boundary for the flatmate to move out. Each turn moves the conversation toward concrete action, from identifying the goal to scripting, selecting a date, and rehearsing responses.

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist demonstrates a strong collaborative stance by co-creating scripts, inviting feedback, and adapting to the client's preferences. The collaboration is not fully equal, however, because the psychiatrist supplies much of the content and structure.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist supports autonomy by asking for preferences, offering choices, and emphasizing ownership of the decision and living space. The prescriptive script-heavy approach slightly limits the perception that the client is fully directing the process.

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The psychiatrist uses evocative techniques such as rulers, outcome questions, and confidence-focused prompts. However, the session often shifts quickly into scripting and practical planning, limiting deeper exploration of the client's internal motivation and values.

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The psychiatrist invites client input and uses some collaborative questions, but frequently assumes an expert role by providing complete scripts and tactical instructions. The interaction feels more coach-like than a true partnership in which the client's expertise is prioritized.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The psychiatrist elicits and reinforces some clear change talk, including the client's desire to be direct and stop being a backup plan. However, the session moves too quickly into scripting and rehearsal, reducing opportunities to deepen and expand the client's own motivation.

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Sustain talk such as guilt, fear of being cruel, and concern about damaging the friendship is acknowledged with balanced reflections and normalizing statements. The psychiatrist does not consistently redirect or soften this sustain talk toward change, often moving to scripts or planning instead.
