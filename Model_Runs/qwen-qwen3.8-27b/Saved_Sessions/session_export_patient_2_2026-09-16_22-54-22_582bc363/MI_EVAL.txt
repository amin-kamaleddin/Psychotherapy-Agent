# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

---

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist maintains a respectful, nonjudgmental, and supportive tone while reducing shame and honoring Aisha’s ambivalence. It falls short of a 5 because the warmth is sometimes slightly formulaic and does not explicitly state unconditional acceptance.

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist demonstrates strong perspective-taking, especially in reflecting Aisha’s central ambivalence and the function of smoking as a “buffer.” It does not reach a 5 because some prompts are relatively generic and the later disclosures about restlessness, tension, and loneliness are not as deeply reflected.

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 5
**Comments:** The session maintains a clear and consistent focus on reducing evening smoking, moving steadily from general readiness to a concrete plan for the fourth cigarette. The psychiatrist effectively keeps the session on target without drifting into vague emotional exploration.

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist builds collaboratively on Aisha’s own concerns, language, and ideas, making her the primary driver of the session content. It is not a 5 because there is less explicit joint agenda-setting or shared goal negotiation, and the clinician sometimes leads with a specific behavioral experiment.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist strongly supports Aisha’s sense of choice by honoring her preference for reduction rather than abstinence and avoiding pressure to make promises. It does not reach a 5 because the clinician could more explicitly reinforce her decision-making authority at moments of doubt.

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist consistently draws motivation from within Aisha through targeted open questions that elicit her own values, concerns, and ideas for change. It falls short of a 5 because the evoked change talk is not consistently developed into stronger confidence or commitment language.

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist conveys that Aisha is the expert on her own life by inviting her to lead the content and following her concerns rather than imposing a standard cessation script. It is not a 5 because this partnership is mostly implicit rather than explicitly negotiated as a shared process.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist frequently evokes useful change talk through open questions and affirmations, helping Aisha articulate concrete confidence statements. It does not reach a 5 because the clinician could more precisely label and consolidate Aisha’s emerging self-efficacy.

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The psychiatrist avoids arguing with or reinforcing Aisha’s minimization, but does not explicitly soften or pivot the sustain talk. Stronger MI responses would use double-sided reflections or value-based questions when she compares herself to coworkers or says she is “not fully sold.”
