# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

---

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The psychiatrist was generally respectful, nonjudgmental, and validating, using phrases such as “That makes sense” and “We can go at your pace.” However, the warmth was more functional than deeply accepting, and repeated medical warnings/suggestions made the tone somewhat instructive.

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The psychiatrist made reasonable efforts to reflect Thomas’s perspective, confusion, and reluctance to escalate the issue. The reflections were largely surface-level and missed deeper values and emotions, such as his desire to unwind, social bonding, and being a responsible parent.

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The session generally stayed relevant to smoking history, alcohol use, liver enzymes, and lung scarring. However, direction was not strategically MI-focused, repeatedly introducing medical information, monitoring options, and risk warnings rather than progressively exploring ambivalence and readiness.

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** There was partial collaboration, especially when the psychiatrist built a plan around Thomas choosing option 3, monitor and retest. However, the interaction remained largely expert-driven, with the psychiatrist supplying most recommendations and problem-solving ideas rather than fully co-creating the plan with the client.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The psychiatrist used several autonomy-supportive statements, such as “Which would you prefer?” and “Trust your judgment.” Autonomy support was weakened because the psychiatrist continued offering reminders, scripts, coping strategies, and harm-reduction ideas after Thomas repeatedly said he wanted to keep it short and handle it himself.

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Some evocative questions were used, such as asking what Thomas would like to focus on and how he felt about the results. However, the dominant style was informative and corrective, with little effort to elicit Thomas’s own motives, values, concerns, or possible reasons for change.

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The psychiatrist showed some partnership by inviting choices and asking for preferences. However, the interaction remained mostly directive, with Thomas largely accepting or declining the psychiatrist’s medical interpretations, monitoring plans, and suggested options.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** The psychiatrist made a few attempts to invite change-related language, such as using a 0–10 importance scale and asking what “social” drinking looks like. These attempts were not skillfully developed, especially when Thomas resisted the scaling question without further gentle evocation of his own reasons, values, or concerns.

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** The psychiatrist acknowledged Thomas’s sustain talk but often validated the status quo in ways that reinforced maintaining current behavior. Instead of using double-sided reflections or ambivalence-resolution techniques, the psychiatrist repeatedly returned to medical risk language, which may have increased defensiveness rather than softening resistance.
