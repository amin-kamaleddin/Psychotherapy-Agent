# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

---

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The psychiatrist is respectful and nonjudgmental, using supportive statements such as thanks and approval of the monitoring plan. However, warmth remains task-focused and does not consistently communicate unconditional positive regard for the client as a person.

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** There is a brief empathic acknowledgment that the blood pressure news can feel unsettling, but overall perspective-taking is limited. The psychiatrist does not deeply reflect the client's possible worry, minimization, or ambivalence about alcohol and health.

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The session maintains a clear focus on blood pressure, home monitoring, and follow-up steps, so it does not wander. However, direction is more logistical and medical than MI-focused, with limited strategic movement toward the alcohol-reduction component.

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** The psychiatrist uses some collaborative language and invites the client's preference, but the interaction remains largely provider-led. The plan is built around the psychiatrist's pre-formed protocol rather than the client's ideas and priorities.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The psychiatrist supports choice by asking what would be helpful and accepting the client's decision to focus on logging for now. However, frequent medical directives and recommendations limit consistent reinforcement of the client's autonomy and ownership.

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** There are a few open-ended and preference-based questions, but the session is dominated by the psychiatrist supplying information and instructions. The client's own motivations, values, and reasons for change are not meaningfully elicited.

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Some collaborative framing is present, such as “we can” and asking what would be most helpful, but the power balance remains tilted toward the psychiatrist. The client's expertise about his own life is not consistently positioned as central to goal-setting.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** The client agrees to keep a log and bring it to his next check-up, which is a weak form of change talk. The psychiatrist does not actively amplify or expand this change talk using reflections, importance/confidence questions, or DARN-C strategies.

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The psychiatrist does not challenge the client's denial of stress or minimization of alcohol use, which is consistent with avoiding argument. However, there is little active softening, such as double-sided reflections or gentle exploration of ambivalence around alcohol.
