# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

---

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The psychiatrist uses a respectful, nonjudgmental tone and asks a direct safety question without shame. However, acceptance feels somewhat formulaic and repetitive, and the frequent shift to practical options reduces sustained warmth and affirmation.

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The psychiatrist offers several accurate reflections of Amira’s shame, helplessness, and feeling alone. The main limitation is that the reflections remain repetitive and do not fully engage deeper beliefs, such as fear of being seen as falling apart or the sense that no one really cares.

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The session generally remains connected to drug use, work consequences, and safety, but the direction is inconsistent. It shifts among stopping/cutting down, harm reduction, withdrawal screening, resources, message drafting, and safety planning, which creates a sense of circling rather than purposeful MI movement.

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** The psychiatrist uses some collaborative language and asks permission to offer options. In practice, the interaction remains counselor-led, with the psychiatrist driving the agenda through practical solutions and message drafting rather than genuine collaboration.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** There are some autonomy-supportive statements, but the psychiatrist repeatedly presents options and asks for location details after the client expresses discomfort. This can feel pressuring and undermines the sense that Amira is fully in charge of her choices.

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** The session begins with an open question, but it quickly relies on closed menus and practical advice. There are few evocative questions about Amira’s values, hopes, strengths, or reasons for change, so her own change talk is not sufficiently developed.

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** The psychiatrist includes some partnership language, such as working on the next step together. However, the counselor repeatedly offers menus of options and drafts a message, functioning more as an expert problem-solver than a collaborative partner.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Amira offers meaningful change talk about regret, not wanting to keep pretending, and wanting the heaviness to stop. The psychiatrist does not consistently amplify this language with evocative questions and instead moves quickly into concrete options.

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** The psychiatrist acknowledges Amira’s frustration and exhaustion, but does not actively soften or redirect her sustain talk. There are few double-sided reflections or efforts to explore ambivalence, and repeated prompts for action may reinforce the sense of being stuck.
