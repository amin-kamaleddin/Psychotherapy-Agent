# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

---

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The psychiatrist is generally respectful and nonjudgmental, using normalizing statements such as “That makes sense” and “Totally understandable.” However, acceptance remains somewhat superficial and often shifts quickly into reassurance or problem-solving rather than deeply acknowledging the client’s conflicted experience.

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** There are several attempts at perspective-taking, but the empathic reflections are often surface-level and miss the emotional core of the client’s concerns. The counselor tends to move toward scripts and practical solutions before fully reflecting the client’s ambivalence, self-criticism, or fear of being seen as difficult.

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The session maintains a clear focus on the target behavior of setting boundaries and specifically addresses the parent email issue. The conversation stays on track and moves from concern to concrete plan, though it becomes somewhat task-centered and repetitive.

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The psychiatrist invites client input and responds to the client’s email draft, creating some collaborative interaction. However, much of the session is driven by counselor-provided templates, scripts, and checklists, which limits true co-construction.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The counselor offers choices and respects the client’s decision to draft and send the email herself. At the same time, frequent directive suggestions, scripts, and prescriptive guidance reduce the client’s sense of ownership and freedom.

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** The session begins with a good open question, but afterward the counselor mainly provides advice, scripts, and practical tips. There is limited effort to draw out the client’s own values, motivations, or reasons for change.

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The psychiatrist asks for preferences and invites the client’s input, which supports some collaborative stance. However, the counselor remains the primary source of strategies, structure, and expertise, making the power balance uneven.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** The client offers some change talk, such as feeling more confident and planning to send the email, but the counselor rarely amplifies or expands it. The questions are mostly task-focused and do not consistently deepen the client’s own reasons for change.

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** The client clearly expresses sustain talk about not wanting to be cold, difficult, or selfish, but the counselor does not consistently use MI-consistent strategies to soften it. Responses are often direct corrections or reassurances, which can bypass the client’s ambivalence rather than working with it.
