# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

---

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 5
**Comments:** The psychiatrist consistently communicates nonjudgmental respect and avoids shame-based language around relapse and substance use. Elias is treated as a person worthy of regard despite ambivalence, family concerns, and self-criticism.

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist shows strong reflective listening and perspective-taking, especially around ambivalence, shame, and the client's cautious stance. It falls short of a 5 because some reflections add hopeful or interpretive framing and the shame/fear themes are not explored with enough depth.

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 5
**Comments:** The session maintains a clear, target-specific focus on preventing relapse during high-risk quiet/off moments and the kitchen counter trigger. Each question progressively narrows the work toward a concrete, observable behavioral plan.

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 5
**Comments:** Elias's ideas directly shape the session, from walking and talking with his wife to using “I’m drifting” and “I was there.” The psychiatrist builds the plan from the client's language rather than imposing a prepackaged recovery strategy.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 5
**Comments:** The psychiatrist consistently reinforces client choice, self-direction, and ownership of the recovery plan. Language such as “You're designing this yourself” and questions about his own next step strongly support autonomy.

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 5
**Comments:** The psychiatrist skillfully elicits Elias's own values, motivations, and confidence language rather than supplying reasons to change. The client generates much of the change talk, including “not because I have to,” “awake, not fixed,” and “I was there.”

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 5
**Comments:** The psychiatrist clearly conveys that expertise about Elias's recovery resides within him. The interaction is sustained, collaborative, and non-hierarchical, with the client actively co-designing the intervention.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist frequently evokes and amplifies Elias's own change language, values, and emerging self-efficacy. It does not reach a 5 because confidence and commitment are not explicitly scaled or developed in a more direct MI-consistent way.

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist handles ambivalence and sustain talk well by reframing caution as “awake, not fixed” and “a note, not a score.” It falls short of a 5 because the focus occasionally remains on risk and heaviness without enough balanced double-sided reflections redirecting toward change.
