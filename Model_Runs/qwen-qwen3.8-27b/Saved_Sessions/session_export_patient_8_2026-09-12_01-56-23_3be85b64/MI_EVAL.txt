# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

---

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist shows consistent respect, warmth, and nonjudgment through affirmations such as acknowledging courage and framing checking the phone as data rather than failure. Some affirmations feel slightly optimistic and do not fully validate the client's ongoing doubt.

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Reflections accurately capture the client's torn feelings, frustration, and fear of missing out, but they remain largely surface-level. Deeper themes such as self-doubt, concern about appearing ineffective, and the value she places on connection are not fully explored.

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The session stays clearly focused on the target behavior and its connection to tiredness, sleep, and academic performance, moving toward a concrete experiment. The direction could be stronger if it more explicitly linked the behavior to the client's values, goals, and discrepancy.

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The final plan is co-constructed using the client's preferred elements, such as phone face-down, a shorter timer, group chat exception, and simple tracking. Collaboration is strong, though the psychiatrist still supplies many of the specific structures and intervention ideas.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist supports autonomy through options, low-pressure language, and respect for the client's pace and choices. There is mild agenda pressure from repeated encouragement to implement steps and arrange a follow-up check-in.

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Open questions and the confidence ruler help elicit the client's concerns and readiness to change. Evocation is limited, however, because the psychiatrist often introduces suggested solutions before drawing out the client's own reasons, values, and discrepancies.

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The psychiatrist maintains a collaborative stance by asking for input and adapting the plan to the client's preferences. The interaction is still somewhat expert-driven, with the psychiatrist leading the agenda and proposing multiple specific behavioral tools.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** There is some effort to elicit change language, including questions about what would be different and confidence in trying the plan. The session lacks consistent use of DARN-C techniques and does not sufficiently amplify the client's own change talk, such as wanting to feel less tired.

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The psychiatrist acknowledges the phone's role in connection and stress relief without arguing, and incorporates these reasons into a workable plan. However, the responses move quickly toward problem-solving rather than using MI-specific strategies like double-sided reflections or exploring discrepancy with the client's goals.
