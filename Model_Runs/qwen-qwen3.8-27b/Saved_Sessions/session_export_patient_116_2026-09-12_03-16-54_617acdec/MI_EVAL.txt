# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

---

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist consistently conveys nonjudgmental respect and avoids shaming the client for medication nonadherence. However, the acceptance remains somewhat procedural and occasionally becomes compliance-focused through repeated safety directives.

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist accurately reflects the client’s stated feelings, reluctance, and frustration, helping the client feel heard. The empathy is mostly cognitive and does not deeply explore the emotional meaning behind the client’s humor and deflection.

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The session maintains a clear focus on medication adherence and safe management of nonadherence. However, the direction is more procedural and safety-focused than strategically oriented toward motivational change.

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The psychiatrist uses some collaborative language and invites the client to choose among options. In practice, the psychiatrist frequently generates the options and clinical information, limiting genuine co-creation of the session.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist regularly supports choice, avoids forcing a decision, and affirms the client’s right to decide. Autonomy support is somewhat weakened by repeated directive safety statements that can feel controlling.

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** There is some initial effort to evoke the client’s goals and concerns, but the psychiatrist quickly shifts to information and practical options. The client’s own reasons for change are not consistently elicited or strengthened.

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The psychiatrist uses collaborative language and invites client choice, supporting a partnership stance. However, the psychiatrist often assumes the expert role and drives the agenda rather than building from the client’s own perspective.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** The client offers brief change-oriented statements, such as wanting to be “less difficult,” but the psychiatrist does not develop or reinforce them. There is limited use of questions or strategies to elicit desire, need, or commitment language.

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The psychiatrist avoids directly challenging the client’s resistance and acknowledges the appeal of waiting or “winging it.” However, the sustain talk remains dominant and is not skillfully redirected to explore its costs or values-based meaning.
