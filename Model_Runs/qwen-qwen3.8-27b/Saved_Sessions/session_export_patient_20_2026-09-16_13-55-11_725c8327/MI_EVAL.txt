# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

---

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist maintains a nonjudgmental, respectful stance and avoids labeling or disapproving of the patient’s drinking. Acceptance is somewhat procedural, with less explicit warmth or deeper validation of the patient’s internal struggle.

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The clinician accurately reflects the patient’s preference for neutral, low-pressure monitoring and control. However, some reflections become formulaic and do not fully explore underlying fear, shame, or deeper emotional meaning.

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 5
**Comments:** The session stays consistently focused on alcohol use and a concrete self-monitoring plan. Each exchange builds purposefully toward a practical, feasible next step.

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 5
**Comments:** The clinician co-constructs the plan through open-ended and menu-based questions, allowing the patient’s ideas to shape the outcome. This is a genuinely collaborative process rather than a prescribed intervention.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 5
**Comments:** The psychiatrist consistently emphasizes choice, ownership, and lack of obligation. The plan is framed as optional, quiet, and non-coercive, which strongly supports autonomy.

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The clinician effectively elicits the patient’s own ideas and change-relevant language, especially around practical implementation. It would be stronger if the deeper motivation, values, confidence, and ambivalence were drawn out more consistently.

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The patient’s preferences strongly shape the session and the resulting plan, showing respect for the patient as the expert. However, agenda-setting remains somewhat clinician-guided and the collaborative stance is not made fully explicit.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist helps the patient express change-relevant reasons such as being sharper, checking the math, and avoiding failure. The change talk could be more effectively amplified by explicitly connecting it to values, confidence, or readiness.

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The patient’s minimization and defensiveness are handled without confrontation and reframed in low-threat language. The clinician could more consistently turn sustain talk toward change through double-sided reflections or exploration of what is at stake.
