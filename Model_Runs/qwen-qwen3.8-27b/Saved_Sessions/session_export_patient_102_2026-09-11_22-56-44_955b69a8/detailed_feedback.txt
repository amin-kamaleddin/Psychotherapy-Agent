                                Motivational Interviewing Feedback Criteria (5-Point Likert Scale)

Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)
------------------------------------
1. Acceptance: Extent to which the counselor communicates unconditional positive regard for the client.
   1 = Judgmental, disapproving, labeling
   2 = Inconsistent respect, occasional judgment
   3 = Neutral stance, basic respect
   4 = Generally respectful and supportive
   5 = Consistently respectful, warm, supportive

2. Empathy: Extent to which the counselor understands and/or makes an effort to accurately understand the client’s perspective.
   1 = Little effort to understand, minimal reflective listening
   2 = Limited perspective-taking, mostly factual focus
   3 = Adequate but inconsistent effort to understand
   4 = Good reflective listening and perspective-taking
   5 = Highly accurate, consistent understanding and reflections

3. Direction – Degree to which the counselor maintains appropriate focus on a specific target behavior or concerns directly tied to it.
1 = No clear focus; session wanders aimlessly
2 = Minimal focus; frequent topic drift
3 = Occasional focus on target behavior but inconsistent
4 = Generally keeps discussion directed toward target behavior
5 = Consistently maintains clear focus and guides toward change

---

4. Collaboration – Extent to which the clinician behaves as if the interview is occurring between two equal partners, both of whom have knowledge that might be useful in the problem under consideration.
1 = Authoritarian or expert-driven stance
2 = Limited collaboration; frequent direction by counselor
3 = Partial collaboration; uneven power-sharing
4 = Strong collaborative stance most of the time
5 = Fully collaborative; client’s ideas shape session direction

---

5. Autonomy Support – Extent to which clinician supports and actively fosters client perception of choice as opposed to attempting to control the client’s behavior or choices.
1 = Directive or pressuring; minimizes choice
2 = Occasionally acknowledges choice but inconsistently
3 = Neutral regarding client autonomy
4 = Regularly affirms client freedom to choose
5 = Consistently reinforces client ownership of change process

---

6. Evocation – Extent to which the clinician conveys an understanding that motivation for change, and the ability to move toward that change, reside mostly within the client and therefore focuses efforts to elicit and expand it within the therapeutic interaction.
1 = Provides reasons for change without exploring client view
2 = Limited interest in client motivations
3 = Some evocation but superficial
4 = Frequently draws out client’s own reasons for change
5 = Consistently elicits and develops client change talk throughout session

---

7. Partnership – Extent to which the clinician conveys an understanding that expertise and wisdom about change reside mostly within the client

1 = Clinician actively assumes the expert role for the majority of the interaction with the client. Collaboration or partnership is absent.
2 = Clinician superficially responds to opportunities to collaborate.
3 = Clinician incorporates client’s contributions but does so in a lukewarm or erratic fashion.
4 = Clinician fosters collaboration and power sharing so that client’s contributions impact the session in ways that they otherwise would not.
5 = Clinician actively fosters and encourages power sharing in the interaction in such a way that client’s contributions substantially influence the nature of the session.

---

8. Cultivating Change Talk – Extent to which the clinician actively encourages the client’s own language in favor of the change goal, and confidence for making that change.
1 = No effort to evoke change talk
2 = Rare or weak efforts
3 = Occasional evocation of change language
4 = Frequent evocation of change talk
5 = Consistent, skillful evocation throughout

---

9. Softening Sustain Talk – Extent that the clinician avoids a focus on the reasons against changing or for maintaining the status quo.
1 = Reinforces sustain talk
2 = Rarely shifts focus away from sustain talk
3 = Occasionally redirects toward change
4 = Often redirects effectively
5 = Consistently pivots client language toward change

Evaluation Guidance
-------------------
- All domains are rated on a 1–5 Likert scale.
- Ratings should reflect overall quality and consistency of behavior.
- Focus on both counselor skillfulness and client speech as indicators of MI process quality.

## Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)

### 1. Acceptance
Score: 4/5

Explanation:
The counselor generally communicates respect and nonjudgment, but the session also contains enough didactic, safety-heavy language that it can feel parental or corrective. Strong examples include the opening “I’m here to listen and support you,” the repeated “I hear you,” the validation that “feeling judged when you don’t think there’s a problem is frustrating,” the admission that “feeling parented sucks,” and the later “You know your choices best” and “I trust you know your limits.” These are genuinely positive-regard statements. However, the counselor also introduces risk-focused framing (“underage drinking raises safety, legal, and brain-development risks”) and then repeatedly provides directives such as “never drive,” “avoid mixing substances,” “keep your phone charged,” and “have an exit plan.” Clinically, safety advice may be appropriate, but from an MI standpoint it can subtly shift the relationship from unconditional acceptance toward caution and control. A 5 would require the counselor to maintain warmth, respect, and acceptance more consistently while reducing the corrective or parental tone.

### 2. Empathy
Score: 3/5

Explanation:
The counselor makes a real effort to reflect Milan’s perspective, but the empathy remains mostly surface-level and repetitive. The counselor accurately captures frustration and defensiveness in statements such as “That sounds really frustrating — it makes sense to feel defensive if people act like you’re doing something wrong” and “I hear you — feeling parented sucks.” These are useful reflections. The limitation is that the counselor does not deeply explore the emotional or relational meaning underneath Milan’s resistance. For example, Milan seems concerned about autonomy, social belonging, being judged by an authority figure, and not wanting to be seen as “the kid who’s causing a scene.” A more advanced empathic response might have been: “It sounds like you want to be seen as responsible and capable, not as someone who needs to be managed, and at the same time you can see that adults are worried about you.” That kind of reflection would validate both sides more fully. As written, the counselor reflects Milan’s stated feelings, but does not consistently move into the deeper meaning, values, or ambivalence.

### 3. Cultivating Change Talk
Score: 2/5

Explanation:
There are a few early attempts to evoke change-relevant language, especially when the counselor asks, “How do you feel about your drinking — satisfied, worried, or unsure?” and says, “we can explore goals (keep as is, cut back, or stop).” Those are appropriate openings. However, the counselor does not follow through by strengthening or developing Milan’s own change talk. Milan does offer several statements that could have been reflected and expanded: “I’m careful,” “I never drive,” “I’m definitely not driving or mixing anything,” and “I know my limits.” These are valuable because they show self-efficacy, responsibility, and existing boundaries. The counselor mostly acknowledges them rather than amplifying them. A stronger MI response might have been: “It sounds like you already have some important boundaries—like not driving or not mixing. What helps you make those choices?” or “When you say you’re careful, what does that look like in practice?” The counselor also does not evoke Milan’s values, hopes, fears, or personal reasons for change. Overall, change talk is present in the patient’s speech, but the counselor does not consistently cultivate it.

### 4. Softening Sustain Talk
Score: 2/5

Explanation:
The counselor does not openly argue with Milan, which is important, but the session does not effectively soften sustain talk. Milan has many statements supporting the status quo, such as “I really don’t think it’s that bad,” “It feels like a lot of fuss for something that’s not actually hurting anyone,” “I’m good where I am right now,” and “I’m not going to cause a problem, I promise.” Instead of gently redirecting these statements toward Milan’s own values or ambivalence, the counselor often adds risk-focused content, such as “Since you’re 17, there are extra legal and brain-development risks even with occasional drinking,” and then offers a checklist of safety tips. A better MI response might have used a double-sided reflection: “On one hand, drinking is part of how you connect with friends and feel like you belong; on the other hand, you can also see why adults would worry about safety and the consequences.” Then the counselor could ask, “What matters most to you in that balance?” or “What would you most want to avoid?” As written, the counselor avoids direct confrontation but does not consistently redirect sustain talk in a way that opens up change.

### 5. Partnership

**Overall Global Ratings Score:** **2.3/5**

This session contains several respectful and empathic moments, but the counselor’s style is more safety-directed and advice-giving than MI-consistent.
