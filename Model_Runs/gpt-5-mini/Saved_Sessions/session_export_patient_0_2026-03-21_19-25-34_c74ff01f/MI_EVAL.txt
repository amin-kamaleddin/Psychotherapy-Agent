# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

---

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 5
**Comments:** The clinician was consistently nonjudgmental and validating, honoring Adrian's choice not to quit and offering harm‑reduction options rather than moralizing. Practical, respectful choices and supportive language were used throughout.

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Strong reflective listening and validation were evident, matching concerns about infection and avoiding lecturing. However, opportunities for deeper affective reflections (e.g., shame, guardedness) and exploration of the social meaning of weekends were missed.

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 5
**Comments:** The session stayed clearly focused on meth use-related reinfection risk and harm reduction, translating concern into concrete behaviors and safety thresholds. Practical steps and a clear target behavior were consistently maintained.

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The interaction was collaborative with co‑created scripts and back‑and‑forth planning, reinforcing joint problem solving. At times the clinician supplied many concrete solutions, which slightly shifted the balance toward expert advice rather than eliciting more of Adrian's own ideas.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 5
**Comments:** The clinician repeatedly offered choices, honored Adrian's decision not to quit, and framed suggestions as optional, including a small time‑limited experiment to preserve agency. Language supported self‑direction and reduced reactance.

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Limited use of evocative MI techniques meant the session relied more on idea‑giving than eliciting Adrian's internal motivations. Few scaling questions, pros/cons exploration, or reflective summaries were used to draw out intrinsic reasons for change.

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The clinician worked with Adrian to draft plans and messages and invited his preferences, demonstrating genuine partnership. Nonetheless, frequent provision of ready‑made options slightly tilted the interaction toward clinician-led solutions.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Some change statements were elicited (e.g., willingness to try harm‑reduction and a two‑week test) but the clinician rarely amplified or deepened desire, ability, reasons, or need. More strategic reflections, scaling, and probes could have strengthened elicitation and reinforcement of change talk.

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The clinician avoided countering sustain talk and respected Adrian's stance, which prevented pushback. However, MI strategies to soften sustain talk—such as double‑sided reflections, rolling with resistance, or summarizing ambivalence—were used infrequently.
