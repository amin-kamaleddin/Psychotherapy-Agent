# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

---

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 5
**Comments:** Consistently conveys unconditional positive regard and nonjudgmental support, validating feelings and accepting the patient’s statement “I’m not ready to quit.” The clinician works from the patient’s position and offers practical harm-reduction rather than shaming or pressuring.

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Demonstrates solid reflective listening and perspective-taking, clarifying details and normalizing guilt and practical concerns. However, the clinician often moves quickly to advice and misses opportunities for deeper, more complex emotional reflections about stress and single-parent conflict.

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 5
**Comments:** Maintains a clear, consistent focus on smoking and protecting the child, repeatedly returning to harm-reduction, NRT, and concrete steps. The session advances toward specific, achievable actions appropriate to the patient’s stage.

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Engages in joint problem-solving and adapts recommendations to the patient’s constraints, offering tailored plans and scripts. At times the clinician delivers rapid, didactic lists instead of inviting the patient to co-create and prioritize each step.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 5
**Comments:** Consistently respects and preserves choice, accepting the patient’s readiness and offering optional strategies (harm-reduction, NRT options, choosing a small rule). Language and options support the patient’s control over decisions without coercion.

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Elicits motivation tied to the patient’s values (protecting her son) and secures a small commitment to change. Could increase open, evocative questions and selective reflections to amplify the patient’s own reasons, importance, and confidence.

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Generally collaborative—asks clarifying questions, offers choices, and co-creates practical plans. Greater agenda-setting and seeking permission before delivering longer advice lists would strengthen the sense of partnership.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Effectively elicits and supports change-oriented statements, offers concrete reduction strategies, and asks the patient to choose a small step. The clinician could more actively amplify and reinforce change talk and probe for confidence and commitment language.

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Avoids arguing and reframes sustain talk into pragmatic harm-reduction alternatives, acknowledging barriers like cold and work. Could more often use double-sided reflections or strategic techniques to gently highlight discrepancy and diffuse sustain arguments.
