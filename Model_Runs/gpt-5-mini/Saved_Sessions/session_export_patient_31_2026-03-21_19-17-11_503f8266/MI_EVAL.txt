# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

---

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Clinician is respectful and nonjudgmental, normalizing the patient’s experience and avoiding blame. Some individualized warmth is lost when the interaction quickly shifts to technical instructions instead of deeper validation of the patient’s feelings (e.g., "I like smoking").

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** There are moments of basic reflective listening (e.g., "that sounds tiring"), but responses are mostly informational rather than sustained reflections. Empathic understanding is adequate but inconsistent and relatively superficial.

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 5
**Comments:** Session maintains a clear, appropriate focus on tobacco harm‑reduction and provides specific, actionable guidance (dosing, technique, step‑down plans). The clinician consistently guides the patient toward practical behavior change aligned with the consultation goal.

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The clinician interacts politely and solicits brief input (e.g., asking about NRT preference), but largely functions as an expert information provider. Shared problem‑solving and joint agenda setting are limited, so the plan is not fully co‑created.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 5
**Comments:** Clinician repeatedly affirms the patient’s choice and uses a noncoercive approach, offering harm‑reduction options consistent with the patient’s preference. Permission was sought before offering help, reinforcing the patient’s sense of control.

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** The clinician supplies extensive information but makes little effort to draw out the patient’s own motivations or reasons for change. There are no questions probing importance, confidence, or desired outcomes to strengthen intrinsic motivation.

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Some collaborative elements are present (respectful answers, asking about preferences), yet the interaction remains clinician‑driven and information‑heavy. The clinician seldom uses reflective summaries or explicit negotiation of next steps as a shared plan.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The clinician creates openings for change talk (offering help choosing NRT, asking about plans) and the patient expresses some change‑oriented language. However, targeted techniques to evoke and amplify change talk (open questions, reflective amplification) are underused.

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Clinician avoids confronting sustain talk and offers harm‑reduction, which prevents escalation, but does not actively soften sustain talk with double‑sided reflections or reframing. Responses are neutral‑to‑redirective rather than consistently aimed at resolving ambivalence.
