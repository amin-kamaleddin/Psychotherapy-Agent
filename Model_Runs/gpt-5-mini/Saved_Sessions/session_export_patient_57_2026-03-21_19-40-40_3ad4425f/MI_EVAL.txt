# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

---

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Clinician is respectful and nonjudgmental, normalizing Jamal’s disclosure and acknowledging difficulty. Falls short of top score because the tone shifts into directive coaching without deeper individualized affirmations of strengths or ambivalence.

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** There are clear empathic reflections that acknowledge stress and fear of judgment, but these are intermittent and brief. The clinician often moves quickly to advice, missing opportunities for deeper reflective listening about Jamal’s emotions and concerns.

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 5
**Comments:** The clinician maintains a clear, appropriate focus on refusing alcohol games and safety planning throughout the session. Every turn advances concrete, behaviorally specific strategies and does not drift off-topic.

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Some joint work occurs (role‑play, asking preference), but the clinician provides most content through scripted suggestions and modeling. Genuine co‑creation is limited; Jamal’s own ideas are not extensively elicited or integrated.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The clinician offers options and asks which tactics feel like Jamal, supporting choice and noncoercive language. It is somewhat prescriptive, however, and lacks explicit reinforcement of Jamal’s ownership of decisions.

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** The clinician rarely elicits Jamal’s intrinsic motivations, values, or reasons for change and quickly supplies solutions instead. Several missed opportunities to draw out importance, confidence, or past successes limit evocation.

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** There are partnership elements like permission‑seeking and checking which lines fit Jamal, but the interaction is largely expert‑driven. The clinician often offers options to choose from rather than co‑creating a plan with Jamal.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Some change talk arises and the clinician invites practice and safety planning, which can support commitment. However, the clinician does not strategically elicit or amplify Jamal’s own motivations, values, or reasons for change.

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The clinician identifies sustain‑talk sources (peer pressure, fear of judgment) and provides alternative tactics to reduce compliance. They rarely validate the legitimacy of sustain talk or use double‑sided reflections to acknowledge ambivalence.
