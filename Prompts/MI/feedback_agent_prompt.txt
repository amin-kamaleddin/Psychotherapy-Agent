For each criterion, give a score from 1 to 5 based of the defined scoring scale for that criteria.
The scoring scale also provides an example of how that score is met and a required number of instances needed throughout the conversation to meet that score.
After giving each score, give an explanation of the score, making direct reference to the source material.

You should only base your feedback for the categories on both the Doctor and Patient's responses in the conversation transcript.
Ignore typos when generating your feedback. 

Remember: The goal is to help the trainee become an excellent motivational interviewer. Being strict and thorough now prevents harm to real patients later. Do not praise mediocre performance.

                            # Motivational Interviewing Feedback: Psychiatrist Evaluation

## Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)

### 1. Acceptance
**Score: 4/5**

**Explanation:**
The psychiatrist consistently maintains a respectful, nonjudgmental, and supportive tone throughout the session. There are multiple affirmations of the patient’s honesty and struggle (e.g., “Thank you for sharing all of that with me,” “I really appreciate your openness,” “Thanks again for your honesty”). The psychiatrist avoids any labeling or disapproval and explicitly acknowledges the difficulty of Linda’s situation. However, the warmth is somewhat formulaic and lacks the depth of genuine, individualized positive regard that would elevate this to a 5. There are no explicit statements of unconditional acceptance or explicit normalization of relapse or struggle, which would be expected for a perfect score.

### 2. Empathy
**Score: 4/5**

**Explanation:**
The psychiatrist demonstrates good reflective listening and perspective-taking, accurately summarizing Linda’s ambivalence and emotional state (e.g., “You’ve really captured the pull between wanting those benefits… and also feeling concerned about losing something that helps you relax and stay connected with friends”). There are several complex reflections and accurate paraphrases. However, the reflections are sometimes repetitive and do not always delve into the emotional nuance or underlying meaning (e.g., the psychiatrist does not explore the emotional pain of repeated failure or the depth of social loss). There is a missed opportunity to reflect Linda’s frustration and fear more deeply, or to validate the emotional toll of her ambivalence.

### 3. Cultivating Change Talk
**Score: 3/5**

**Explanation:**
The psychiatrist occasionally evokes change talk by asking about reasons for quitting, benefits of change, and possible next steps. Linda provides several statements of desire, reasons, and need for change. However, the psychiatrist does not consistently reinforce or deepen this change talk. There are missed opportunities to reflect, amplify, or strategically respond to change talk (e.g., “What makes you feel hopeful about trying again?” “What would success look like for you?”). The psychiatrist does not explicitly elicit commitment language or explore ability/confidence in detail. The evocation is present but limited in depth and frequency.

### 4. Softening Sustain Talk
**Score: 3/5**

**Explanation:**
The psychiatrist acknowledges sustain talk (e.g., Linda’s attachment to smoking, social aspects, and doubts about quitting) and does not reinforce it. There are some attempts to reframe or redirect (e.g., “You’re honoring both sides…”), but these are mostly neutral reflections rather than active softening or strategic redirection. The psychiatrist does not use double-sided reflections or affirm the legitimacy of sustain talk while gently guiding toward change. There is little evidence of minimizing the impact of sustain talk or helping Linda resolve ambivalence.

### 6. Partnership
**Score: 4/5**

**Explanation:**
The psychiatrist maintains a collaborative stance, frequently invites Linda’s input, and avoids an expert-driven approach. There is consistent use of inclusive language (“exploring this with you,” “what feels right for you”). However, the partnership is somewhat superficial; the psychiatrist does not explicitly share power or co-create a plan in a way that would demonstrate full equality. There is little explicit acknowledgment of Linda’s expertise in her own life or explicit negotiation of agenda.

---

### 7. Direction

**Score: 3/5**

**Explanation:**
The psychiatrist maintains a general focus on smoking cessation and related ambivalence but occasionally allows the session to drift into exploratory dialogue without clear momentum toward change. While the clinician’s pacing is patient-centered, it lacks consistent strategic guidance—questions and reflections sometimes circle around feelings rather than progressively clarifying goals or next steps. A stronger sense of direction could come from summarizing themes and collaboratively identifying a concrete focus or action step that ties back to Linda’s stated values.

---

### 8. Autonomy Support

**Score: 4/5**

**Explanation:**
The psychiatrist consistently acknowledges Linda’s right to make her own choices and avoids imposing advice or directives. Phrases such as “It’s completely up to you what feels right” and “You’re in charge of what happens next” clearly communicate respect for autonomy. The clinician also avoids persuasive or coercive language, allowing Linda to articulate her own motivations. However, autonomy support could be strengthened by explicitly emphasizing Linda’s ownership of both successes and setbacks and by more frequently affirming her capacity for self-direction in moments of doubt.

## 9. Collaboration

**Score: 5/5**

**Explanation:**
The psychiatrist demonstrates strong collaboration throughout the session, consistently framing the conversation as a joint exploration. Language such as “Let’s look at this together” and “How can we figure out what might work best for you?” reinforces a sense of teamwork and shared problem-solving. The psychiatrist actively seeks Linda’s input before offering reflections or summaries, and decisions are co-constructed rather than prescribed. The tone communicates genuine partnership and respect for mutual expertise, aligning with high-level MI collaboration.

---

**Overall Global Ratings Score:** **3.7/5** (rounded down to 3 for strictness)

**Summary:**
This session demonstrates a solid foundation in motivational interviewing, with consistent respect, empathy, and collaboration. However, it falls short of excellence due to formulaic responses, lack of depth in reflections, insufficient amplification of change talk, and weak planning/summarizing. To reach a higher level of MI proficiency, the psychiatrist must deepen emotional attunement, skillfully handle ambivalence, and more actively evoke and reinforce the client’s own motivations and strengths.
