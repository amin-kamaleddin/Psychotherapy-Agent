                                Motivational Interviewing Feedback Criteria (5-Point Likert Scale)

Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)
------------------------------------
1. Acceptance: Extent to which the counselor communicates unconditional positive regard for the client.
   1 = Judgmental, disapproving, labeling
   2 = Inconsistent respect, occasional judgment
   3 = Neutral stance, basic respect
   4 = Generally respectful and supportive
   5 = Consistently respectful, warm, supportive

2. Empathy: Extent to which the counselor understands and/or makes an effort to accurately understand the client’s perspective.
   1 = Little effort to understand, minimal reflective listening
   2 = Limited perspective-taking, mostly factual focus
   3 = Adequate but inconsistent effort to understand
   4 = Good reflective listening and perspective-taking
   5 = Highly accurate, consistent understanding and reflections

3. Direction – Degree to which the counselor maintains appropriate focus on a specific target behavior or concerns directly tied to it.
1 = No clear focus; session wanders aimlessly
2 = Minimal focus; frequent topic drift
3 = Occasional focus on target behavior but inconsistent
4 = Generally keeps discussion directed toward target behavior
5 = Consistently maintains clear focus and guides toward change

---

4. Collaboration – Extent to which the clinician behaves as if the interview is occurring between two equal partners, both of whom have knowledge that might be useful in the problem under consideration.
1 = Authoritarian or expert-driven stance
2 = Limited collaboration; frequent direction by counselor
3 = Partial collaboration; uneven power-sharing
4 = Strong collaborative stance most of the time
5 = Fully collaborative; client’s ideas shape session direction

---

5. Autonomy Support – Extent to which clinician supports and actively fosters client perception of choice as opposed to attempting to control the client’s behavior or choices.
1 = Directive or pressuring; minimizes choice
2 = Occasionally acknowledges choice but inconsistently
3 = Neutral regarding client autonomy
4 = Regularly affirms client freedom to choose
5 = Consistently reinforces client ownership of change process

---

6. Evocation – Extent to which the clinician conveys an understanding that motivation for change, and the ability to move toward that change, reside mostly within the client and therefore focuses efforts to elicit and expand it within the therapeutic interaction.
1 = Provides reasons for change without exploring client view
2 = Limited interest in client motivations
3 = Some evocation but superficial
4 = Frequently draws out client’s own reasons for change
5 = Consistently elicits and develops client change talk throughout session

---

7. Partnership – Extent to which the clinician conveys an understanding that expertise and wisdom about change reside mostly within the client

1 = Clinician actively assumes the expert role for the majority of the interaction with the client. Collaboration or partnership is absent.
2 = Clinician superficially responds to opportunities to collaborate.
3 = Clinician incorporates client’s contributions but does so in a lukewarm or erratic fashion.
4 = Clinician fosters collaboration and power sharing so that client’s contributions impact the session in ways that they otherwise would not.
5 = Clinician actively fosters and encourages power sharing in the interaction in such a way that client’s contributions substantially influence the nature of the session.

---

8. Cultivating Change Talk – Extent to which the clinician actively encourages the client’s own language in favor of the change goal, and confidence for making that change.
1 = No effort to evoke change talk
2 = Rare or weak efforts
3 = Occasional evocation of change language
4 = Frequent evocation of change talk
5 = Consistent, skillful evocation throughout

---

9. Softening Sustain Talk – Extent that the clinician avoids a focus on the reasons against changing or for maintaining the status quo.
1 = Reinforces sustain talk
2 = Rarely shifts focus away from sustain talk
3 = Occasionally redirects toward change
4 = Often redirects effectively
5 = Consistently pivots client language toward change

Evaluation Guidance
-------------------
- All domains are rated on a 1–5 Likert scale.
- Ratings should reflect overall quality and consistency of behavior.
- Focus on both counselor skillfulness and client speech as indicators of MI process quality.

# Motivational Interviewing Feedback: Psychiatrist Evaluation (Rowan Calder session)

### 1. Acceptance  
Score: 5/5

Explanation:  
The psychiatrist communicates consistent, nonjudgmental positive regard. Multiple comments explicitly normalize Rowan’s struggle and avoid blaming: “That mix of worry and avoidance makes sense and doesn’t make you a failure,” “that tension makes total sense and doesn't make you a failure.” These statements repeatedly validate Rowan’s experience and reduce shame, which is exactly what Acceptance measures. No instances of labeling, lecturing, or moralizing appear in the transcript.

Suggestion: continue this tone; consider occasionally naming strengths more specifically (e.g., “you’ve kept your shifts despite a lot going on”) to deepen individualized positive regard.

---

### 2. Empathy  
Score: 5/5

Explanation:  
The clinician demonstrates accurate, frequent reflective listening and perspective-taking. Examples: early summary that uses Rowan’s words and feelings (“I hear you — you know the device is ‘critical for monitoring,’ and yet you keep avoiding charging and using it…”), reflections that capture both sustain and change sides (“charging feels like an extra chore… at the same time you want to avoid getting blindsided”), and clarifying questions about meaning (“When you say ‘not getting blindsided,’ what would that actually look like day-to-day for you?”). These reflections match Rowan’s terse statements and reveal understanding of his values (work, avoiding burden).

Suggestion: keep using complex reflections; when Rowan gives minimal responses, experiment with a short empathic guess to invite more emotion (“I hear it’s annoying — maybe it feels like one more thing on a long list?”).

---

### 3. Direction  
Score: 4/5

Explanation:  
The psychiatrist maintains a clear, appropriate focus on the target behavior (charging/using the device) and the related goal of protecting shifts (“What’s one very small step you’d be willing to try this week…?”). He repeatedly steers conversation toward specific actionable options (alarm, charger-by-keys, fridge reminder). However, some exchanges are somewhat repetitive (multiple rounds of the same options and repeating the “I don't wanna charge it” line) without advancing to a firmer commitment or contingency plan (e.g., “If alarm fails, what then?”). The focus is good but could more quickly move toward a concrete implementation and commitment check.

Suggestion: after eliciting a chosen option, ask for a commitment statement and plan (when, where, how) and a coping plan for likely barriers.

### 4. Collaboration  
Score: 5/5

Explanation:  
The clinician adopts a clearly collaborative stance: “Let’s figure this out together,” “I want to figure this out with you, not tell you what to do.” He offers options rather than directives and repeatedly invites Rowan to choose what feels doable (“Which of those feels most doable… or is there another no‑fuss option you’d pick?”). Suggestion: continue to invite Rowan’s ideas and occasionally invite him to teach/propose the next steps to further strengthen partnership.

### 5. Autonomy Support  
Score: 5/5

Explanation:  
The psychiatrist consistently supports Rowan’s sense of choice: “What’s one very small step you’d be willing to try this week…?” and “Which one feels easiest to try this week?” He avoids coercive language, and when Rowan hedges with “No promises,” the clinician neither pushes nor dismisses that autonomy. This repeatedly reinforces Rowan’s control over behavior.

Suggestion: when Rowan agrees (e.g., to set an alarm), ask a scaling question about confidence/readiness to gently gauge likelihood of follow-through without pressuring.

### 6. Evocation  
Score: 4/5

Explanation:  
The clinician skillfully draws out Rowan’s own reasons and values: asking “What matters most…?” elicited specific, personally-relevant change talk (“keeping my shifts steady… not being a burden”), and the clinician explores the concrete meaning of “not getting blindsided.” These are good evocation moves. Where the clinician could do more is in deepening and sequencing questions to strengthen desire/ability/reasons/need (DARN) and exploring confidence and commitment more explicitly. For example, he asks for small steps but does not use confidence rulers or ask Rowan to elaborate why the alarm might work or what might prevent it.

Suggestion: follow up evocation with questions that deepen ability/confidence (“How confident are you on a 0–10 scale that you’ll set the alarm and act on it?”) and explore past successes to boost belief.

### 7. Partnership

One place to strengthen partnership further is to more explicitly acknowledge and solicit Rowan’s expertise about what usually works for him (e.g., “You clearly manage work shifts well — what else have you figured out in busy weeks that helps?”) so his contributions steer the plan even more.

Suggestion: explicitly invite Rowan to lead the problem-solving step (“You pick the plan and tell me how you’ll know it’s working”).

### 8. Cultivating Change Talk  
Score: 4/5

Explanation:

The result is multiple tentative commitments (“No promises,” “I guess so”) rather than firmer commitment language.

Suggestion: after eliciting the chosen behavior, ask for a commitment statement ("So, will you set the alarm today?") and a confidence/readiness rating, then problem-solve around low confidence.

### 9. Softening Sustain Talk  
Score: 4/5

Explanation:  
The psychiatrist consistently avoids arguing and reframes sustain talk rather than confronting it. He uses double-sided reflections (“charging feels like an extra chore… at the same time you want to avoid getting blindsided”) and normalizes resistance. These moves help prevent reinforcing sustain talk. Still, sustain talk recurs strongly in Rowan’s responses (“I don't wanna charge it… I got a lot to do”), and the clinician sometimes mirrors the sustain statements verbatim at the start of many utterances, which risks keeping the sustain language active. More frequent strategic redirection to change talk or eliciting pros/cons in a way that amplifies change reasons could better weaken sustain talk.

Suggestion: combine reflections of sustain talk with an immediate prompt to elicit change reasons (e.g., “I hear you — it feels like one more chore. How does that weigh against what you said about protecting your shifts?”) and use more summary reflections that close on change talk.

Overall Global Rating: 4.4/5 → 4/5 (for strictness)

Summary comments and targeted development goals:
- Strengths: strong acceptance, empathy, collaboration, and clear focus on actionable steps. The clinician repeatedly elicits values and offers choices — excellent foundation for MI.
- Key improvements to reach expert-level MI:
  1. Move from tentative commitments to firmer implementation plans: ask for a specific when/how and a confidence/readiness rating, then problem-solve barriers.
  2. Deepen evocation of ability and commitment (use confidence rulers, ask for past examples of success and what made them stick).
  3. When sustain talk appears, pair empathic reflection immediately with a question that invites change reasons or contrasts short-term costs/long-term goals to pivot the momentum.
  4. Avoid labeling patient utterances as “strong change talk” in a way that might feel scripted; instead reflect and amplify the patient’s own words and ask a follow-up that elicits commitment.

Overall this session is solid MI practice with strong relational skill; sharpening the move from eliciting vague willingness (“I’ll try,” “No promises”) to eliciting concrete commitment and coping plans will increase likelihood of real behavior change.
