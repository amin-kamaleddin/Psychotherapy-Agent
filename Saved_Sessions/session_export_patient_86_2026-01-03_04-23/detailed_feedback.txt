                                Motivational Interviewing Feedback Criteria (5-Point Likert Scale)

Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)
------------------------------------
1. Acceptance: Extent to which the counselor communicates unconditional positive regard for the client.
   1 = Judgmental, disapproving, labeling
   2 = Inconsistent respect, occasional judgment
   3 = Neutral stance, basic respect
   4 = Generally respectful and supportive
   5 = Consistently respectful, warm, supportive

2. Empathy: Extent to which the counselor understands and/or makes an effort to accurately understand the client’s perspective.
   1 = Little effort to understand, minimal reflective listening
   2 = Limited perspective-taking, mostly factual focus
   3 = Adequate but inconsistent effort to understand
   4 = Good reflective listening and perspective-taking
   5 = Highly accurate, consistent understanding and reflections

3. Direction – Degree to which the counselor maintains appropriate focus on a specific target behavior or concerns directly tied to it.
1 = No clear focus; session wanders aimlessly
2 = Minimal focus; frequent topic drift
3 = Occasional focus on target behavior but inconsistent
4 = Generally keeps discussion directed toward target behavior
5 = Consistently maintains clear focus and guides toward change

---

4. Collaboration – Extent to which the clinician behaves as if the interview is occurring between two equal partners, both of whom have knowledge that might be useful in the problem under consideration.
1 = Authoritarian or expert-driven stance
2 = Limited collaboration; frequent direction by counselor
3 = Partial collaboration; uneven power-sharing
4 = Strong collaborative stance most of the time
5 = Fully collaborative; client’s ideas shape session direction

---

5. Autonomy Support – Extent to which clinician supports and actively fosters client perception of choice as opposed to attempting to control the client’s behavior or choices.
1 = Directive or pressuring; minimizes choice
2 = Occasionally acknowledges choice but inconsistently
3 = Neutral regarding client autonomy
4 = Regularly affirms client freedom to choose
5 = Consistently reinforces client ownership of change process

---

6. Evocation – Extent to which the clinician conveys an understanding that motivation for change, and the ability to move toward that change, reside mostly within the client and therefore focuses efforts to elicit and expand it within the therapeutic interaction.
1 = Provides reasons for change without exploring client view
2 = Limited interest in client motivations
3 = Some evocation but superficial
4 = Frequently draws out client’s own reasons for change
5 = Consistently elicits and develops client change talk throughout session

---

7. Partnership – Extent to which the clinician conveys an understanding that expertise and wisdom about change reside mostly within the client

1 = Clinician actively assumes the expert role for the majority of the interaction with the client. Collaboration or partnership is absent.
2 = Clinician superficially responds to opportunities to collaborate.
3 = Clinician incorporates client’s contributions but does so in a lukewarm or erratic fashion.
4 = Clinician fosters collaboration and power sharing so that client’s contributions impact the session in ways that they otherwise would not.
5 = Clinician actively fosters and encourages power sharing in the interaction in such a way that client’s contributions substantially influence the nature of the session.

---

8. Cultivating Change Talk – Extent to which the clinician actively encourages the client’s own language in favor of the change goal, and confidence for making that change.
1 = No effort to evoke change talk
2 = Rare or weak efforts
3 = Occasional evocation of change language
4 = Frequent evocation of change talk
5 = Consistent, skillful evocation throughout

---

9. Softening Sustain Talk – Extent that the clinician avoids a focus on the reasons against changing or for maintaining the status quo.
1 = Reinforces sustain talk
2 = Rarely shifts focus away from sustain talk
3 = Occasionally redirects toward change
4 = Often redirects effectively
5 = Consistently pivots client language toward change

Evaluation Guidance
-------------------
- All domains are rated on a 1–5 Likert scale.
- Ratings should reflect overall quality and consistency of behavior.
- Focus on both counselor skillfulness and client speech as indicators of MI process quality.

Motivational Interviewing Feedback: Psychiatrist Evaluation

### 1. Acceptance
Score: 5/5

Explanation:
The psychiatrist repeatedly conveys explicit, nonjudgmental acceptance. Examples: “I accept where you are,” “I accept that and I’ll keep it short and neutral,” and multiple reflections that acknowledge Marco’s stated preferences (“don’t lecture me,” “it’s my call”). These are not merely polite phrases — they are used at key moments when Marco pushes back (after “I can’t see myself changing now”; after “don’t lecture me”), which reduces the likelihood of defensiveness and keeps him engaged. The clinician maintains this stance consistently across the visit (at least 4–5 discrete acceptance moments), so a top score is warranted.

What to tighten:
Avoid repeating the same acceptance phrase without adding individualized content. Some acceptance statements were verbatim repeats; briefly tying acceptance to a specific value or history (e.g., “I accept that — after open‑heart surgery and prostate cancer, that frustration makes total sense”) would deepen the acceptance.

### 2. Empathy
Score: 4/5

Explanation:
The psychiatrist demonstrates good reflective listening and perspective-taking. Accurate reflections include: “you’ve smoked for 40 years… and you hate being lectured,” “you want to enjoy your coffee, wine and a smoke,” and summarizing mixed concerns (enjoyment, rituals, and fear of hospitals). These reflections helped Marco move from resistant lines (“I can’t see myself changing”) to concrete planning (“for two weeks I’ll only smoke outside…”). The clinician used multiple complex reflections and invited elaboration with open questions (e.g., “What matters most to you…?”; “What part of that plan would feel most like a real win?”).

What kept it from a 5:
Reflections mostly stayed at a behavioral and pragmatic level; there was limited exploration of underlying emotion (e.g., resignation, fear, pride) or deeper meaning (guilt, identity as a smoker). At times reflections were slightly repetitive rather than deepening (“I hear you — you’ve smoked since 14…” repeated). A 5/5 would include more consistent affective reflections and occasional reflections that name ambivalence (“On the one hand you want peace at home and on the other hand smoking is part of how you enjoy your days”).

### 3. Autonomy Support
Score: 5/5

Explanation:
The clinician consistently emphasizes Marco’s control and supports choice: “that’s your choice,” “it’s your call,” “one check‑in’s fine,” “no lectures, no surprise calls.” The psychiatrist negotiates the follow‑up format and exact wording of the text (“Would you prefer the exact text…?” / Marco: “Keep it exactly: 'How’s the two‑week test?'”), explicitly asks permission to check in, and repeatedly frames the two‑week trial as Marco’s self‑chosen experiment. These repeated, concrete behaviors (several explicit autonomy supports across the session) model high fidelity to MI autonomy principles.

What to tighten:
Continue to link autonomy to competence — e.g., after affirming choice, briefly ask how Marco’s past successes might inform this plan (further supports agency while bolstering efficacy).

### 4. Cultivating Change Talk (Evocation)
Score: 4/5

Explanation:
The psychiatrist successfully elicits specific change talk and helps the client specify a concrete plan. Key evoking moves: “what do you think would work for you right now…?” and “what small change would feel like enough of a win…?” These questions produced explicit change language from Marco: “okay, yeah. I could go for that. I’m always open to listen,” and a concrete commitment to a two‑week trial (smoke outside, half the amount, use gum). The clinician then asks what would indicate success and how to set reminders — reinforcing and operationalizing change talk.

What kept it from a top score:
The clinician did not consistently amplify or reflect and build on Marco’s change statements to push toward stronger commitment language. For example, when Marco said “I could go for that” and then proposed the two‑week trial, the clinician accepted but did not use targeted reflections to magnify motivation (e.g., “So you’re saying you could actually prove to yourself you can do this — how important would that be on a scale of 1–10?”) and did not elicit confidence or commitment scaling. More strategic summarizing of change talk and asking for a clear commitment statement (e.g., “So will you try this for two weeks?”) would have strengthened evocation.

---

### 5. Softening Sustain Talk
Score: 4/5

Explanation:
The psychiatrist effectively avoids reinforcing sustain talk and uses rolling with resistance. Examples: when Marco says “I can’t see myself changing now” and “I don’t like lectures,” the clinician reflects and validates rather than arguing, and reframes toward a self‑chosen “trial.” The clinician shifts focus from reasons for not changing to practical, low‑threat steps, which reduces sustain talk and helps Marco offer a tentative plan. The clinician also negotiates the follow‑up in a way that removes pressure (“no lectures, no surprise calls”), which keeps sustain talk from re‑escalating.

What kept it from a 5:
The clinician missed several opportunities to use double‑sided reflections (e.g., “You’re tired of being lectured, and part of you wants fewer hassles at home”) and to explicitly acknowledge the legitimacy of sustain talk while pivoting toward change. At moments the clinician could have reframed sustain talk more actively (e.g., “You’ve tried before and it didn’t work — what would make this time different?”) to reduce fatalism. Those techniques would move sustain talk further toward change talk.

Overall summary and recommendations (brief)
- Overall performance is solid and mostly consistent with MI: high acceptance and autonomy support, good empathy, effective evocation that produced a concrete behavior change plan, and skillful rolling with sustain talk.
- Key improvements: more affective reflections; strategic amplification of Marco’s change talk (to elicit stronger commitment/confidence language); targeted use of double‑sided reflections and summarizing to both validate sustain talk and pivot toward change; consider brief scaling questions (importance/confidence) to quantify motivation and guide next steps.
- Suggested next practice targets for the trainee: practice turning tentative change phrases into amplified reflections and eliciting commitment language (e.g., ask for a scaled importance/confidence and a specific commitment statement), and practice naming ambivalence with double‑sided reflections to further soften sustain talk.

Overall global rating (strict): 4/5 — good MI foundation with some clear, fixable gaps to reach high fidelity.
