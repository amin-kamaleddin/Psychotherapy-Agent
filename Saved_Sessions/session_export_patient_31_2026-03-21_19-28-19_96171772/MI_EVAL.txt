# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

---

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Opens warmly and avoids blame, with explicit safety screening and respect for the patient's ambivalence. Acceptance feels professional and consistent but is not deeply personalized or normalized.

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** There are brief acknowledgments and an open opening question, but limited reflective listening overall. The clinician moves quickly to information and misses opportunities to explore what smoking means to the patient.

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 5
**Comments:** Maintains a clear, consistent focus on smoking cessation and harm reduction throughout the visit. Provides concrete, practical steps and a clear incremental plan (two‑week patch + gum trial).

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Includes some collaborative elements (offering choices, pharmacist question list) but is largely clinician-driven. The patient's values and lived experience do not substantially shape the recommendations.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Offers options, asks which is most doable, and respects the patient's preference to avoid counseling. However, some language is prescriptive and the expert stance can undermine a stronger autonomy-supportive approach.

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Clinician supplies many reasons for quitting and technical guidance rather than drawing out the patient's own motivations. Minimal elicitation of the patient's goals, reasons, or confidence for change.

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Session is primarily clinician-led with transactional invitations rather than true co‑creation. The clinician rarely frames the patient as the primary expert or jointly develops the plan.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** There is a brief invitation and a two‑week trial agreement, but the clinician does not elicit, amplify, or explore that change talk. Missed opportunities to ask about importance, reasons, or confidence for the trial.

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** The patient expresses sustain talk repeatedly, yet the clinician responds with didactic health risks rather than validating or normalizing ambivalence. Few strategies (e.g., double‑sided reflections) are used to gently reframe resistance.
