# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

---

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Clinician used nonjudgmental language and frequent validation, honoring the patient's request to avoid a quit lecture; however, affirmations were somewhat generic rather than individualized to the patient's strengths or circumstances.

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Multiple accurate reflections and perspective-taking showed understanding (normalizing minimization, acknowledging reluctance), but the clinician missed opportunities to probe deeper emotional meaning and explicitly reflect the patient's defensiveness.

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 5
**Comments:** Session stayed consistently focused on mini‑cigar use and oral health risks, with concrete next steps (harm‑reduction tips, dentist note, follow-up), maintaining clear clinical direction throughout.

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** There was genuine co-construction (asking what would help, drafting the requested note, checking preferences), though the clinician still tended to lead with medical expertise rather than fully share decision-making.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 5
**Comments:** The clinician repeatedly offered options, respected the patient's choice not to quit now, and left decisions to the patient while offering help, consistently reinforcing personal agency.

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Some elicitation of motives (taste, stress relief) occurred, but the clinician did little to build on those responses to draw out intrinsic reasons for change or to explore personal values and discrepancy.

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** There were collaborative moves and invitations for input, yet the interaction often reverted to an expert-driven approach with the clinician directing actions and content.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** A few open questions produced factual statements, but the clinician missed opportunities to amplify emerging change language, use scaling/confidence queries, or elicit the patient's own arguments for change.

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The clinician avoided argument, validated minimization, and offered neutral harm‑reduction options, though at times relied on didactic risk statements instead of double‑sided reflections to further soften sustain talk.
