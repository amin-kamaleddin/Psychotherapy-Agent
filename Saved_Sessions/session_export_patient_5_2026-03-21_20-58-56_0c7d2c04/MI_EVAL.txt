# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

---

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 5
**Comments:** Consistently warm, nonjudgmental, and validating from the opening line onward, normalizing the patient's caution and thanking them for engagement.

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Shows good perspective-taking and reflective listening that acknowledges safety concerns, but the interaction stayed more informational and could probe emotional nuance and ambivalence more deeply.

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 5
**Comments:** Maintains clear focus on the target behavior — safely switching from phenelzine to citalopram — and guides the patient through concrete steps (washout timing, tapering, monitoring, scripts).

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 5
**Comments:** Highly collaborative: drafts materials, invites edits, offers options (tone/length/role‑play), and incorporates patient preferences in co‑constructed messages and plans.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 5
**Comments:** Consistently preserves patient choice and control by offering options, asking how to proceed, and requesting permission before filling in details, rather than directing decisions.

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Elicits actionable motivation and next steps (scripts, SMS, sending plans) but does less to draw out the patient's underlying values, reasons for change, or deeper motivation.

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 5
**Comments:** Treats the patient as an expert in their experience, repeatedly invites collaboration, and co‑creates messages rather than prescribing a unilateral plan.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Effectively moves the patient toward preparatory and mobilizing change talk by helping plan and draft messages, though could more frequently amplify commitment language and ask explicit commitment questions.

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Acknowledges and redirects sustain talk toward safety and concrete planning, but missed opportunities for techniques like double‑sided reflections to more explicitly resolve ambivalence.
