                                Motivational Interviewing Feedback Criteria (5-Point Likert Scale)

Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)
------------------------------------
1. Acceptance: Extent to which the counselor communicates unconditional positive regard for the client.
   1 = Judgmental, disapproving, labeling
   2 = Inconsistent respect, occasional judgment
   3 = Neutral stance, basic respect
   4 = Generally respectful and supportive
   5 = Consistently respectful, warm, supportive

2. Empathy: Extent to which the counselor understands and/or makes an effort to accurately understand the client’s perspective.
   1 = Little effort to understand, minimal reflective listening
   2 = Limited perspective-taking, mostly factual focus
   3 = Adequate but inconsistent effort to understand
   4 = Good reflective listening and perspective-taking
   5 = Highly accurate, consistent understanding and reflections

3. Direction – Degree to which the counselor maintains appropriate focus on a specific target behavior or concerns directly tied to it.
1 = No clear focus; session wanders aimlessly
2 = Minimal focus; frequent topic drift
3 = Occasional focus on target behavior but inconsistent
4 = Generally keeps discussion directed toward target behavior
5 = Consistently maintains clear focus and guides toward change

---

4. Collaboration – Extent to which the clinician behaves as if the interview is occurring between two equal partners, both of whom have knowledge that might be useful in the problem under consideration.
1 = Authoritarian or expert-driven stance
2 = Limited collaboration; frequent direction by counselor
3 = Partial collaboration; uneven power-sharing
4 = Strong collaborative stance most of the time
5 = Fully collaborative; client’s ideas shape session direction

---

5. Autonomy Support – Extent to which clinician supports and actively fosters client perception of choice as opposed to attempting to control the client’s behavior or choices.
1 = Directive or pressuring; minimizes choice
2 = Occasionally acknowledges choice but inconsistently
3 = Neutral regarding client autonomy
4 = Regularly affirms client freedom to choose
5 = Consistently reinforces client ownership of change process

---

6. Evocation – Extent to which the clinician conveys an understanding that motivation for change, and the ability to move toward that change, reside mostly within the client and therefore focuses efforts to elicit and expand it within the therapeutic interaction.
1 = Provides reasons for change without exploring client view
2 = Limited interest in client motivations
3 = Some evocation but superficial
4 = Frequently draws out client’s own reasons for change
5 = Consistently elicits and develops client change talk throughout session

---

7. Partnership – Extent to which the clinician conveys an understanding that expertise and wisdom about change reside mostly within the client

1 = Clinician actively assumes the expert role for the majority of the interaction with the client. Collaboration or partnership is absent.
2 = Clinician superficially responds to opportunities to collaborate.
3 = Clinician incorporates client’s contributions but does so in a lukewarm or erratic fashion.
4 = Clinician fosters collaboration and power sharing so that client’s contributions impact the session in ways that they otherwise would not.
5 = Clinician actively fosters and encourages power sharing in the interaction in such a way that client’s contributions substantially influence the nature of the session.

---

8. Cultivating Change Talk – Extent to which the clinician actively encourages the client’s own language in favor of the change goal, and confidence for making that change.
1 = No effort to evoke change talk
2 = Rare or weak efforts
3 = Occasional evocation of change language
4 = Frequent evocation of change talk
5 = Consistent, skillful evocation throughout

---

9. Softening Sustain Talk – Extent that the clinician avoids a focus on the reasons against changing or for maintaining the status quo.
1 = Reinforces sustain talk
2 = Rarely shifts focus away from sustain talk
3 = Occasionally redirects toward change
4 = Often redirects effectively
5 = Consistently pivots client language toward change

Evaluation Guidance
-------------------
- All domains are rated on a 1–5 Likert scale.
- Ratings should reflect overall quality and consistency of behavior.
- Focus on both counselor skillfulness and client speech as indicators of MI process quality.

# Motivational Interviewing Feedback: Psychiatrist Evaluation

Overall global rating: 3.0/5 — competent but not motiva­tional. The clinician is respectful and keeps the session focused on exposure reduction and stress management, but relies heavily on information-giving and directive planning. There are multiple missed opportunities to evoke and amplify the patient’s own reasons for change and to strategically soften sustain talk. Below are itemized ratings with evidence from the transcript and concrete, specific suggestions for improvement.

1. Acceptance  
Score: 4/5

Explanation: The clinician consistently communicates respect and nonjudgmental regard. Examples: “You’re juggling a lot — that sounds exhausting,” “It’s good you’re careful about smoking,” and repeated “Be kind to yourself.” The psychiatrist does not label or shame Elena for smoking and explicitly validates her burden. This is solid. It falls short of a 5 because the acceptance sometimes feels cursory and is followed immediately by problem-focused instruction rather than deeper validation of the patient’s emotions and circumstances (e.g., no explicit normalization of the difficulty of quitting or acknowledgment of the moral conflict she expresses in “I try really hard not to”).

2. Empathy  
Score: 3/5

Explanation: There are brief empathic reflections (“that sounds exhausting”) that acknowledge the surface emotional state. However, deeper emotional content and ambivalence are not explored. For example, Elena says “I’m tired… I do smoke, but I don't smoke around him… I try really hard not to,” which expresses guilt, stress, and rationalization; the clinician does not reflect that complexity (no reflection like “It sounds like you feel torn — you want to protect him but also need a way to cope with exhaustion”). Empathic statements are present but shallow and intermittent.

3. Direction (focus on target behavior)  
Score: 4/5

Explanation: The session keeps a clear and appropriate focus on smoking exposure and stress management tied to the child’s ear infections. The clinician repeatedly brings the conversation back to practical exposure reduction and small behavior changes (checklist, smoke-jacket, 5–10 minute breaks). This is effective for safety/health objectives. It is not a 5 because the direction is predominantly clinician-driven (advice and checklist) rather than a jointly negotiated path toward the patient’s own change goals.

4. Collaboration  
Score: 3/5

Explanation: There are moments of collaborative language early on (“Would you like brief, practical steps…? Any preference what to focus on first?”) and the clinician invites patient choice about a one-page checklist. But much of the interaction becomes prescriptive: lengthy lists of steps, repeated scripted closings (“Take care; session closed”), and instruction to “Please copy the one-page checklist you posted and email/print it.” The clinician does not consistently elicit Elena’s expertise about what would be realistic for her (beyond asking which to focus on).

5. Autonomy Support  
Score: 4/5

Explanation: The clinician explicitly affirms choice several times: “Any preference what to focus on first?” “It’s okay to postpone quitting for now,” and “When you’re ready, we can plan quitting.” These statements protect autonomy and avoid pressuring the patient into an immediate quit attempt. It loses a point because the clinician still pushes specific action steps repeatedly (e.g., insisting she pick a small step) in a way that can feel directive rather than fully patient-driven.

6. Evocation  
Score: 2/5

Explanation: The clinician does little to elicit Elena’s own motivations, values, or reasons for change. The transcript contains only one brief open invitation (“Any preference what to focus on first?”), and the clinician misses follow-ups when Elena says she’s thought about quitting. There is no exploration of Elena’s reasons for quitting, what matters about protecting her child, or what might make quitting possible when stress subsides. Evocation is therefore minimal.

7. Partnership  
Score: 3/5

Explanation: The clinician gestures toward partnership early but largely assumes an expert/teacher role after that. The bulk of the session is didactic (checklist, list of practical steps, instructions about emailing/copying the checklist), with limited co-construction of plans. A stronger partnership would have drawn on Elena’s experience (e.g., “What have you tried before when you thought about quitting? What helped or got in the way?”) and invited her to shape the next step more substantively.

8. Cultivating Change Talk  
Score: 2/5

Explanation: Change talk from the patient is sparse but present (“I’ve thought about quitting”), yet the clinician does not amplify or explore that statement. No questions were asked to elicit Desire, Ability, Reasons, Need, or Commitment (DARN-C). The clinician does acknowledge the patient’s precautions (“It’s good you’re careful about smoking”), but does not use double-sided reflections, reframe sustain talk, or explore the legitimacy of the patient’s reasons while gently moving toward change. Summary of strengths and critical gaps
- Strengths: Respectful tone, clear focus on exposure reduction and stress management, affirmation of patient autonomy, practical and concrete safety advice.  
- Critical gaps: Failure to evoke and deepen the patient’s own motivations; overreliance on education and directive advice; limited reflective listening to the patient’s ambivalence and emotional experience; missed opportunities to amplify the one existing change statement (“I’ve thought about quitting”).

Practical, prioritized suggestions for improving MI skill in future sessions
1. Evoke before advise: When Elena says “I’ve thought about quitting,” pause and ask open questions to elicit change talk (e.g., “What makes you think about quitting?” “What would be better if you did quit?”). Reflect and amplify any change language.  
2. Use complex reflections and double-sided reflections to hold ambivalence (e.g., “On one hand you’re careful and step outside… and on the other, you rely on smoking to manage exhaustion”). This softens sustain talk and validates the patient’s experience.  
3. Elicit importance and confidence (scaling questions) before offering plans: “On a scale 0–10 how important is quitting for you? What would make it a 1–2 point higher?” This helps tailor any future quit planning.  
4. Turn advice into collaboration: Offer options rather than instructions, and ask the patient to choose (e.g., “Here are 3 brief steps people try — which feels most doable for you?”). Avoid repeated scripted closings that can shut down exploration.  
5. Amplify small change talk and use summaries: When Elena agrees to a 5–10 minute break and neighbor help, reflect and summarize the meaning of that choice (“You’re prioritizing small practical steps now — that shows you’re balancing immediate needs with longer-term goals”), which can build momentum toward later commitment.

Final overall rating: 3/5 — competent medical care with respectful tone and useful practical advice, but insufficient use of core MI strategies (evocation, eliciting and reinforcing change talk, strategic reflections) needed to increase intrinsic motivation for quitting smoking.
