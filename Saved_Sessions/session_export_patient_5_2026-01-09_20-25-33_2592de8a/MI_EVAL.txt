# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

---

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The clinician is respectful, nonjudgmental, and reassuring about safety, but some messages are directive and clinical in tone, reducing warmer expressions of unconditional acceptance.

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The psychiatrist demonstrates understanding of practical concerns and checks on safety, but largely provides information without reflective listening or labeling of the patient's emotional experience.

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 5
**Comments:** Clear, focused, and consistently centered on medication safety, washout timing, warning signs, and who to contact, with no drifting into unrelated topics.

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The clinician asks clarifying questions, offers scripts, and supports contacting the prescriber, but generally leads the content with clinical expertise rather than fully co‑constructing plans.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist promotes patient agency by encouraging contact with the prescriber and offering options and scripts, though necessary prescriptive safety statements remain somewhat directive.

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Interaction is predominantly informational and directive with minimal elicitation of the patient's own motivations, values, or readiness for change.

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** There are collaborative elements and support for the patient's role, but the encounter is clinician‑driven with limited co‑development of the plan tailored to the patient's preferences.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** The clinician offers clear medical advice and scripts but rarely asks open questions to draw out the patient's reasons, importance, or confidence about change.

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The psychiatrist redirects sustain talk toward safety and options effectively, yet seldom uses MI techniques (e.g., double‑sided reflections or normalization) to acknowledge ambivalence.
