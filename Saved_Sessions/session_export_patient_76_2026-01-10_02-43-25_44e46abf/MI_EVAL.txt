# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

---

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 5
**Comments:** Consistently expresses unconditional positive regard and nonjudgmental support, validating the patient's experience and praising effort throughout the session.

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 5
**Comments:** Demonstrates accurate perspective-taking and reflective listening, attending to affect and somatic sensations and tracking small changes while checking for safety.

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 5
**Comments:** Maintains a clear, appropriate focus on self-harm and immediate safety, mapping triggers, co-creating a brief safety plan, and giving explicit next steps and contingency instructions.

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 5
**Comments:** Clearly a collaborative process—invites the patient's input on strategies, offers choices, and co-constructs messages and plans rather than imposing decisions.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 5
**Comments:** Consistently supports patient choice and agency by offering options, asking permission for interventions, and respecting limits (e.g., not forcing removal of the razor).

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Elicits internal material about triggers, sensations, and coping successes, but does not consistently probe deeper values, pros/cons, or amplify faint change talk into stronger motivation.

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 5
**Comments:** Frames the session as a joint effort with permission-seeking language and shared decision-making, ensuring the patient's preferences shape the plan.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Frequently invites alternatives and small experiments that elicit tentative change language, though there are missed opportunities to expand and amplify the patient's own reasons for change.

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Avoids arguing and redirects sustain talk with pragmatic harm-minimization and delay strategies, but could more often use double-sided reflections to explicitly tip ambivalence toward change.
