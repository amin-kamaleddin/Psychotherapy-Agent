                                Motivational Interviewing Feedback Criteria (5-Point Likert Scale)

Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)
------------------------------------
1. Acceptance: Extent to which the counselor communicates unconditional positive regard for the client.
   1 = Judgmental, disapproving, labeling
   2 = Inconsistent respect, occasional judgment
   3 = Neutral stance, basic respect
   4 = Generally respectful and supportive
   5 = Consistently respectful, warm, supportive

2. Empathy: Extent to which the counselor understands and/or makes an effort to accurately understand the client’s perspective.
   1 = Little effort to understand, minimal reflective listening
   2 = Limited perspective-taking, mostly factual focus
   3 = Adequate but inconsistent effort to understand
   4 = Good reflective listening and perspective-taking
   5 = Highly accurate, consistent understanding and reflections

3. Direction – Degree to which the counselor maintains appropriate focus on a specific target behavior or concerns directly tied to it.
1 = No clear focus; session wanders aimlessly
2 = Minimal focus; frequent topic drift
3 = Occasional focus on target behavior but inconsistent
4 = Generally keeps discussion directed toward target behavior
5 = Consistently maintains clear focus and guides toward change

---

4. Collaboration – Extent to which the clinician behaves as if the interview is occurring between two equal partners, both of whom have knowledge that might be useful in the problem under consideration.
1 = Authoritarian or expert-driven stance
2 = Limited collaboration; frequent direction by counselor
3 = Partial collaboration; uneven power-sharing
4 = Strong collaborative stance most of the time
5 = Fully collaborative; client’s ideas shape session direction

---

5. Autonomy Support – Extent to which clinician supports and actively fosters client perception of choice as opposed to attempting to control the client’s behavior or choices.
1 = Directive or pressuring; minimizes choice
2 = Occasionally acknowledges choice but inconsistently
3 = Neutral regarding client autonomy
4 = Regularly affirms client freedom to choose
5 = Consistently reinforces client ownership of change process

---

6. Evocation – Extent to which the clinician conveys an understanding that motivation for change, and the ability to move toward that change, reside mostly within the client and therefore focuses efforts to elicit and expand it within the therapeutic interaction.
1 = Provides reasons for change without exploring client view
2 = Limited interest in client motivations
3 = Some evocation but superficial
4 = Frequently draws out client’s own reasons for change
5 = Consistently elicits and develops client change talk throughout session

---

7. Partnership – Extent to which the clinician conveys an understanding that expertise and wisdom about change reside mostly within the client

1 = Clinician actively assumes the expert role for the majority of the interaction with the client. Collaboration or partnership is absent.
2 = Clinician superficially responds to opportunities to collaborate.
3 = Clinician incorporates client’s contributions but does so in a lukewarm or erratic fashion.
4 = Clinician fosters collaboration and power sharing so that client’s contributions impact the session in ways that they otherwise would not.
5 = Clinician actively fosters and encourages power sharing in the interaction in such a way that client’s contributions substantially influence the nature of the session.

---

8. Cultivating Change Talk – Extent to which the clinician actively encourages the client’s own language in favor of the change goal, and confidence for making that change.
1 = No effort to evoke change talk
2 = Rare or weak efforts
3 = Occasional evocation of change language
4 = Frequent evocation of change talk
5 = Consistent, skillful evocation throughout

---

9. Softening Sustain Talk – Extent that the clinician avoids a focus on the reasons against changing or for maintaining the status quo.
1 = Reinforces sustain talk
2 = Rarely shifts focus away from sustain talk
3 = Occasionally redirects toward change
4 = Often redirects effectively
5 = Consistently pivots client language toward change

Evaluation Guidance
-------------------
- All domains are rated on a 1–5 Likert scale.
- Ratings should reflect overall quality and consistency of behavior.
- Focus on both counselor skillfulness and client speech as indicators of MI process quality.

# Motivational Interviewing Feedback: Psychiatrist Evaluation (Kieran Solano)

Overall summary score: 3/5

Brief overall impression: The clinician kept a clear, useful behavioral focus (missed appointments) and produced a practical, simple plan that the patient could follow. However, many opportunities to use core MI skills were missed: reflections and evocative questions were minimal, change talk was not elicited or amplified, sustain talk was acknowledged once but not explored or reframed, and the clinician became directive at times (e.g., “take that step now,” repeated instructions and repeated closures). The interaction was respectful but somewhat formulaic and repetitive. Below are itemized ratings with evidence and recommendations.

Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)

1. Acceptance
Score: 4/5

Explanation:
- The clinician maintained a respectful, nonjudgmental tone throughout and explicitly validated Kieran early: “I hear you — that exhaustion and obligation makes sense.” The clinician avoided blaming language and repeatedly affirmed the practical steps Kieran took (“You did the practical steps — alarm, note, neighbor — and that matters”).
- Why not 5: The acceptance felt polite and formulaic rather than deeply individualized. The clinician did not normalize Kieran’s frustration beyond a single validation nor explicitly acknowledge the difficulty of sustaining engagement under mandated conditions (no statements like “It makes total sense to feel fed up when things feel routine and imposed” that would deepen acceptance).

Recommendation: Continue validating but add more individualized, normalizing statements and occasional brief affirmations of effort (e.g., “Showing up and doing this even when you don’t want to — that’s not nothing”).

2. Empathy
Score: 3/5

Explanation:
- There was an accurate but brief empathic statement early: “I hear you — that exhaustion and obligation makes sense.” That matched Kieran’s presentation.
- However, empathy was not developed further. The clinician moved quickly to problem-solving and instructions instead of reflecting Kieran’s flat affect, resignation, or frustration in more depth (e.g., Kieran’s repeated “It’s the same thing every day” and “Yeah, whatever” were not explored with reflective paraphrase or complex reflection).
- Missed opportunities: no reflections of the emotion (“sounds exhausting and kind of pointless”), no reflective summaries of ambivalence, and virtually no exploration of why Kieran shows up despite disengagement.

Recommendation: Use more reflective listening (simple and complex reflections) and occasional summaries to show deeper understanding of Kieran’s emotional state (e.g., “You keep showing up because you don’t want to lose support, but part of you feels worn out by how repetitive it is”).

3. Direction
Score: 5/5

Explanation:
- The clinician maintained a clear and appropriate behavioral focus (missed appointments) from the first targeted question and consistently brought the conversation back to a concrete plan to prevent missed appointments.
- The plan was specific and actionable (alarm, sticky note, backup transport, breathing technique, a single-line script). The clinician asked concrete questions that narrowed focus (“Which barrier is most likely... and which single reminder feels doable?”).

Recommendation: Maintain this strength while integrating more MI-consistent evocation and reflections within that directional focus.

4. Collaboration
Score: 3/5

Explanation:
- Some collaborative elements present: clinician asked “Which part would you like to focus on first?” and invited Kieran to choose a barrier and a reminder. The plan was discussed with choices (alarm, sticky note, neighbor).
- However, much of the session was clinician-led and directive (multiple iterations of the same plan, “take that step now,” repeated closing scripts). The clinician often told rather than co-created (e.g., listing steps and instructing “If you’ve set the alarm, say ‘set’”).
- The repetition of identical closing statements reduced the sense of a shared, evolving plan.

Recommendation: Share control more explicitly: reflect patient priorities before offering options, and when giving advice use “permission + information” (ask “Would it be OK if I suggest a few options?”) and collaboratively weigh options with Kieran’s input.

5. Autonomy Support
Score: 3/5

Explanation:
- The clinician occasionally supported choice (asking which barrier to focus on, offering multiple reminder options). Statements like “Which part would you like to focus on first?” and offering several backup options are autonomy-supportive.
- Yet there were also directive moments: “take that step now,” asking Kieran to say “set” as a test, and repeated instructive closures. That reduces the sense of client ownership and can feel mildly coercive for a disengaged, mandated client.

Recommendation: Emphasize choice and ownership more consistently (e.g., “If you decide to set an alarm, what time would feel right for you?”; avoid asking for immediate compliance as a test).

6. Evocation
Score: 3/5

Explanation:
- The clinician elicited minimal motivational content: asked about which barrier was most likely and which reminder felt doable — these are evoking questions about practical readiness.
- However, there was little exploration of Kieran’s own reasons, values, or past successes that might build internal motivation. The clinician did not ask about consequences of missing appointments for Kieran personally, or what keeping support would mean to him beyond “don’t lose my support, I guess.”

Recommendation: Use more open, evocative questions to draw out change talk (e.g., “What would it mean for you to keep this support?” “How would things be different if you didn’t miss appointments?”). Reflect and amplify any emergent change talk.

7. Partnership
Score: 3/5

Explanation:
- The clinician sometimes positioned the work as joint (inviting choice). Yet the heavy use of instructive language and repeated unilateral summaries (“This session is finished. Take care.”) gave an expert/leader impression rather than a true partnership.

Recommendation: Strengthen partnership by soliciting Kieran’s ideas for managing barriers (“What has worked at all in the past when you needed to remember something?”) and co-creating contingency plans based on his preferences.

8. Cultivating Change Talk
Score: 2/5

Explanation:
- Minimal elicitation and reinforcement of change talk. The clinician did not strategically amplify Kieran’s modest statements of intention or commitment (e.g., when Kieran said “Set” and later “I’ll try that breathing thing,” the clinician could have reflected and amplified these as early signs of commitment but instead moved on).
- Most clinician utterances were procedural or directive rather than designed to pull out desire, ability, reasons, need, or commitment language.
- Result: Kieran’s change talk remained weak and behavioral (setting alarm) rather than motivationally strengthened.

Recommendation: When Kieran says “I’ll try” or “I’ll set one,” reflect and amplify (“You’re willing to try the alarm — that shows you see value in staying connected to your supports”), ask scaling/confidence questions, and elicit more specific commitment language (“What would make you confident you’ll use the alarm?”).

9. Softening Sustain Talk
Score: 2/5

Explanation:
- The clinician acknowledged Kieran’s sustain talk once (“that exhaustion and obligation makes sense”) but did not use strategies to soften it (no double-sided reflections, no reframe of sustain talk, no exploration of the pros and cons that might shift language).
- Instead, the session largely pivoted to logistics and repeated admonitions to take steps, without resolving ambivalence or addressing the meaning of Kieran’s repeated “Yeah, whatever” and “don’t expect miracles.”
- Because sustain talk was not actively reframed or integrated, the patient’s default dismissive stance remained unchallenged.

Recommendation: Use double-sided reflections (“On the one hand it’s exhausting and feels pointless; on the other hand you don’t want to lose support”), roll with resistance, and strategically reframe sustain talk into a precursor for change talk (e.g., ask “What worries you most about trying something different?” then reflect any small movement toward change).

Overall recommendations (brief, prioritized)
- Increase reflective listening (both simple and complex reflections) and occasional summaries to demonstrate deeper empathic understanding.
- When the patient offers any change talk (even “I’ll try”), pause and amplify it with reflection and follow-up questions to build commitment.
- Reduce directive language that asks for immediate compliance; instead, offer options and collaborative problem-solving to bolster autonomy.
- Use evocative open questions that explore values and consequences (e.g., “What would it mean to you to keep this support?”), and use scaling questions to assess and build confidence.
- Replace repeated identical closures with brief, tailored summaries that highlight the patient’s own language and next steps (e.g., “You said you’ll set an alarm and maybe ask your neighbor — that feels manageable to you; what will you do first?”).

Final overall score rationale: The clinician is competent at directing toward a clear, practical behavioral target (Direction = 5) and is respectful (Acceptance = 4). However, key MI processes that generate and amplify intrinsic motivation — empathic depth, evocation, cultivating change talk, and softening sustain talk — were only partially present or weak. These deficits produce an overall moderate MI fidelity (around 3/5).
