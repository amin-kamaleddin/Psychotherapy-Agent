# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

---

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Clinician conveyed respect and positive regard, validating Mei’s concerns about family history, cost, and judgment to create a nonjudgmental tone. To reach a top rating they could add more individualized normalization and explicit unconditional acceptance of ambivalence.

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** There were accurate reflections and perspective-taking (family risk, protective friends, panic after unprotected sex) and responsiveness to anxiety. Many responses were informational rather than deeply emotionally reflective, missing chances to mirror shame and panic more explicitly.

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 5
**Comments:** Maintained clear focus on reducing risky drinking and improving contraceptive reliability, moving efficiently from assessment to concrete, actionable planning (EC options, appointment booking, harm‑reduction strategies).

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Interaction was collaborative—co‑constructing an action plan, rehearsing calls, and offering to stay on chat—supporting shared work. The clinician sometimes shifted to providing lists of advice without always asking permission or inviting Mei to interpret them, which slightly reduced co‑creation.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 5
**Comments:** Consistently supported choice and control by offering options, asking what felt doable, and affirming Mei’s preferences while avoiding coercive language. The clinician repeatedly upheld patient autonomy in decision points and planning.

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Elicited practical motivations and choices (what’s doable, clinic preferences) and used patient concerns to prompt action. There was limited elicitation of deeper intrinsic motives, values, or targeted open questions to amplify and stack change talk.

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Generally collaborative—offering choices, rehearsing calls, and inviting patient input fostered partnership. At times the interaction leaned toward provider expertise with medical lists instead of consistently soliciting Mei’s perspective first.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Encouraged change talk by asking what felt doable, offering options, and affirming chosen steps while linking reduced drinking to better contraception. Missed opportunities included amplifying Mei’s own change statements with reflective summaries and asking follow‑up questions to deepen and stack change talk.

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Clinician avoided endorsing sustain talk and redirected to practical strategies, but used few MI techniques specifically to soften sustain talk (e.g., double‑sided reflections or validation). A sustain comment about not caring was met with logistics rather than reflective exploration to reduce resistance.
