# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

---

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 5
**Comments:** Clinician consistently used a warm, nonjudgmental stance and validated the patient's experience, affirming small efforts (e.g., calling the 30‑minute delay a “kind test”). Language avoided blame and normalized ambivalence throughout.

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 5
**Comments:** Reflections accurately captured both content and emotion (e.g., shame, desire to please daughter) and the clinician checked what would be most helpful, showing strong perspective‑taking and responsive listening.

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 5
**Comments:** Session maintained a clear focus on smoking behavior and moved logically from motivation to a specific, time‑bound plan (30‑minute delay plus supports) and next‑week trial. Questions consistently narrowed toward concrete action.

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 5
**Comments:** Interaction was joint problem‑solving: the clinician offered options, asked the patient to choose, and tailored support (scripts, role‑play) based on the patient's preferences. Power was shared and the plan was co‑created.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 5
**Comments:** Clinician emphasized choice and small tests, offered multiple options without pressure, and left room to adjust the plan if it proved too hard, supporting the patient's control over decisions.

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 5
**Comments:** The psychiatrist drew motivation from the patient's own values (showing daughter, feeling less gross) and repeatedly linked those values to a concrete change, eliciting the patient's reasons and specific commitments.

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 5
**Comments:** Highly collaborative language was used (e.g., asking which feels doable, offering script options) and the clinician positioned themselves as a supportive partner rather than an authority. The patient led key choices.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 5
**Comments:** Clinician actively elicited and reinforced change language with open questions, reflections, and amplification, guiding the patient toward a concrete, graded plan and strengthening commitment to a small test.

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Sustain talk was acknowledged and redirected toward change options, but the clinician missed some strategic opportunities for double‑sided reflections and explicit reframing to more strongly weaken sustain talk. A brief confidence check or eliciting past successes could have further softened resistance.
