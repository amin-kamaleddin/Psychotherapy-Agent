# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

---

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 5
**Comments:** Clinician is consistently nonjudgmental and validating, honoring the patient's fears and condition‑specific requests (expedited tests, gentle med only after confirmation). This conveys clear respect and positive regard throughout the visit.

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Shows accurate perspective‑taking with reflective summaries and open questions, demonstrating active listening. Could deepen affective reflections by naming the patient's fear and anxiety more explicitly to reach the highest level.

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 5
**Comments:** Maintains a clear, pragmatic focus on prioritising expedited diagnostic tests and a single acceptable symptom‑management step. Each exchange returns to arranging referrals, confirmation method, and medication conditions, keeping the visit goal‑directed.

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 5
**Comments:** Interaction reads as joint problem‑solving: the clinician solicits preferences, offers to act (book/tests/calls), and co‑constructs decisions with the patient. Power and responsibility are shared in arranging next steps.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 5
**Comments:** Repeatedly affirms patient choice and control by offering options and requiring consent before starting medication. The clinician tailors the plan to the patient's comfort and explicitly supports her decision authority.

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Elicits motivation by asking what would help and what feels safe, producing conditional change statements about trying a med. Could further evoke internal motivations, values, anticipated benefits, and confidence to strengthen change language.

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 5
**Comments:** Consistently frames decisions as collaborative and asks the patient which option she prefers, offering multiple confirmation methods and joint selection of medication. This demonstrates strong partnership throughout the interaction.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Elicits some pragmatic change talk (willingness to try a mild med if tests are booked) by offering concrete options, but misses opportunities to amplify benefits, explore confidence, and deepen commitment language. Change talk is present but not strongly developed.

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Does not confront sustain talk; instead uses double‑sided reflections and negotiated compromise to normalise ambivalence and reduce oppositional reinforcement. Could further shift sustain language by exploring costs of waiting or contrasting the patient's own reasons for change.
