# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

---

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Conveys respect and nonjudgmental support several times (e.g., 'I’m here to support you', normalizing slips). Tends to pair acceptance with directive solutions rather than offering deeper affirmations of strengths or the difficulty of the situation.

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Shows reliable understanding and soothing reflections, validating stress and specific worries about side effects and caregiving. Empathy is somewhat cursory and often shifts quickly into solution-focused advice rather than deeper emotional exploration.

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 5
**Comments:** Maintains a clear, appropriate focus on smoking cessation and caregiving-related stress and moves logically from assessment to concrete planning. Every major turn returns to practical steps to reduce exposure and support quitting.

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Interaction is largely collaborative—responds to requests, adapts recommendations, and offers to co-write a pharmacist script. Occasionally defaults to expert-driven solutions instead of eliciting the patient's ideas first.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Frequently affirms choice and emphasizes patient control by offering options and asking permission for a plan. Some recommendations are prescriptive rather than consistently negotiated, but overall autonomy is respected.

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Demonstrates limited evocation of intrinsic motivation, focusing mostly on information, advice, and practical steps. Misses multiple opportunities to ask open, evocative questions about values, reasons, or confidence for change.

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Shows collaborative moves (asking permission, tailoring the plan) but the stance is largely clinician-led with many prescriptive recommendations. Power sharing occurs but is uneven and could be strengthened by eliciting the patient's ideas first.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Occasionally elicits change-oriented language (offers a short plan and prompt), and the patient expresses some willingness. However, the clinician does not systematically evoke or amplify reasons, desire, ability, or confidence, moving quickly to advice and planning.

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Avoids reinforcing sustain talk, normalizes slips, and offers harm-reduction alternatives. Misses opportunities for strategic double-sided reflections that explicitly name both sustain and change motivations to resolve ambivalence.
