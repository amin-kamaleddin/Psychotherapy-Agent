# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

---

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 5/5
**Comments:** Consistently warm, nonjudgmental language and validation throughout (e.g., “I’m glad you’re here,” “That’s a real experiment, not perfection,” “Thank you for committing to try, Elira — that takes courage”). Avoids moralizing. Minor missed opportunity: tie affirmations more tightly to specific strengths (e.g., concrete planning Elira already does).

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 5/5
**Comments:** Accurate, specific reflections and feeling-labeling used repeatedly (“You planned the recording… anxiety and scrolling pulled you away,” “I hear that loop — ‘it won’t be good’ — and the tight chest”). Patient acknowledgements (“Exactly,” “Yeah”) show strong attunement. Could add more reflective summaries synthesizing ambivalence.

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 5/5
**Comments:** Clear focus on target behavior (post-practice rehearsal/recording) and stepwise progression to a specific plan (phone away, breaths, 3‑min run‑through, labeled alarm, reward). Session moves logically from problem → obstacles → experiment.

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 4/5
**Comments:** Frequent invitations for patient input (“What might get in the way…?”, “Would you pick one specific practice day…?”) and co-created plan (patient suggests alarm, tea/text). Clinician sometimes proposes specific options (3‑minute practice‑lite), which is useful but slightly shifts initiative to clinician.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 4/5
**Comments:** Frames plan as an experiment and asks for input (“That’s a real experiment, not perfection,” “Would adding a ‘practice‑lite’ option…?”). Reinforces choice, but offering phrased suggestions (alarm wording, concrete steps) is mildly prescriptive—could ask before offering suggestions to strengthen autonomy.

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 4/5
**Comments:** Elicits patient-generated solutions and barriers (“What usually goes through your mind…?”, patient proposes phone‑away, timers, reward). Effective at drawing out ideas but could probe values/meaning (e.g., what submitting the audition would mean) to deepen intrinsic motivation.

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 4/5
**Comments:** Joint problem-solving tone, shared follow-up (“I’ll check how it went next time”), and patient-selected elements of the plan. Occasionally clinician-led suggestions and phrasing reduce sole client authorship—more explicit invitations to choose among options would enhance partnership.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 4/5
**Comments:** Uses confidence scales and “what would raise your confidence by one point?” which elicited concrete commitments (“I’ll try it Thursday… I’ll set an alarm”). Patient language remains tentative (“I’m still nervous… I’ll try”); clinician could amplify and reflect change talk more deliberately to strengthen commitment.

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 4/5
**Comments:** Acknowledges sustain talk (perfectionism, exhaustion, “what’s the point”) and normalizes setbacks (“be curious, not critical,” “experiment, not perfection”), redirecting toward small experiments. Could more often use double-sided reflections or re-weighting to actively diffuse sustain talk when patient repeats doubt.
