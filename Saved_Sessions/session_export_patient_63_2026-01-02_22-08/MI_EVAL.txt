# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

---

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Clinician used respectful, nonjudgmental language and normalized Jordan’s struggle (e.g., “That’s understandable,” “It’s normal to still want things”). Positive regard was clear and blame avoided. Slightly evaluative praise/reassurance (“I’m proud of the plan,” “I believe you”) reduced purity of acceptance.

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Accurate reflections and summaries captured ambivalence and affect (fear of ridicule, desire to avoid court). Multiple complex reflections were used, but probes into deeper emotional core (shame, meaning of “keeping a clean record”) were limited.

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Maintained clear focus on stopping shoplifting and recidivism; used scaling questions, elicited concrete if‑then plans, rehearsal, and follow‑up actions. Occasionally became solution‑heavy/prescriptive before fully amplifying client reasons for change.

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Patient engaged and involved in planning (chose rehearsal, sister/contact), but clinician at times shifted into directive problem‑solving and reassurance rather than co‑creating options and exploring patient‑generated solutions more fully.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Offered choices (pick a plan, who will be contact, clinician vs sister), invited patient to choose what felt doable. Some prescriptive language and reassurance slightly undercut full autonomy support.

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Elicited motivation via importance/confidence scales and “what would need to change” prompts; drew out concrete preparatory change talk and plans. Could have amplified spontaneous change statements more before offering solutions.

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Worked collaboratively in rehearsal and planning (practiced refusal line, tested saved contact), and offered to be a one‑off contact. Partnership was solid though clinician sometimes led solution selection.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Successfully drew change talk (“I can’t keep doing it,” “I have to stop,” concrete plans) using scaling and focused prompts; missed some opportunities to reflect/expand brief change statements into stronger commitment language.

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Sustain talk was acknowledged and normalized (peer pressure, desire for items), avoiding argumentation. However, clinician rarely used strategic double‑sided reflections or discrepancy techniques to soften sustain talk before moving to problem solving.
