                                Motivational Interviewing Feedback Criteria (5-Point Likert Scale)

Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)
------------------------------------
1. Acceptance: Extent to which the counselor communicates unconditional positive regard for the client.
   1 = Judgmental, disapproving, labeling
   2 = Inconsistent respect, occasional judgment
   3 = Neutral stance, basic respect
   4 = Generally respectful and supportive
   5 = Consistently respectful, warm, supportive

2. Empathy: Extent to which the counselor understands and/or makes an effort to accurately understand the client’s perspective.
   1 = Little effort to understand, minimal reflective listening
   2 = Limited perspective-taking, mostly factual focus
   3 = Adequate but inconsistent effort to understand
   4 = Good reflective listening and perspective-taking
   5 = Highly accurate, consistent understanding and reflections

3. Direction – Degree to which the counselor maintains appropriate focus on a specific target behavior or concerns directly tied to it.
1 = No clear focus; session wanders aimlessly
2 = Minimal focus; frequent topic drift
3 = Occasional focus on target behavior but inconsistent
4 = Generally keeps discussion directed toward target behavior
5 = Consistently maintains clear focus and guides toward change

---

4. Collaboration – Extent to which the clinician behaves as if the interview is occurring between two equal partners, both of whom have knowledge that might be useful in the problem under consideration.
1 = Authoritarian or expert-driven stance
2 = Limited collaboration; frequent direction by counselor
3 = Partial collaboration; uneven power-sharing
4 = Strong collaborative stance most of the time
5 = Fully collaborative; client’s ideas shape session direction

---

5. Autonomy Support – Extent to which clinician supports and actively fosters client perception of choice as opposed to attempting to control the client’s behavior or choices.
1 = Directive or pressuring; minimizes choice
2 = Occasionally acknowledges choice but inconsistently
3 = Neutral regarding client autonomy
4 = Regularly affirms client freedom to choose
5 = Consistently reinforces client ownership of change process

---

6. Evocation – Extent to which the clinician conveys an understanding that motivation for change, and the ability to move toward that change, reside mostly within the client and therefore focuses efforts to elicit and expand it within the therapeutic interaction.
1 = Provides reasons for change without exploring client view
2 = Limited interest in client motivations
3 = Some evocation but superficial
4 = Frequently draws out client’s own reasons for change
5 = Consistently elicits and develops client change talk throughout session

---

7. Partnership – Extent to which the clinician conveys an understanding that expertise and wisdom about change reside mostly within the client

1 = Clinician actively assumes the expert role for the majority of the interaction with the client. Collaboration or partnership is absent.
2 = Clinician superficially responds to opportunities to collaborate.
3 = Clinician incorporates client’s contributions but does so in a lukewarm or erratic fashion.
4 = Clinician fosters collaboration and power sharing so that client’s contributions impact the session in ways that they otherwise would not.
5 = Clinician actively fosters and encourages power sharing in the interaction in such a way that client’s contributions substantially influence the nature of the session.

---

8. Cultivating Change Talk – Extent to which the clinician actively encourages the client’s own language in favor of the change goal, and confidence for making that change.
1 = No effort to evoke change talk
2 = Rare or weak efforts
3 = Occasional evocation of change language
4 = Frequent evocation of change talk
5 = Consistent, skillful evocation throughout

---

9. Softening Sustain Talk – Extent that the clinician avoids a focus on the reasons against changing or for maintaining the status quo.
1 = Reinforces sustain talk
2 = Rarely shifts focus away from sustain talk
3 = Occasionally redirects toward change
4 = Often redirects effectively
5 = Consistently pivots client language toward change

Evaluation Guidance
-------------------
- All domains are rated on a 1–5 Likert scale.
- Ratings should reflect overall quality and consistency of behavior.
- Focus on both counselor skillfulness and client speech as indicators of MI process quality.

# Motivational Interviewing Feedback: Psychiatrist Evaluation

Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)

1. Acceptance
Score: 4/5

Explanation:
The psychiatrist conveys consistent respect and nonjudgmental tone (e.g., “You’ve said you smoke mini‑cigars…that tension sounds frustrating,” “I hear you…you’re not ready to quit”). The clinician normalizes ambivalence and avoids labeling or moralizing when the patient repeatedly minimizes (“I don't smoke cigarettes…they’re just little, mini, little cigars”). That said, the acceptance is somewhat procedural — warm but formulaic — and there are missed opportunities for stronger individualized affirmation (e.g., explicit acknowledgment of the difficulty of changing a pleasurable habit or affirming the patient’s past successes in more detail). A top score would show more consistent, individualized positive regard and explicit normalization of the patient’s resistance.

2. Empathy
Score: 4/5

Explanation:
The psychiatrist demonstrates good reflective listening and perspective-taking: multiple reflections capture both sides (“You’re balancing two real things: enjoying occasional mini cigars and wanting oral health…”), and the clinician repeats and clarifies the patient’s priorities (work/family, breath). These reflections match the patient’s language and concerns (references to keeping job, family support). However, reflections tend to stay at the practical/behavioral level and less often explore deeper emotions (fear of loss, identity tied to smoking, shame, or pride). More complex or affect‑focused reflections (e.g., “It sounds like you’re worried about letting your family down, and that scares you”) would deepen empathy.

3. Direction
Score: 5/5

Explanation:
The clinician keeps a clear, consistent focus on the target behavior (mini‑cigar use) and its functional consequences for work and family across the entire exchange. Every question and reflection steers toward a concrete, feasible change (avoid before work, rinse after, mouthwash-by-lunchbox) and toward measuring outcomes (less irritation, better breath). The session maintains momentum from values to specific behavior change plans without drifting.

4. Collaboration
Score: 5/5

Explanation:
The psychiatrist repeatedly invites the patient to choose among options and co‑creates the plan (“What one small, realistic change could you try…?”, “Which of those outcomes…matters most to you?”). The patient’s selected solutions (mouthwash, timing, visible cue) drive the plan. The clinician refrains from imposing a single “right” solution, instead eliciting the patient’s ideas and preferences, consistent with a genuine collaborative stance.

5. Autonomy Support
Score: 5/5

Explanation:
Autonomy is explicitly supported throughout: the clinician uses open questions and reflections rather than directives and repeatedly acknowledges the patient’s choice (“I’m not saying I’ll quit…”, which the clinician accepts). The psychiatrist avoids pressure and affirms the patient’s control over how far to go (small steps vs quitting). This respect for choice is consistent and clear.

6. Evocation
Score: 5/5

Explanation:
The clinician consistently draws out the patient’s own motivations, values, and reasons for change (work stability, family, breath/appearance). The psychiatrist elicits concrete change talk (willingness to try routines, conditions that would convince him) and connects behavior to personally meaningful outcomes. Multiple deliberate prompts (e.g., “what would matter most…”, “what would that mean for you and your family?”, “what would make you confident…”) effectively evoke intrinsic reasons and practical motivators.

7. Partnership
Score: 5/5

Explanation:
The interaction is plainly a joint problem‑solving effort: the clinician frames items as choices, invites the patient’s past examples, and asks the patient to select the most relevant supports. The patient’s lived expertise (what works in his schedule, what cues he already uses) shapes the plan. The psychiatrist uses the patient’s examples (packing lunch cue) to co‑construct a feasible routine, showing active power sharing.

8. Cultivating Change Talk
Score: 4/5

Score: 4/5

Overall Global Ratings Score: 4.6/5 (rounded down to 4 for strictness)

Summary and Key Recommendations
- Strengths: Clear directional focus; strong evocation of personally relevant reasons; collaborative co‑construction of a realistic plan; consistent autonomy support and respectful tone.

These changes would help move conditional willingness toward firmer commitment while preserving the collaborative, autonomy‑supportive stance already demonstrated.
