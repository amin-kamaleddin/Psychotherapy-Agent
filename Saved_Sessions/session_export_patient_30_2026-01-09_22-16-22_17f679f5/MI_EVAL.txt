# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

---

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 5
**Comments:** Consistently nonjudgmental and validating, honoring the patient's choice to decline medication and offering a respectful shared‑decision plan. Tone and language convey positive regard and normalize the need for information.

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Shows good empathic reflections and attends to the patient's fear of weakness, tailoring recommendations accordingly. Missed a few deeper reflective summaries and invitations to explore underlying values driving reluctance.

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 5
**Comments:** Maintains clear focus on cholesterol management and the statin decision, repeatedly returning to concrete next steps and a specific 3‑month timeline. Little topic drift and clear actionable planning.

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Interaction is largely collaborative with co‑constructed plans and offers to draft notes together. At times the clinician supplies detailed medical information without first eliciting the patient's perspective, which slightly reduces equal power‑sharing.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 5
**Comments:** Consistently affirms the patient's right to choose, frames options rather than directives, and explicitly honors the decision to delay statins. Sets monitoring and stopping rules that reinforce patient control.

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Elicits modest motivation (agreement to a lifestyle trial and bringing questions) but relies more on offering solutions than on drawing out the patient's own reasons, values, or confidence. Would benefit from more open, evocative questions and scaling to strengthen internal motivation.

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Fosters shared decision making and invites patient input, using collaborative language and joint note drafting. Occasionally defaults to an expert stance by providing unsolicited data and options without asking permission first.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Generates some change language through a concrete 3‑month plan and practical supports, leading to patient agreement. However, the clinician does not systematically evoke or amplify the patient's own reasons, desires, or confidence to build stronger commitment.

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Effectively validates concerns and redirects sustain talk with alternatives, risk context, and practical mitigation strategies (e.g., strength training, monitoring). Could further soften ambivalence using double‑sided reflections or reframing to make the shift more explicit.
