# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

---

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Clinician is consistently respectful and nonjudgmental with affirmations (“You’ve done well thinking this through,” “I’m proud of you”) and safety offers. Occasionally feels formulaic rather than deeply personalized; could provide more individualized normalization of ambivalence linked to Lukas’s values.

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Basic reflective listening and validation present (e.g., “You’re juggling fitting in…”), but reflections are surface-level and the clinician moves quickly to problem-solving rather than exploring feelings about being left out or teasing.

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 5
**Comments:** Clear, appropriate focus on refusal skills and safety planning throughout. Clinician consistently maintains target behavior and offers concrete strategies (refusal lines, exit plan, texting a friend).

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Clinician invites choice (asks which option feels doable, offers role-play) but interaction becomes clinician-driven with prescriptive lines and repeated instructions rather than co-constructing responses with Lukas.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Frequent acknowledgement of choice and control (options offered; asks which is doable). Occasional prescriptive goals (e.g., target confidence increase) reduce pure autonomy support.

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Elicits values and priorities early (“What matters most to you?”) but does not sustain elicitation of Lukas’s own reasons, past successes, or deeper motivations before shifting to advice.

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Some joint problem-solving (choice, role‑play offer) but partnership is weakened by repeated clinician-led suggestions and lack of client-generated language for refusal lines.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Patient offers strong anchors for change (grades, team, college), but clinician fails to consistently reflect, amplify, or elicit further desire/ability/reasons/commitment language.

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Clinician acknowledges sustain-talk (fear of being left out) and offers alternatives, but rarely uses double-sided reflections or normalizing language to diffuse ambivalence and shift toward change.
