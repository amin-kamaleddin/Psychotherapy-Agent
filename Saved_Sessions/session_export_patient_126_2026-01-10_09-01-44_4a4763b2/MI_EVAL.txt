# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

---

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 5
**Comments:** The clinician shows consistent unconditional positive regard—warm, nonjudgmental language and repeated acknowledgements of Thomas's perspective. No shaming or dismissive remarks are present.

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 5
**Comments:** Strong, accurate reflective listening and perspective-taking are used throughout, with apt reflections and summaries that capture content and tone. These moves build rapport and validate Thomas's feelings.

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 5
**Comments:** The clinician maintains clear focus on past smoking and occasional heavy drinking and moves the conversation toward a specific, actionable two‑week plan. The session progresses logically from exploration to a concrete experiment with rehearsal and implementation details.

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 5
**Comments:** The interaction is a genuine two‑way partnership, eliciting Thomas's input at each step and tailoring recommendations to his goals. Power is shared and Thomas's ideas clearly shape the plan.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 5
**Comments:** The clinician consistently supports choice and control by offering options, asking permission, and framing the plan as a low‑pressure trial. This approach fosters ownership and reduces coercion.

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The clinician elicits motivations, resources, and preferred strategies but does not consistently deepen intrinsic motives or link them strongly to values. There are missed opportunities to draw out more personal reasons and amplify change talk into commitment language.

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 5
**Comments:** The clinician repeatedly asks for Thomas's preferences and co‑constructs the plan, including wording and rehearsal for conversations with his wife. Suggestions are offered as options and integrated with Thomas's responses.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Some change talk is elicited (concerns about mornings, willingness to try a two‑week rule) and options are offered, but the clinician could more actively amplify and extend change language with selective reflections and evocative questions. There are missed chances to deepen desire, importance, and confidence earlier.

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The clinician avoids argumentation and responds to minimizations with neutral reflections and options, preventing escalation. More use of double‑sided reflections or strategic summaries would better acknowledge sustain talk while highlighting client concerns to shift the balance toward change.
