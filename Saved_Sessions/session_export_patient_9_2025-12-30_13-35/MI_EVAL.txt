# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

---

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 5
- [x] Warm greeting / invited engagement ("I’m glad you reached out")
- [x] Nonjudgmental language
- [x] Validations of feelings ("I hear how much you care…", "I hear how scared you are—that’s understandable")
- [x] No labeling/shaming

**Comments:** Clinician consistently communicates respect and nonjudgmental support across the session with repeated normalizing and validating statements; meets highest standard for acceptance.

---

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 5
- [x] Reflective listening of both content and feeling
- [x] Accurate reflections of client’s experience and affect ("that argument pattern… understandably scares your son")
- [x] Multiple empathic reflections throughout the session
- [x] Follow‑up empathic support

**Comments:** Strong, accurate perspective‑taking and frequent reflections of both facts and emotions; clinician demonstrates sustained empathic attunement.

---

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 5
- [x] Clear focus on target behavior (bedtime/screen limits and co‑parenting conflict)
- [x] Goal setting (proposes a clear, doable goal)
- [x] Progression from assessment → strategy → rehearsal → safety planning
- [x] Concrete, actionable steps (2‑week experiment, scripts, safety/legal contingencies)

**Comments:** Session stays tightly on the parenting/co‑parenting target and builds logically to specific, actionable steps including contingency/safety planning.

---

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
- [x] Invites client input ("To help, which issue...?", "Would you like to practice that sentence now?")
- [x] Joint problem‑solving (invites role‑play and asks permission)
- [x] Shares responsibility for wording/practice with client
- [ ] Purely client‑driven (clinician sometimes adopts expert role)

**Comments:** Clinician fosters partnership and frequently invites practice/input, but occasionally shifts toward directive scripting and expert guidance rather than wholly co‑constructed solutions.

---

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
- [x] Respects client choice and invites permission before activities (role‑play, practice)
- [x] Offers multiple options/compromises
- [x] Encourages client agency (supports boundary choices)
- [x] Provides necessary concrete advice when safety concerns arise

**Comments:** Regularly affirms choice and offers options while also providing concrete guidance (appropriate given safety context); a slight lean toward advising reduces pure autonomy support.

---

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
- [x] Elicits motivations/reasons for change (opening prompt; child‑focused framing)
- [x] Asks for concrete examples to surface patterns and triggers
- [x] Uses client values (child’s sleep/health) to frame change

- [ ] Deep exploration of broader values/confidence or past successes (missed opportunities)

**Comments:** Frequent elicitation of reasons tied to child’s well‑being and practice of client‑generated language; could deepen exploration of personal values, strengths, and confidence to strengthen evocation.

---

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
- [x] Asks which issue matters most to client
- [x] Invites role‑play and practice (shares power in session tasks)
- [x] Encourages client to generate wording and next steps

- [ ] Occasionally leans into expert scripting rather than fully client‑driven phrasing

**Comments:** Clinician generally acts as a collaborative partner with frequent invitations to practice; some provision of ready scripts reduces maximal client ownership.

---

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
- [x] Elicits and reinforces existing change talk (client expresses love for son, desire for consistency, willingness to try)
- [x] Uses reflections to acknowledge client motivation

- [ ] Limited targeted elicitation to amplify or deepen change talk (few questions about importance/confidence or eliciting consequences of change)
- [ ] Few strategic summaries to consolidate change language

**Comments:** Session elicits useful change language and clinician reflects it, but misses systematic, targeted moves to amplify and deepen commitment language (importance, confidence, consequences).

---

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
- [x] Acknowledges barriers and sustain‑talk without judgment (validates co‑parenting conflict and fear)
- [x] Redirects toward behavioral experiments and contingency plans (pause scripts, 2‑week trial)
- [x] Offers practical neutralizing strategies (compromises, safety/legal steps)

- [ ] Limited use of double‑sided reflections or strategic reframes to explicitly contrast sustain vs change talk

**Comments:** Clinician effectively validates sustain talk and pivots to practical steps that reduce sustain talk’s influence; more strategic reflective techniques could further weaken sustain arguments.
