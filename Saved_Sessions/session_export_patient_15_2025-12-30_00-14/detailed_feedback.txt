                                Motivational Interviewing Feedback Criteria (5-Point Likert Scale)

Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)
------------------------------------
1. Acceptance: Extent to which the counselor communicates unconditional positive regard for the client.
   1 = Judgmental, disapproving, labeling
   2 = Inconsistent respect, occasional judgment
   3 = Neutral stance, basic respect
   4 = Generally respectful and supportive
   5 = Consistently respectful, warm, supportive

2. Empathy: Extent to which the counselor understands and/or makes an effort to accurately understand the client’s perspective.
   1 = Little effort to understand, minimal reflective listening
   2 = Limited perspective-taking, mostly factual focus
   3 = Adequate but inconsistent effort to understand
   4 = Good reflective listening and perspective-taking
   5 = Highly accurate, consistent understanding and reflections

3. Direction – Degree to which the counselor maintains appropriate focus on a specific target behavior or concerns directly tied to it.
1 = No clear focus; session wanders aimlessly
2 = Minimal focus; frequent topic drift
3 = Occasional focus on target behavior but inconsistent
4 = Generally keeps discussion directed toward target behavior
5 = Consistently maintains clear focus and guides toward change

---

4. Collaboration – Extent to which the clinician behaves as if the interview is occurring between two equal partners, both of whom have knowledge that might be useful in the problem under consideration.
1 = Authoritarian or expert-driven stance
2 = Limited collaboration; frequent direction by counselor
3 = Partial collaboration; uneven power-sharing
4 = Strong collaborative stance most of the time
5 = Fully collaborative; client’s ideas shape session direction

---

5. Autonomy Support – Extent to which clinician supports and actively fosters client perception of choice as opposed to attempting to control the client’s behavior or choices.
1 = Directive or pressuring; minimizes choice
2 = Occasionally acknowledges choice but inconsistently
3 = Neutral regarding client autonomy
4 = Regularly affirms client freedom to choose
5 = Consistently reinforces client ownership of change process

---

6. Evocation – Extent to which the clinician conveys an understanding that motivation for change, and the ability to move toward that change, reside mostly within the client and therefore focuses efforts to elicit and expand it within the therapeutic interaction.
1 = Provides reasons for change without exploring client view
2 = Limited interest in client motivations
3 = Some evocation but superficial
4 = Frequently draws out client’s own reasons for change
5 = Consistently elicits and develops client change talk throughout session

---

7. Partnership – Extent to which the clinician conveys an understanding that expertise and wisdom about change reside mostly within the client

1 = Clinician actively assumes the expert role for the majority of the interaction with the client. Collaboration or partnership is absent.
2 = Clinician superficially responds to opportunities to collaborate.
3 = Clinician incorporates client’s contributions but does so in a lukewarm or erratic fashion.
4 = Clinician fosters collaboration and power sharing so that client’s contributions impact the session in ways that they otherwise would not.
5 = Clinician actively fosters and encourages power sharing in the interaction in such a way that client’s contributions substantially influence the nature of the session.

---

8. Cultivating Change Talk – Extent to which the clinician actively encourages the client’s own language in favor of the change goal, and confidence for making that change.
1 = No effort to evoke change talk
2 = Rare or weak efforts
3 = Occasional evocation of change language
4 = Frequent evocation of change talk
5 = Consistent, skillful evocation throughout

---

9. Softening Sustain Talk – Extent that the clinician avoids a focus on the reasons against changing or for maintaining the status quo.
1 = Reinforces sustain talk
2 = Rarely shifts focus away from sustain talk
3 = Occasionally redirects toward change
4 = Often redirects effectively
5 = Consistently pivots client language toward change

Evaluation Guidance
-------------------
- All domains are rated on a 1–5 Likert scale.
- Ratings should reflect overall quality and consistency of behavior.
- Focus on both counselor skillfulness and client speech as indicators of MI process quality.

Motivational Interviewing Feedback: Psychiatrist Evaluation

Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)

1. Acceptance
Score: 4/5

Explanation:
The psychiatrist communicates respect and nonjudgmental tone throughout the encounter. Examples: “I’m sorry you’re exhausted — that’s miserable,” “Thanks — that plan sounds reasonable,” and “I appreciate you trying this despite work constraints.” The clinician does not label or criticize the patient and explicitly acknowledges limits imposed by work. To reach a 5, the clinician would need more individualized warmth and explicit normalization of the patient’s struggle (e.g., “Many people with busy jobs find this hard — that makes total sense”), and fewer directive repetitions that can feel procedural rather than warmly accepting.

2. Empathy
Score: 4/5

Explanation:
There are several empathic statements and some reflective phrasing: “I’m sorry you’re exhausted,” “I hear you — it makes sense your job pushes your evening,” and repeated acknowledgements of the patient’s constraints (“I appreciate your honesty about work limits”). The clinician accurately recognizes the practical barrier of work and the patient’s limited readiness. However, reflections are brief and mostly pragmatic rather than exploring emotional nuance (no reflection of frustration, fatigue beyond the surface, or exploration of how poor sleep affects meaning/values). A higher score would require deeper, amplified reflections and more verbal checking that the clinician’s understanding matches the patient’s experience.

3. Direction
Score: 5/5

Explanation:
The psychiatrist consistently maintains a clear, specific focus on sleep behavior change. From the first turn they provide targeted sleep-hygiene strategies, ask focused questions (“How long has this been going on?”; “What time do you usually go to bed and have your last caffeinated drink?”), and repeatedly brings the discussion back to a concrete plan (2‑week diary, 2 pm caffeine cutoff, stimulus control). The session does not wander and momentum toward a specific behavior change is sustained throughout.

4. Collaboration
Score: 3/5

Explanation:
There are elements of collaboration (the clinician asks a permission-style question: “Would you try moving caffeine earlier and fixing one sleep schedule for the next two weeks?” and offers alternatives: “If that’s impossible, pick one small change”), but the encounter is largely clinician-led. Much of the interaction is information delivery and repeated recommendations rather than jointly generating options from the patient’s perspective. The clinician could improve by inviting the patient to propose which change feels most doable, eliciting ideas, and co-constructing the plan rather than primarily prescribing it.

5. Autonomy Support
Score: 4/5

Explanation:
The psychiatrist frequently reinforces choice and minimizes pressure: “If it’s not affecting your days now, track it,” “If sleep worsens… consider CBT‑I or medical review,” and “You can check back anytime.” The clinician offers options (caffeine cutoff vs. fixed wake time) and acknowledges the patient’s right to decide. To score 5 the clinician would emphasize the patient’s ownership of the change more explicitly and use more language that empowers self-efficacy (e.g., asking “Which of these would you prefer to try?” and affirming the patient’s agency in selecting and adapting the plan).

6. Evocation
Score: 3/5

Explanation:
There is some attempt to draw on the patient’s perspective (asking duration, bedtime and caffeine timing, and “Would you try…?”), and the patient does give tentative commitment language (“I’ll try… no promises”). However, the clinician relies heavily on giving advice and strategies rather than eliciting the patient’s own reasons for change, values, or confidence. There are missed opportunities to ask open questions that would deepen motivation (e.g., “What makes you willing to try this despite your job?” “How important is better sleep to you right now?” “How confident are you that you could cut caffeine by 2 pm?”).

7. Partnership
Score: 3/5

Explanation:
The psychiatrist displays polite teamwork language occasionally (“Thanks for trying this despite your job”), and offers choices. Nonetheless, the clinician predominantly occupies the expert role—presenting a menu of interventions and repeatedly restating the plan. True partnership would show the patient’s ideas shaping the plan more (e.g., eliciting the patient’s preferred small step and adapting recommendations to his workflow) and checking back that the plan fits the patient’s life before finalizing it.

Overall summary and key recommendations

Concrete, practical suggestions for the clinician

- Elicit patient-generated strategies: “Given your job, what seems most doable — moving caffeine earlier, fixing a wake time, or something else?”
- Brief summaries that selectively amplify change talk can consolidate commitment: “You’ve tried reducing one thing before and you’re willing to try this caffeine cutoff for two weeks; you’re realistic about work getting in the way, but you want to see if it helps.”
