# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

---

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 5
**Comments:** Consistently warm and nonjudgmental, with explicit statements of support and normalization (e.g., “I’m here to listen and support you,” and acceptance of staying a smoker while addressing work). The clinician avoids shaming and communicates unconditional positive regard throughout.

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Shows clear perspective-taking and reflective listening (acknowledging frustration, validating choices), but reflections are mostly content-level and miss deeper emotional nuance or more complex reflections about roles and values. There were opportunities to amplify feelings and ambivalence that were not fully taken.

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 5
**Comments:** Keeps the session tightly focused on workplace smoking management with a clear agenda, concrete tactics, dosing guidance, and a scheduled Friday check-in. There is no evidence of session drift and each exchange ties back to the target behavior and next steps.

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 5
**Comments:** Functioned as a genuine collaboration with co-construction of a specific plan (gum choice, urge strategies, phrasing for coworkers) and frequent solicitation of the patient’s preferences. The clinician modified recommendations based on patient input and positioned both parties as contributing expertise.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 5
**Comments:** Strongly supports patient autonomy by accepting the patient’s choice not to quit now, offering options instead of directives, and asking permission for planning and check-ins. The clinician reinforces that decisions and adjustments are the patient’s to make.

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Elicits behavioral commitments (gum, urge-plan, check-in) but does little to draw out intrinsic motivations, values, or discrepancy between smoking and other goals. The encounter focuses on pragmatic tactics rather than exploring personal reasons for change or importance/confidence.

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Frames work as collaborative and invites input (e.g., "Which two tactics do you want to try first?"), but some recommendations were clinician-driven rather than fully elicited from the patient’s expertise. More explicit invitations to the patient’s past-successes or preferences would strengthen partnership.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Effectively elicits and reinforces specific change commitments (choice of tactics, concrete plan, and check-in), producing explicit behavioral commitments from the patient. The clinician could further deepen and amplify change talk by asking open questions about reasons, importance, and confidence.

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Avoids arguing and does not strengthen sustain talk, but generally responds neutrally rather than using strategic moves (double-sided reflections or eliciting discrepancy) to soften sustain talk and shift ambivalence toward change. There were missed opportunities to gently pivot sustain statements into change language.
