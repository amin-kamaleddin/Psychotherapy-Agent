                                Motivational Interviewing Feedback Criteria (5-Point Likert Scale)

Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)
------------------------------------
1. Acceptance: Extent to which the counselor communicates unconditional positive regard for the client.
   1 = Judgmental, disapproving, labeling
   2 = Inconsistent respect, occasional judgment
   3 = Neutral stance, basic respect
   4 = Generally respectful and supportive
   5 = Consistently respectful, warm, supportive

2. Empathy: Extent to which the counselor understands and/or makes an effort to accurately understand the client’s perspective.
   1 = Little effort to understand, minimal reflective listening
   2 = Limited perspective-taking, mostly factual focus
   3 = Adequate but inconsistent effort to understand
   4 = Good reflective listening and perspective-taking
   5 = Highly accurate, consistent understanding and reflections

3. Direction – Degree to which the counselor maintains appropriate focus on a specific target behavior or concerns directly tied to it.
1 = No clear focus; session wanders aimlessly
2 = Minimal focus; frequent topic drift
3 = Occasional focus on target behavior but inconsistent
4 = Generally keeps discussion directed toward target behavior
5 = Consistently maintains clear focus and guides toward change

---

4. Collaboration – Extent to which the clinician behaves as if the interview is occurring between two equal partners, both of whom have knowledge that might be useful in the problem under consideration.
1 = Authoritarian or expert-driven stance
2 = Limited collaboration; frequent direction by counselor
3 = Partial collaboration; uneven power-sharing
4 = Strong collaborative stance most of the time
5 = Fully collaborative; client’s ideas shape session direction

---

5. Autonomy Support – Extent to which clinician supports and actively fosters client perception of choice as opposed to attempting to control the client’s behavior or choices.
1 = Directive or pressuring; minimizes choice
2 = Occasionally acknowledges choice but inconsistently
3 = Neutral regarding client autonomy
4 = Regularly affirms client freedom to choose
5 = Consistently reinforces client ownership of change process

---

6. Evocation – Extent to which the clinician conveys an understanding that motivation for change, and the ability to move toward that change, reside mostly within the client and therefore focuses efforts to elicit and expand it within the therapeutic interaction.
1 = Provides reasons for change without exploring client view
2 = Limited interest in client motivations
3 = Some evocation but superficial
4 = Frequently draws out client’s own reasons for change
5 = Consistently elicits and develops client change talk throughout session

---

7. Partnership – Extent to which the clinician conveys an understanding that expertise and wisdom about change reside mostly within the client

1 = Clinician actively assumes the expert role for the majority of the interaction with the client. Collaboration or partnership is absent.
2 = Clinician superficially responds to opportunities to collaborate.
3 = Clinician incorporates client’s contributions but does so in a lukewarm or erratic fashion.
4 = Clinician fosters collaboration and power sharing so that client’s contributions impact the session in ways that they otherwise would not.
5 = Clinician actively fosters and encourages power sharing in the interaction in such a way that client’s contributions substantially influence the nature of the session.

---

8. Cultivating Change Talk – Extent to which the clinician actively encourages the client’s own language in favor of the change goal, and confidence for making that change.
1 = No effort to evoke change talk
2 = Rare or weak efforts
3 = Occasional evocation of change language
4 = Frequent evocation of change talk
5 = Consistent, skillful evocation throughout

---

9. Softening Sustain Talk – Extent that the clinician avoids a focus on the reasons against changing or for maintaining the status quo.
1 = Reinforces sustain talk
2 = Rarely shifts focus away from sustain talk
3 = Occasionally redirects toward change
4 = Often redirects effectively
5 = Consistently pivots client language toward change

Evaluation Guidance
-------------------
- All domains are rated on a 1–5 Likert scale.
- Ratings should reflect overall quality and consistency of behavior.
- Focus on both counselor skillfulness and client speech as indicators of MI process quality.

# Motivational Interviewing Feedback: Psychiatrist Evaluation (Nabil Haddad role-play)

Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)

1. Acceptance
Score: 4/5

Explanation:
- The clinician opens with a clear acceptance statement (“Hi — I’m here to listen and support you.”) and explicitly normalizes the patient’s feelings (“I hear you — your anger and feeling disrespected make sense.”). These are appropriate demonstrations of respect and nonjudgment.
- Acceptance is present but somewhat formulaic and largely limited to early moments of the interaction. After the initial validation, the clinician moves quickly into tasks and directives without further individualized acceptance (no explicit normalization of anger or statements that acknowledge the legitimacy of repeated upset). A 5 would require sustained, personalized positive regard throughout the interaction.

2. Empathy
Score: 3/5

Explanation:
- The clinician uses at least one accurate, affect-focused reflection (“I hear you — your anger and feeling disrespected make sense.”), which shows perspective-taking.
- However, empathic responses are not developed or repeated; the clinician does not explore the emotional nuance (e.g., the patient’s anxiety about job consequences, the identity/esteem threat of being disrespected). The remainder of the session shifts quickly to strategies and instructions without deeper reflective listening. Empathy is adequate but inconsistent.

3. Direction
Score: 5/5

Explanation:
- The clinician consistently maintains a practical, behaviorally focused agenda: immediate coping (breaths, grounding), a single achievable next step (short message to manager), rehearsal/drafting, and subsequent documentation/escalation steps (dated log, HR). This focus is maintained through multiple turns (“One achievable next step: send a short, calm message...”, “After you send it, notice how you feel…”, repeated guidance to take a five‑minute break and to document).
- There is a clear, continuous push toward concrete action — exactly what the Direction scale measures — so this is a strength.

4. Collaboration
Score: 3/5

Explanation:
- There are collaborative elements: the clinician asks permission to draft the message (“Want me to draft that message with you?”) and offers choices (“Want that before you send?”). The clinician accepts the patient’s wording preferences and modifies the draft accordingly.
- Despite these moments, the clinician often moves into directive commands (“Send it exactly as written,” “Send it now,” repeated session closures) and repeats the same instructions multiple times. Decision-making is shared sometimes, but the clinician frequently adopts an authoritative stance. Collaboration is present but uneven.

5. Autonomy Support
Score: 3/5

Explanation:
- The clinician intermittently supports autonomy by asking the patient whether they want drafting help and offering options (breathing, grounding, rehearsal). The patient is given the final choice to send the message and to escalate to HR.
- However, after initially offering choices the clinician repeatedly instructs the patient to “send it” and repeatedly concludes the session in directive language. Those directives reduce the sense of client control. Autonomy support is moderate but undermined by repeated authoritative prompts.

6. Evocation
Score: 3/5

Explanation:
- The clinician elicits a single practical action from the patient (sending the message) and asks whether the patient wants drafting/rehearsal help, which draws out immediate behavioral intentions.
- The clinician does not systematically explore the client’s underlying motivations, values, or long-term goals (e.g., why preserving job/time matters to him, how this connects to larger life priorities). Evocation of the client’s own motivations is superficial and episodic.

7. Partnership
Score: 3/5

- The clinician frames the interaction as supportive and occasionally invites input (drafting the message together). The clinician honors the patient’s specific wording request and adds the meeting line as requested.
- However, the clinician repeatedly takes charge of next steps and closes the session in a way that centers the clinician’s plan (repeated “This session is concluded” and instruction sequences). Overall Global Ratings Score: 3.2/5 (rounded down to 3 for strictness)

Summary strengths and missed opportunities (actionable points)
- Strengths: The clinician kept strong Direction and produced a clear, achievable action plan that the patient accepted. There was an early, accurate empathic reflection and appropriate safety checks.
- Key missed opportunities and corrections:
  1. Deepen empathy and reflection: after the opening reflection, use additional complex reflections (“It sounds like you feel disrespected and worried about what this means for your job…”) and explore emotion and values to build rapport and reduce arousal before shifting to action.
  2. Elicit and amplify change talk: ask open questions to draw out reasons and commitment (“What would it mean for you if this pattern stopped?” “What makes you confident you can follow through with documenting and contacting HR?”) and reflect/summary these statements.
  3. Soften sustain talk: use double-sided reflections and normalizing language (“On one hand you’re angry and understandably see this as disrespectful; on the other, you want to resolve it without escalating in a way that harms your standing at work.”).
  4. Increase collaborative stance and autonomy: after offering a plan, ask the patient to choose next steps and to set their own timeline (“Which of these steps feels most doable to you right now?”), and avoid repeated authoritative commands (“Send it now”).
  5. Use summaries to consolidate change talk and set collaborative action: before ending, summarize the patient’s reasons, strengths, and chosen actions to strengthen commitment.

Final verdict
- The clinician effectively moved the patient to immediate, practical action (high Direction), but did so in a somewhat directive style that missed substantial opportunities to explore motivation, deepen empathy, and strategically cultivate and amplify change talk or to soften sustain talk. Focused practice on reflective listening, eliciting/strengthening change talk, and using double-sided reflections will improve MI fidelity and result in safer, more patient-centered outcomes.
