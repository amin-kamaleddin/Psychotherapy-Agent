# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

---

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Shows respect and nonjudgmental tone with validating and normalizing statements (e.g., safety check, thanking honesty). Acceptance feels somewhat procedural and could use more personalized affirmations of the patient's strengths.

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Demonstrates solid empathic listening and some reflective content, validating worries and following the patient's lead on harm reduction. Reflections are brief and would benefit from more complex, affect‑focused reflections to deepen emotional understanding.

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 5
**Comments:** Keeps the session tightly focused on harm reduction with clear, actionable steps (naloxone, SSPs, tele‑MOUD) and repeatedly orients toward safety goals. The clinician consistently redirects logistical concerns into concrete next steps.

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Generally adopts a joint problem‑solving stance by asking permission and soliciting the patient's input (ZIP, role‑play). At times the interaction shifts to clinician‑driven information delivery rather than co‑creating the plan with the patient.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 5
**Comments:** Consistently supports patient choice and avoids pressure, offering options and framing offers as optional (e.g., asking permission to look up resources). This strong respect for autonomy aligns well with MI principles.

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Elicits basic information and permission to help but misses opportunities to draw out the patient's own reasons for change or to explore what makes the patient a '10'. The interaction relies more on providing resources than on strengthening intrinsic motivation.

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Demonstrates power‑sharing through offers to role‑play, asking permission, and tailoring information to readiness. The clinician could further invite the patient to set priorities and co‑design next steps to enhance partnership.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Supports actionable change with harm‑reduction offers and concrete resources, but does not systematically elicit or amplify the patient's change language (e.g., asking what makes them a 10). Much of the work is informational rather than strategically drawing out change talk.

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Addresses practical barriers and offers safer‑use recommendations that indirectly reduce sustain talk, but lacks explicit MI strategies like double‑sided reflections or affirmations that would reframe sustain talk into change talk. The approach is more directive than gently reframing ambivalence.
