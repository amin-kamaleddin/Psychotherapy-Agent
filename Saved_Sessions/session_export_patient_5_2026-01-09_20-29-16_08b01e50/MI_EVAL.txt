# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

---

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 5
**Comments:** Clinician uses a respectful, nonjudgmental tone and expresses clear positive regard throughout (e.g., opening line and thanking the patient). No dismissive or blaming remarks; concerns are normalized and met with support.

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Some basic validation and acknowledgement of safety worries are present, but few reflective or interpretive statements name the patient's feelings. Interaction is largely informational rather than consistently emotion-focused.

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 5
**Comments:** The session stays tightly focused on the medication switch, washout timing, risks, and concrete safety strategies. The clinician repeatedly brings conversation back to timing, alternatives, and criteria for urgent care.

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The clinician checks in, offers sample questions, and tailors responses to the patient's worries, creating a collaborative tone. However, there is more clinician-provided information than patient-led exploration, so power-sharing could be increased.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 5
**Comments:** The psychiatrist supports choice by offering options (CBT, non‑serotonergic meds, PRN plans) and empowers the patient with wording to use with their prescriber. Decisions are framed for shared planning rather than mandates.

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The clinician occasionally elicits the patient's agenda and offers to help phrase questions, but rarely probes values, motivations, or internal reasons for change. Interaction is primarily clinician-driven information delivery.

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The clinician invites collaboration (e.g., offering to craft questions and asking preferences) and co-constructs scripts and plans with the patient. At times the exchange shifts to necessary directive medical advice, slightly reducing equal partnership.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** There are useful prompts and concrete scripts that encourage action (e.g., asking permission to help with wording), but the clinician does not consistently elicit or amplify the patient's own reasons, goals, or values for change. Change talk is facilitated occasionally but not systematically.

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist validates concerns about stopping phenelzine and redirects toward safety planning and alternatives rather than reinforcing inaction. More frequent use of double-sided reflections could further address ambivalence.
