                                Motivational Interviewing Feedback Criteria (5-Point Likert Scale)

Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)
------------------------------------
1. Acceptance: Extent to which the counselor communicates unconditional positive regard for the client.
   1 = Judgmental, disapproving, labeling
   2 = Inconsistent respect, occasional judgment
   3 = Neutral stance, basic respect
   4 = Generally respectful and supportive
   5 = Consistently respectful, warm, supportive

2. Empathy: Extent to which the counselor understands and/or makes an effort to accurately understand the client’s perspective.
   1 = Little effort to understand, minimal reflective listening
   2 = Limited perspective-taking, mostly factual focus
   3 = Adequate but inconsistent effort to understand
   4 = Good reflective listening and perspective-taking
   5 = Highly accurate, consistent understanding and reflections

3. Direction – Degree to which the counselor maintains appropriate focus on a specific target behavior or concerns directly tied to it.
1 = No clear focus; session wanders aimlessly
2 = Minimal focus; frequent topic drift
3 = Occasional focus on target behavior but inconsistent
4 = Generally keeps discussion directed toward target behavior
5 = Consistently maintains clear focus and guides toward change

---

4. Collaboration – Extent to which the clinician behaves as if the interview is occurring between two equal partners, both of whom have knowledge that might be useful in the problem under consideration.
1 = Authoritarian or expert-driven stance
2 = Limited collaboration; frequent direction by counselor
3 = Partial collaboration; uneven power-sharing
4 = Strong collaborative stance most of the time
5 = Fully collaborative; client’s ideas shape session direction

---

5. Autonomy Support – Extent to which clinician supports and actively fosters client perception of choice as opposed to attempting to control the client’s behavior or choices.
1 = Directive or pressuring; minimizes choice
2 = Occasionally acknowledges choice but inconsistently
3 = Neutral regarding client autonomy
4 = Regularly affirms client freedom to choose
5 = Consistently reinforces client ownership of change process

---

6. Evocation – Extent to which the clinician conveys an understanding that motivation for change, and the ability to move toward that change, reside mostly within the client and therefore focuses efforts to elicit and expand it within the therapeutic interaction.
1 = Provides reasons for change without exploring client view
2 = Limited interest in client motivations
3 = Some evocation but superficial
4 = Frequently draws out client’s own reasons for change
5 = Consistently elicits and develops client change talk throughout session

---

7. Partnership – Extent to which the clinician conveys an understanding that expertise and wisdom about change reside mostly within the client

1 = Clinician actively assumes the expert role for the majority of the interaction with the client. Collaboration or partnership is absent.
2 = Clinician superficially responds to opportunities to collaborate.
3 = Clinician incorporates client’s contributions but does so in a lukewarm or erratic fashion.
4 = Clinician fosters collaboration and power sharing so that client’s contributions impact the session in ways that they otherwise would not.
5 = Clinician actively fosters and encourages power sharing in the interaction in such a way that client’s contributions substantially influence the nature of the session.

---

8. Cultivating Change Talk – Extent to which the clinician actively encourages the client’s own language in favor of the change goal, and confidence for making that change.
1 = No effort to evoke change talk
2 = Rare or weak efforts
3 = Occasional evocation of change language
4 = Frequent evocation of change talk
5 = Consistent, skillful evocation throughout

---

9. Softening Sustain Talk – Extent that the clinician avoids a focus on the reasons against changing or for maintaining the status quo.
1 = Reinforces sustain talk
2 = Rarely shifts focus away from sustain talk
3 = Occasionally redirects toward change
4 = Often redirects effectively
5 = Consistently pivots client language toward change

Evaluation Guidance
-------------------
- All domains are rated on a 1–5 Likert scale.
- Ratings should reflect overall quality and consistency of behavior.
- Focus on both counselor skillfulness and client speech as indicators of MI process quality.

# Motivational Interviewing Feedback: Psychiatrist Evaluation

### 1. Acceptance

Explanation: The clinician consistently communicates unconditional positive regard and avoids judgment. Examples: “Thanks — I hear you: you wanted a change, not to be dictated to. That’s valid,” “I respect your choice and won’t push you to remove it,” and multiple reassurances (“You’re safe; take care”). These statements validate Marisol’s autonomy and stance and counter the dentist’s perceived judgment. Strength: clear, frequent verbal acceptance. Suggestion: continue this stance but occasionally add brief normalizing statements about why people get piercings (e.g., “lots of people want a change”) to deepen acceptance.

### 2. Empathy  
Score: 4/5

Explanation: The clinician shows good reflective listening and perspective-taking but with limited depth. Reflections such as “That sounds fair — you want expert input without being judged” and “That was clear and assertive — good job” show understanding of Marisol’s concern about judgment. Missed opportunities: the clinician rarely explores underlying feelings (e.g., curiosity, identity, or pride in self‑expression) beyond surface validation. Suggestion: use more complex reflections (e.g., “It sounds like you liked the change and are protective of your independence — and you don’t want others deciding what’s right for you”) to demonstrate deeper empathic understanding.

---

### 3. Direction  
Score: 5/5

Explanation: The clinician keeps a clear, appropriate focus on the referral concern (oral health and decision about the piercing) throughout the session. They repeatedly steer toward concrete next steps (ask piercer, monitor signs, consider dental follow‑up) and close with a clear plan: “ask the piercer your questions, check the jewelry options they suggest, and only do anything if they show it’s really risky or expensive to fix.” This maintained focus is clinically appropriate for the referral. Suggestion: when maintaining direction, occasionally summarize the client’s goals to explicitly tie the plan back to their values (e.g., “You want to keep the look while avoiding expensive damage”).

---

### 4. Collaboration  
Score: 4/5

Explanation: The clinician behaves as a collaborative partner: offering options, asking which aligns with Marisol’s values (“Which option feels most aligned with your values and what would you like to try first?”), and letting Marisol choose (“Which would you like?”). The clinician offers help (draft questions, role‑play) rather than prescribing. Minor limitation: some clinician statements move quickly to giving scripted language and specific instructions (drafting questions, exact wording), which slightly shifts control back to the clinician. Suggestion: continue offering practical help but check in more often with open invites (“Would you like me to draft those with your voice?”) so the client’s phrasing and authority remain primary.

---

### 5. Autonomy Support  
Score: 5/5

Explanation: The clinician repeatedly affirms choice and avoids pressure: “I respect your choice and won’t push you to remove it,” “I’m not removing it today; I just want safe options,” and “You’re safe; take care.” These explicit autonomy statements reinforce Marisol’s ownership of the decision. Strength: consistent, explicit support for autonomy. Suggestion: occasionally reflect back the consequences of that autonomy in a nonjudgmental way (“If you choose to keep it, here are safe ways to do that”) — the clinician already does this but could link it to client-stated priorities (appearance, independence).

---

### 6. Evocation  
Score: 3/5

Explanation: There is some elicitation of Marisol’s preferences (choice of option 2, selection of drafting questions, choosing to role‑play #4), but the clinician does limited work to draw out Marisol’s own motivations, values, or reasons for change. Most clinician speech offers options and information rather than exploring internal motivations (e.g., “What matters most to you about keeping the look?” was not asked). Suggestion: ask more open questions to evoke internal motivations and values (e.g., “What about the piercing matters most to you?” or “What would be different for you if you had to change it?”) to uncover latent change talk.

---

### 7. Partnership  
Score: 4/5

Explanation: The clinician generally fosters partnership: they invite choice, co‑create pragmatic steps (questions to ask, role‑play), and request consent for the next move (“Which would you like?”). The interaction is collaborative rather than expert-driven. Limitation: several moments of prescriptive instruction (precise wording to use, specific signs to watch) felt more clinician‑led than co‑constructed. Suggestion: more frequently invite Marisol to craft wording and solutions in her own words (e.g., “How would you like to phrase that?” before offering an edited line).

### 8. Cultivating Change Talk  
Score: 2/5

Explanation: The clinician elicits almost no change talk and does not effectively amplify the sparse indications of openness. Overall Global Ratings Score: 4.0/5

Summary and Actionable Training Points
- Strengths: strong acceptance and autonomy support; excellent focus and practical, collaborative planning. The clinician maintained a nonjudgmental tone throughout and offered concrete next steps the client accepted.
- Areas to improve: elicit and amplify change talk more proactively (follow up conditional language like “might” or “if”), use deeper empathic/complex reflections to explore underlying values, and employ double‑sided reflections to gently soften sustain talk into ambivalence that can be worked with.
- Concrete next steps for the clinician: practice (1) open questions that invite reasons and values (“What matters to you about keeping this look?”), (2) follow‑ups to conditional/change‑oriented language (“What would it take for you to…?”), and (3) double‑sided reflections to highlight ambivalence without pressure.

You handled the client’s autonomy and safety well; with a few targeted shifts toward evocation and reflection depth, your MI will better cultivate and amplify change talk when it appears.
