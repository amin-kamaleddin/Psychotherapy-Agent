                                Motivational Interviewing Feedback Criteria (5-Point Likert Scale)

Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)
------------------------------------
1. Acceptance: Extent to which the counselor communicates unconditional positive regard for the client.
   1 = Judgmental, disapproving, labeling
   2 = Inconsistent respect, occasional judgment
   3 = Neutral stance, basic respect
   4 = Generally respectful and supportive
   5 = Consistently respectful, warm, supportive

2. Empathy: Extent to which the counselor understands and/or makes an effort to accurately understand the client’s perspective.
   1 = Little effort to understand, minimal reflective listening
   2 = Limited perspective-taking, mostly factual focus
   3 = Adequate but inconsistent effort to understand
   4 = Good reflective listening and perspective-taking
   5 = Highly accurate, consistent understanding and reflections

3. Direction – Degree to which the counselor maintains appropriate focus on a specific target behavior or concerns directly tied to it.
1 = No clear focus; session wanders aimlessly
2 = Minimal focus; frequent topic drift
3 = Occasional focus on target behavior but inconsistent
4 = Generally keeps discussion directed toward target behavior
5 = Consistently maintains clear focus and guides toward change

---

4. Collaboration – Extent to which the clinician behaves as if the interview is occurring between two equal partners, both of whom have knowledge that might be useful in the problem under consideration.
1 = Authoritarian or expert-driven stance
2 = Limited collaboration; frequent direction by counselor
3 = Partial collaboration; uneven power-sharing
4 = Strong collaborative stance most of the time
5 = Fully collaborative; client’s ideas shape session direction

---

5. Autonomy Support – Extent to which clinician supports and actively fosters client perception of choice as opposed to attempting to control the client’s behavior or choices.
1 = Directive or pressuring; minimizes choice
2 = Occasionally acknowledges choice but inconsistently
3 = Neutral regarding client autonomy
4 = Regularly affirms client freedom to choose
5 = Consistently reinforces client ownership of change process

---

6. Evocation – Extent to which the clinician conveys an understanding that motivation for change, and the ability to move toward that change, reside mostly within the client and therefore focuses efforts to elicit and expand it within the therapeutic interaction.
1 = Provides reasons for change without exploring client view
2 = Limited interest in client motivations
3 = Some evocation but superficial
4 = Frequently draws out client’s own reasons for change
5 = Consistently elicits and develops client change talk throughout session

---

7. Partnership – Extent to which the clinician conveys an understanding that expertise and wisdom about change reside mostly within the client

1 = Clinician actively assumes the expert role for the majority of the interaction with the client. Collaboration or partnership is absent.
2 = Clinician superficially responds to opportunities to collaborate.
3 = Clinician incorporates client’s contributions but does so in a lukewarm or erratic fashion.
4 = Clinician fosters collaboration and power sharing so that client’s contributions impact the session in ways that they otherwise would not.
5 = Clinician actively fosters and encourages power sharing in the interaction in such a way that client’s contributions substantially influence the nature of the session.

---

8. Cultivating Change Talk – Extent to which the clinician actively encourages the client’s own language in favor of the change goal, and confidence for making that change.
1 = No effort to evoke change talk
2 = Rare or weak efforts
3 = Occasional evocation of change language
4 = Frequent evocation of change talk
5 = Consistent, skillful evocation throughout

---

9. Softening Sustain Talk – Extent that the clinician avoids a focus on the reasons against changing or for maintaining the status quo.
1 = Reinforces sustain talk
2 = Rarely shifts focus away from sustain talk
3 = Occasionally redirects toward change
4 = Often redirects effectively
5 = Consistently pivots client language toward change

Evaluation Guidance
-------------------
- All domains are rated on a 1–5 Likert scale.
- Ratings should reflect overall quality and consistency of behavior.
- Focus on both counselor skillfulness and client speech as indicators of MI process quality.

These behaviors create a supportive atmosphere and likely helped the patient remain cooperative. However, some acceptance statements are formulaic and occasionally inconsistent with the patient’s own words (e.g., opening: “That sounds stressful…” when the patient repeatedly denies being stressed: “Not really, I haven't been stressed.”). A perfect score would show consistently tailored warmth and more individualized normalization (e.g., explicit normalization of ambivalence about alcohol/BP).

2. Empathy  
Score: 3/5

Explanation:
The clinician shows adequate reflective listening and perspective-taking — reflections of goals and strengths occur often (“You’ve already made a big change—quitting smoking…”, “you want to avoid lots of meds…”). Several accurate paraphrases are present. But there are notable mismatches and missed opportunities: the initial statement that the situation “sounds stressful” contradicts the patient’s repeated “I haven't been stressed,” and the clinician rarely probes or reflects the patient’s minimizing tone about drinking. Reflections are often surface-level and repetitive rather than deepening emotional understanding (no exploration of why the patient minimizes alcohol or how he feels about medication dependence), which limits demonstration of high-level empathy.

3. Direction  
Score: 5/5

Explanation:
The psychiatrist consistently maintains a clear focus on the target behavior (blood pressure management) and concrete behavioral steps (20‑minute walks, salt reduction). The clinician repeatedly steers the conversation toward a measurable plan (scheduling walks, calendar/alarms, accountability text) and ties these actions to the patient’s stated goal (avoid meds, better BP). 4. Collaboration  
Score: 4/5

Explanation:
The clinician adopts a collaborative stance: asking open-ish questions, inviting the patient to select what feels doable (“which of those…feels most doable for you?”), and building on the patient’s own past success (smoking cessation). 5. Autonomy Support

Explanation:
The psychiatrist clearly supports autonomy throughout: acknowledging the patient’s preferences (keeping things private from spouse), asking which option “feels most doable,” and avoiding pressure to adopt any specific change. The clinician effectively elicits motivation for the targeted behavioral change (walks, salt reduction) and draws on the patient’s past success to strengthen commitment. Overall Global Ratings Score: 3.8/5 (rounded down to 3 for strictness)

Summary strengths:
- Strong direction and concrete planning (calendar, alarms, walks).
- Good autonomy support and collaborative elements.
- Effective use of the patient’s prior success (smoking cessation) to bolster confidence for behavioral change.

Major areas for improvement (actionable):
1. Address alcohol more directly but respectfully. The patient repeatedly minimizes drinking; the clinician should gently evoke the patient’s perspective (e.g., “Help me understand what makes you think alcohol isn't part of this?”), use open questions, and explore any pros/cons—without lecturing—to elicit change talk or at least resolve misperceptions about alcohol’s role in BP.
2. Soften sustain talk strategically. Use double-sided reflections, normalized ambivalence, and strategic summaries to acknowledge the patient’s viewpoint while gently pointing to discrepancy (e.g., “You want to avoid meds and keep BP down, and you also feel drinking isn’t related—how do those fit together for you?”).
3. Increase empathic precision. Avoid mismatches (don’t label the situation “stressful” when the patient denies stress). Use more complex reflections to capture the patient’s minimizing tone and possible underlying concerns (e.g., fear of being lectured, desire for privacy).
4. Evoke deeper motivation and commitment language. Ask scaling questions (confidence/change rulers), and draw out specifics about why blood pressure matters to him emotionally/role-wise (work, spouse), then connect those values to behavior change including alcohol.
5. Co-create the agenda more explicitly. At the start or when a new clinically relevant topic (alcohol) appears, invite the patient to prioritize topics so that alcohol can be addressed with permission (“Would it be okay to explore alcohol briefly since it can affect BP?”).

Overall comment:
The clinician demonstrates many competent MI-consistent behaviors (direction, autonomy support, collaborative planning) and succeeds in producing specific behavior-change commitments (walks, salt). However, there is a significant, recurring missed opportunity to explore and address the patient’s sustain talk about alcohol. Given the clinical relevance of alcohol for hypertension, future sessions should more actively evoke change talk and soften sustain talk on this topic while maintaining the supportive, autonomy-respecting stance already present.
