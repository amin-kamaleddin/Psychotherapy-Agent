# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

---

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Maintained nonjudgmental tone and validated ambivalence (“That makes sense — it’s okay to like smoking and still want options.”). Offered choices and respected patient stance. Could add more personalized affirmations of strengths and the challenge of shift work to reach a 5.

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Some perspective‑taking (acknowledged safety concerns, discreet use at work) but responses were mainly informational rather than reflective of feelings. Missed opportunities to reflect emotion (e.g., “smoking helps you decompress after a tough shift”).

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 5
**Comments:** Strong, consistent focus on tobacco use and harm‑reduction options (NRT types, dosing, trial plan, costs). Kept session on topic and returned to practical next steps appropriately.

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Invited input and offered choices, but interaction was clinician‑driven with long didactic segments. Could increase co‑creation by asking the patient how options fit his life before giving details.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 5
**Comments:** Consistently supported patient choice and avoided pressure (“it’s okay to like smoking…”, accepting “not promising” stance). Emphasized patient control over decisions.

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Provided extensive information but made minimal effort to elicit patient motivations, values, or reasons for change. Missed open probes to draw out change talk (e.g., “What makes you consider cutting down now?”).

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Framed interaction as supportive and offered help, but remained expert‑led. Could improve by asking collaborative questions (e.g., “How many pieces could you realistically use per shift?”) to transfer planning to the patient.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Patient produced tentative change statements (“cutting down sounds doable,” “I might try the gum”), but clinician rarely reflected or amplified these to build desire/ability/reasons. Should reflect and probe when change talk appears.

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Avoided argument and used neutral acceptance, which prevented escalation. However, clinician did not use double‑sided reflections or highlight discrepancies to actively soften sustain talk; more deliberate reflections could help.
