# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

---

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 5
**Comments:** Consistently warm and nonjudgmental with explicit permission language and repeated respect for the patient’s choice to cut down rather than quit. No shaming or minimizing; clinician normalizes concerns and reassures autonomy.

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Good reflective listening and perspective-taking that captures ambivalence and practical concerns. Could deepen emotional reflection by exploring stress, identity, or the meaning of smoking in his life.

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 5
**Comments:** Clear and consistent focus on the target behavior of cutting down at work with practical options and stepwise planning. Medical details are tied back to the stated goal throughout the session.

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 5
**Comments:** Interaction is genuinely collaborative: the clinician invites choices, adapts to patient preferences, and co-creates plans and backups. Patient knowledge about work constraints meaningfully shapes the intervention.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 5
**Comments:** Repeatedly upholds patient autonomy with permission-seeking, choice of options, and reassurance that the patch can be removed anytime. No coercive or prescriptive language was used.

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Elicits motivations, confidence ratings, and concrete steps that draw out patient change talk. Relies more on medical/procedural information than on frequent open, evocative questions or amplifying reflections of personal reasons.

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Generally respectful and co-creative, asking permission and inviting input, but often positions the clinician as the expert by supplying detailed medical recommendations. More patient-led brainstorming before offering solutions would strengthen partnership.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Effectively elicits intentions, specific plans, and commitment language (confidence ratings, first steps). Missed opportunities to amplify and reflect change language and to draw out deeper personal reasons or values for cutting down.

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Does not confront sustain talk and pragmatically honors the patient’s preference to continue smoking while offering harm-reduction options. Could use more double-sided reflections and explore costs of sustain talk to help resolve ambivalence.
