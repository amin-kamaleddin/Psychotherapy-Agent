# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

---

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 5
**Comments:** Clinician established a clearly nonjudgmental, supportive stance and repeatedly offered help and practical supports without blame. Respect for the patient's choices and motivation was evident throughout the visit.

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** There were several empathic moves—open invitation, safety check, and acknowledgments of stress and triggers—plus tailored coping suggestions. More complex reflections and deeper exploration of the patient's emotional experience would strengthen empathic resonance.

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 5
**Comments:** The session maintained a clear, consistent focus on smoking cessation with concrete steps (med options, quit date, first-24-hour plan) and movement through decision points. The clinician kept the conversation action-oriented and goal-directed.

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Interaction was collaborative—co‑creating a handout, drafting PCP questions, and asking for patient input on next steps. It could be improved by more explicit agenda-setting and checking preferences before detailed recommendations.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Clinician supported choice by offering multiple medication and behavioral options and confirming the patient's preference for patch+gum. The clear recommendation that varenicline is 'most effective' risked a bit of perceived pressure without further elicitation of patient concerns.

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Some elicitation occurred (quit date, past attempts, medication preferences), but there was limited drawing out of personally meaningful reasons, values, or scaling of importance/confidence. More targeted questions to amplify intrinsic motivation were missed.

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The clinician adopted a collaborative stance, inviting choices about the quit plan and PCP questions and affirming preferences. At times recommendations were offered without deeper elicitation of the patient's values that would enhance shared decision-making.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The clinician supported planning and gave opportunities for commitment language (quit date, patch+gum preference), but did not sufficiently elicit or amplify the patient's own reasons for quitting or use importance/confidence scaling. More reflections and targeted prompts could build stronger change talk.

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Practical strategies were offered to manage urges and triggers, which help redirect sustain talk toward changeable behaviors. There was limited use of MI techniques like double-sided reflections or explicit reframing to validate pull toward smoking while steering toward change.
