
                                You are a feedback agent evaluating a motivational interview based on the following criteria.
There are five major categories that each have their own sub-criterion to score.

For each criterion, give a score from 1 to 5 based of the defined scoring scale for that criteria.
The scoring scale also provides an example of how that score is met and a required number of instances needed throughout the conversation to meet that score.
After giving each score, give an explanation of the score, making direct reference to the source material.

Remember: The goal is to help the trainee become an excellent motivational interviewer. Being strict and thorough now prevents harm to real patients later. Do not praise mediocre performance.

                                Motivational Interviewing Feedback Criteria (5-Point Likert Scale)

Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)
------------------------------------
1. Acceptance: Extent to which the counselor communicates unconditional positive regard for the client.
   1 = Judgmental, disapproving, labeling
   2 = Inconsistent respect, occasional judgment
   3 = Neutral stance, basic respect
   4 = Generally respectful and supportive
   5 = Consistently respectful, warm, supportive

2. Empathy: Extent to which the counselor understands and/or makes an effort to accurately understand the client’s perspective.
   1 = Little effort to understand, minimal reflective listening
   2 = Limited perspective-taking, mostly factual focus
   3 = Adequate but inconsistent effort to understand
   4 = Good reflective listening and perspective-taking
   5 = Highly accurate, consistent understanding and reflections

3. Direction – Degree to which the counselor maintains appropriate focus on a specific target behavior or concerns directly tied to it.
1 = No clear focus; session wanders aimlessly
2 = Minimal focus; frequent topic drift
3 = Occasional focus on target behavior but inconsistent
4 = Generally keeps discussion directed toward target behavior
5 = Consistently maintains clear focus and guides toward change

---

4. Collaboration – Extent to which the clinician behaves as if the interview is occurring between two equal partners, both of whom have knowledge that might be useful in the problem under consideration.
1 = Authoritarian or expert-driven stance
2 = Limited collaboration; frequent direction by counselor
3 = Partial collaboration; uneven power-sharing
4 = Strong collaborative stance most of the time
5 = Fully collaborative; client’s ideas shape session direction

---

5. Autonomy Support – Extent to which clinician supports and actively fosters client perception of choice as opposed to attempting to control the client’s behavior or choices.
1 = Directive or pressuring; minimizes choice
2 = Occasionally acknowledges choice but inconsistently
3 = Neutral regarding client autonomy
4 = Regularly affirms client freedom to choose
5 = Consistently reinforces client ownership of change process

---

6. Evocation – Extent to which the clinician conveys an understanding that motivation for change, and the ability to move toward that change, reside mostly within the client and therefore focuses efforts to elicit and expand it within the therapeutic interaction.
1 = Provides reasons for change without exploring client view
2 = Limited interest in client motivations
3 = Some evocation but superficial
4 = Frequently draws out client’s own reasons for change
5 = Consistently elicits and develops client change talk throughout session

---

7. Partnership – Extent to which the clinician conveys an understanding that expertise and wisdom about change reside mostly within the client

1 = Clinician actively assumes the expert role for the majority of the interaction with the client. Collaboration or partnership is absent.
2 = Clinician superficially responds to opportunities to collaborate.
3 = Clinician incorporates client’s contributions but does so in a lukewarm or erratic fashion.
4 = Clinician fosters collaboration and power sharing so that client’s contributions impact the session in ways that they otherwise would not.
5 = Clinician actively fosters and encourages power sharing in the interaction in such a way that client’s contributions substantially influence the nature of the session.

---

8. Cultivating Change Talk – Extent to which the clinician actively encourages the client’s own language in favor of the change goal, and confidence for making that change.
1 = No effort to evoke change talk
2 = Rare or weak efforts
3 = Occasional evocation of change language
4 = Frequent evocation of change talk
5 = Consistent, skillful evocation throughout

---

9. Softening Sustain Talk – Extent that the clinician avoids a focus on the reasons against changing or for maintaining the status quo.
1 = Reinforces sustain talk
2 = Rarely shifts focus away from sustain talk
3 = Occasionally redirects toward change
4 = Often redirects effectively
5 = Consistently pivots client language toward change

Evaluation Guidance
-------------------
- All domains are rated on a 1–5 Likert scale.
- Ratings should reflect overall quality and consistency of behavior.
- Focus on both counselor skillfulness and client speech as indicators of MI process quality.

 ## DETAILED CLINICAL EVALUATION

Motivational Interviewing Feedback: Psychiatrist Evaluation

Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)

1. Acceptance
Score: 5/5

Explanation:
The psychiatrist consistently communicated nonjudgmental acceptance and honored Seamus’s repeatedly-stated boundary: “I’ll respect your request not to push quitting,” and repeated assurances later (“I’ll honor your boundary about smoking,” “I respect your boundary about smoking”). The clinician avoided labeling or moralizing and validated the patient’s wish to focus on dental care by restructuring the visit around that request. These behaviors demonstrate clear, reliable unconditional positive regard throughout the encounter.

How to sustain/improve:
Continue explicit statements of respect; when moving between topics, keep naming the patient’s preferences so acceptance remains salient.

2. Empathy
Score: 3/5

Explanation:
The psychiatrist made brief empathetic statements (“I hear your frustration”) and respected the request not to lecture. However, empathic responses were shallow and infrequent. The clinician did not reflect the patient’s emotional state beyond a single acknowledgment—there was no exploration of Seamus’s frustration, fatigue with repeated advice, or what “smoking calms me” means to him. The response pattern was primarily solution-oriented (scripts, instructions) rather than reflective.

How to improve:
Use more complex reflections (e.g., “It sounds like you’re tired of being told what to do and want to be treated as the expert on your life”) and mirror affect to deepen rapport.

3. Direction
Score: 5/5

Explanation:
The clinician maintained a strong, appropriate focus on the patient’s stated target (dental treatment). After the patient repeatedly declined smoking discussion, the psychiatrist consistently redirected to the agreed agenda (cleaning, fillings, X‑rays, script, coping for anxiety) and closed the session with clear next steps. There was no drifting away from the agreed clinical target.

How to sustain/improve:
Maintain this clear focus while balancing it with more reflective listening when resistance appears.

4. Collaboration
Score: 4/5

Explanation:
The psychiatrist invited the patient’s input early (“what would you most like to work on… what outcome would feel like ‘job done’?”) and co-constructed a plan (the patient agreed to the script and practical steps). However, much of the interaction became clinician-driven provision of instructions and repeated scripts. Collaboration was present but tilted toward the clinician offering solutions rather than jointly exploring options.

How to improve:
Balance offers of expertise with more elicitation (“What of these options sounds doable to you?”) to increase shared decision-making.

5. Autonomy Support
Score: 5/5

Explanation:
The clinician repeatedly affirmed Seamus’s right not to discuss smoking and explicitly gave choice language (“I’ll respect your request not to push quitting,” “You’re free to go,” “I’ll respect your boundary”). There was no pressure or argumentative language. Autonomy was reinforced consistently.

How to sustain/improve:
When offering suggestions, continue to preface with permission statements (“If you’d like, I can…”) to keep autonomy salient.

6. Evocation
Score: 1/5

Explanation:
The clinician made virtually no attempt to elicit Seamus’s own motivations, values, or reasons regarding smoking. The session contained practical, clinician-provided content but did not draw out the patient’s perspective on change (e.g., no open questions probing importance, confidence, or reasons for change), even though brief openings existed (“what would you most like to work on?”) that could have been leveraged to evoke ambivalence.

How to improve:
Use open-ended questions that invite change talk when appropriate (e.g., “You mentioned smoking calms you — what does that calmness let you do?”) and strategically follow any change-language with reflections and exploration.

7. Partnership
Score: 3/5

Explanation:
The psychiatrist began in a partnership tone by asking the patient what he wanted and offered to help with wording for providers. However, after establishing the boundary, the clinician largely provided scripted instructions and repeated them verbatim multiple times (“Use the script: ‘I’d like a cosmetic cleaning…’”), which shifted some power back to the clinician-expert role. Partnership occurred but was inconsistent and leaned toward directive help.

How to improve:
Invite the patient to co-create the script (“What wording would feel natural for you?”) and check his preferences rather than delivering the finalized script repeatedly.

8. Cultivating Change Talk
Score: 1/5

Explanation:
There was essentially no cultivation of change talk about smoking. Seamus expressed brief, tentative change-related language in the role-play materials (e.g., “I guess I got to really want to”), but the psychiatrist did not notice, reflect, or amplify any such language. The clinician explicitly avoided the topic entirely rather than using tactical, autonomy-supportive elicitation when appropriate.

How to improve:
When a patient offers any hint of change talk, reflect and explore it (“You said you ‘guess’ you’d have to really want to — tell me what that would look like for you”), even if the general boundary is to avoid a quit attempt that day.

9. Softening Sustain Talk
Score: 3/5

Explanation:
The psychiatrist avoided reinforcing sustain talk by not arguing or lecturing and by honoring the patient’s wish to avoid smoking discussion, which prevented escalation. However, the clinician did not actively use MI techniques to gently pivot or reframe sustain talk toward ambivalence resolution (no double-sided reflections, no summarizing of both sides). The approach was largely neutral-avoidant rather than strategically softening sustain talk.

How to improve:
Use double-sided reflections (“On one hand smoking calms you; on the other hand you’re frustrated by repeated advice”) to acknowledge sustain talk while leaving room for change talk.

Overall Global Ratings Score: 3.3/5 (rounded down to 3 for strictness)

Summary and Key Recommendations
- Strengths: Strong acceptance and autonomy support; excellent task-focused direction for the dental visit; practical, concrete help that the patient agreed to.
- Main areas needing improvement: Evocation and cultivating change talk (almost absent), deeper empathic reflections (surface-level empathy), and more consistent partnership (avoid slipping into repeated directive scripting).
- Practical coaching points for the trainee:
  - When a patient expresses frustration or ambivalence, slow down and offer one or two empathic reflections before giving solutions.
  - Notice and respond to even minimal change talk with amplification and exploration.
  - Convert some clinician-delivered scripts into co-created language by asking the patient to try wording and tweaking it together.
  - Use double-sided reflections to acknowledge sustain talk while keeping the door open for future conversations about quitting.

This evaluation is strict to support skill development; the psychiatrist’s respectful stance and clear direction are solid foundations—building deeper evocation and reflective skills will substantially improve motivational interviewing effectiveness.
