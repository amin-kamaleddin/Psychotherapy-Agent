                                Motivational Interviewing Feedback Criteria (5-Point Likert Scale)

Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)
------------------------------------
1. Acceptance: Extent to which the counselor communicates unconditional positive regard for the client.
   1 = Judgmental, disapproving, labeling
   2 = Inconsistent respect, occasional judgment
   3 = Neutral stance, basic respect
   4 = Generally respectful and supportive
   5 = Consistently respectful, warm, supportive

2. Empathy: Extent to which the counselor understands and/or makes an effort to accurately understand the client’s perspective.
   1 = Little effort to understand, minimal reflective listening
   2 = Limited perspective-taking, mostly factual focus
   3 = Adequate but inconsistent effort to understand
   4 = Good reflective listening and perspective-taking
   5 = Highly accurate, consistent understanding and reflections

3. Direction – Degree to which the counselor maintains appropriate focus on a specific target behavior or concerns directly tied to it.
1 = No clear focus; session wanders aimlessly
2 = Minimal focus; frequent topic drift
3 = Occasional focus on target behavior but inconsistent
4 = Generally keeps discussion directed toward target behavior
5 = Consistently maintains clear focus and guides toward change

---

4. Collaboration – Extent to which the clinician behaves as if the interview is occurring between two equal partners, both of whom have knowledge that might be useful in the problem under consideration.
1 = Authoritarian or expert-driven stance
2 = Limited collaboration; frequent direction by counselor
3 = Partial collaboration; uneven power-sharing
4 = Strong collaborative stance most of the time
5 = Fully collaborative; client’s ideas shape session direction

---

5. Autonomy Support – Extent to which clinician supports and actively fosters client perception of choice as opposed to attempting to control the client’s behavior or choices.
1 = Directive or pressuring; minimizes choice
2 = Occasionally acknowledges choice but inconsistently
3 = Neutral regarding client autonomy
4 = Regularly affirms client freedom to choose
5 = Consistently reinforces client ownership of change process

---

6. Evocation – Extent to which the clinician conveys an understanding that motivation for change, and the ability to move toward that change, reside mostly within the client and therefore focuses efforts to elicit and expand it within the therapeutic interaction.
1 = Provides reasons for change without exploring client view
2 = Limited interest in client motivations
3 = Some evocation but superficial
4 = Frequently draws out client’s own reasons for change
5 = Consistently elicits and develops client change talk throughout session

---

7. Partnership – Extent to which the clinician conveys an understanding that expertise and wisdom about change reside mostly within the client

1 = Clinician actively assumes the expert role for the majority of the interaction with the client. Collaboration or partnership is absent.
2 = Clinician superficially responds to opportunities to collaborate.
3 = Clinician incorporates client’s contributions but does so in a lukewarm or erratic fashion.
4 = Clinician fosters collaboration and power sharing so that client’s contributions impact the session in ways that they otherwise would not.
5 = Clinician actively fosters and encourages power sharing in the interaction in such a way that client’s contributions substantially influence the nature of the session.

---

8. Cultivating Change Talk – Extent to which the clinician actively encourages the client’s own language in favor of the change goal, and confidence for making that change.
1 = No effort to evoke change talk
2 = Rare or weak efforts
3 = Occasional evocation of change language
4 = Frequent evocation of change talk
5 = Consistent, skillful evocation throughout

---

9. Softening Sustain Talk – Extent that the clinician avoids a focus on the reasons against changing or for maintaining the status quo.
1 = Reinforces sustain talk
2 = Rarely shifts focus away from sustain talk
3 = Occasionally redirects toward change
4 = Often redirects effectively
5 = Consistently pivots client language toward change

Evaluation Guidance
-------------------
- All domains are rated on a 1–5 Likert scale.
- Ratings should reflect overall quality and consistency of behavior.
- Focus on both counselor skillfulness and client speech as indicators of MI process quality.

# Motivational Interviewing Feedback: Psychiatrist Evaluation

Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)

1. Acceptance  
Score: 4/5

Explanation: The psychiatrist maintains a respectful, nonjudgmental tone throughout (“Thanks for coming in,” “You’re handling this practically,” “You’re not alone in this”). They validate Julian’s worry about finishing on time (“I hear you — being worried about finishing on time makes sense”) and repeatedly reassure him they will help. This is consistent and supportive. It falls short of a 5 because the acceptance is somewhat utilitarian and repetitive (mostly functionally reassuring rather than offering individualized normalization of Julian’s feelings or more explicit unconditional positive regard).

2. Empathy  
Score: 3/5

Explanation: There are accurate but surface-level empathic statements (“that sounds stressful,” “I hear you — you’re worried about finishing on time”), and the clinician notes Julian’s anxiety and guardedness. However, the clinician rarely reflects the emotional content in depth (e.g., the persistent mild anxiety, guardedness, and ambivalence) or explores the experience behind the brief phrases (“I guess,” “I think”). Opportunities to name and deepen the emotional content (“It sounds like the deadline feels overwhelming and a bit impossible right now”) were missed. Empathy is present but inconsistent in depth.

3. Direction  
Score: 5/5

Explanation: The clinician keeps a very clear, consistent focus on the target behavior (completing court‑ordered community service and resolving paperwork/logistics). They repeatedly steer the session toward concrete next steps (checklist, 48‑hour follow‑up, contacting shelters, extension request) and do not allow the session to drift. This is an appropriate and effective application of direction for this practical, time‑limited problem.

4. Collaboration  
Score: 3/5

Explanation: The psychiatrist uses collaborative language at times (offering choices: “which would you like to start with?”, “Which would help most right now?”, asking permission to draft scripts or role‑play). However, much of the interaction quickly becomes clinician-led problem solving — delivering checklists, scripts, and concrete instructions. Julian’s input is invited but his ideas do not substantially shape the plan beyond accepting the suggested steps. This is partial collaboration rather than fully shared decision‑making.

5. Autonomy Support  
Score: 4/5

Explanation: The clinician consistently acknowledges Julian’s preferences and gives him control (offering to wait for replies, asking whether to draft a script or role‑play, respecting his reluctance to practice calls now). Summary and Key Recommendations
- Strengths: Clear focus and pragmatic help (checklists, scripts, timelines); consistent respect for autonomy; repeated reassurance and logistical guidance appropriate to the problem.
- Main deficits: The session is heavily clinician-driven and solution-focused at the expense of evoking Julian’s internal motivation and exploring the emotional content of his guardedness. The clinician missed repeated opportunities to deepen empathy and selectively reflect or amplify any small change talk the client offered.
- Concrete suggestions to improve MI skillfulness:
  - Elicit and explore Julian’s reasons and values (even briefly): e.g., “Even if this is for the court, is there anything about finishing that would matter to you personally?” Use open, curiosity‑driven questions.
  - Amplify small change talk with reflective summaries: when Julian says “I’ll send the text now,” reflect and build: “You’re willing to take action now to avoid bigger problems later” to strengthen commitment.
  - Use double‑sided reflections to soften sustain talk: “On one hand the deadline feels overwhelming and you worry you won’t finish; on the other hand you’ve already taken steps like sending the text and setting a reminder.”
  - Limit premature advice-giving: ask permission before offering detailed scripts/checklists (“Would it be okay if I offered a short checklist that others have found helpful?”) and invite Julian to co‑create wording first.
  - Deepen empathic reflections: name the emotion and its impact (“It sounds like the deadline makes you feel cornered and anxious, and that makes calls harder”) to help reduce avoidance.

Be stricter about using patient language and reflections to cultivate change talk in future sessions. The clinician’s practical help is necessary here, but integrating more evocation and reflective listening would increase the likelihood Julian moves from external compliance toward genuine follow‑through.
