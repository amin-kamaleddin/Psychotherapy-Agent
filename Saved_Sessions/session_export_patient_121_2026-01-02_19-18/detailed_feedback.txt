                                Motivational Interviewing Feedback Criteria (5-Point Likert Scale)

Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)
------------------------------------
1. Acceptance: Extent to which the counselor communicates unconditional positive regard for the client.
   1 = Judgmental, disapproving, labeling
   2 = Inconsistent respect, occasional judgment
   3 = Neutral stance, basic respect
   4 = Generally respectful and supportive
   5 = Consistently respectful, warm, supportive

2. Empathy: Extent to which the counselor understands and/or makes an effort to accurately understand the client’s perspective.
   1 = Little effort to understand, minimal reflective listening
   2 = Limited perspective-taking, mostly factual focus
   3 = Adequate but inconsistent effort to understand
   4 = Good reflective listening and perspective-taking
   5 = Highly accurate, consistent understanding and reflections

3. Direction – Degree to which the counselor maintains appropriate focus on a specific target behavior or concerns directly tied to it.
1 = No clear focus; session wanders aimlessly
2 = Minimal focus; frequent topic drift
3 = Occasional focus on target behavior but inconsistent
4 = Generally keeps discussion directed toward target behavior
5 = Consistently maintains clear focus and guides toward change

---

4. Collaboration – Extent to which the clinician behaves as if the interview is occurring between two equal partners, both of whom have knowledge that might be useful in the problem under consideration.
1 = Authoritarian or expert-driven stance
2 = Limited collaboration; frequent direction by counselor
3 = Partial collaboration; uneven power-sharing
4 = Strong collaborative stance most of the time
5 = Fully collaborative; client’s ideas shape session direction

---

5. Autonomy Support – Extent to which clinician supports and actively fosters client perception of choice as opposed to attempting to control the client’s behavior or choices.
1 = Directive or pressuring; minimizes choice
2 = Occasionally acknowledges choice but inconsistently
3 = Neutral regarding client autonomy
4 = Regularly affirms client freedom to choose
5 = Consistently reinforces client ownership of change process

---

6. Evocation – Extent to which the clinician conveys an understanding that motivation for change, and the ability to move toward that change, reside mostly within the client and therefore focuses efforts to elicit and expand it within the therapeutic interaction.
1 = Provides reasons for change without exploring client view
2 = Limited interest in client motivations
3 = Some evocation but superficial
4 = Frequently draws out client’s own reasons for change
5 = Consistently elicits and develops client change talk throughout session

---

7. Partnership – Extent to which the clinician conveys an understanding that expertise and wisdom about change reside mostly within the client

1 = Clinician actively assumes the expert role for the majority of the interaction with the client. Collaboration or partnership is absent.
2 = Clinician superficially responds to opportunities to collaborate.
3 = Clinician incorporates client’s contributions but does so in a lukewarm or erratic fashion.
4 = Clinician fosters collaboration and power sharing so that client’s contributions impact the session in ways that they otherwise would not.
5 = Clinician actively fosters and encourages power sharing in the interaction in such a way that client’s contributions substantially influence the nature of the session.

---

8. Cultivating Change Talk – Extent to which the clinician actively encourages the client’s own language in favor of the change goal, and confidence for making that change.
1 = No effort to evoke change talk
2 = Rare or weak efforts
3 = Occasional evocation of change language
4 = Frequent evocation of change talk
5 = Consistent, skillful evocation throughout

---

9. Softening Sustain Talk – Extent that the clinician avoids a focus on the reasons against changing or for maintaining the status quo.
1 = Reinforces sustain talk
2 = Rarely shifts focus away from sustain talk
3 = Occasionally redirects toward change
4 = Often redirects effectively
5 = Consistently pivots client language toward change

- All domains are rated on a 1–5 Likert scale.
- Ratings should reflect overall quality and consistency of behavior.
- Focus on both counselor skillfulness and client speech as indicators of MI process quality.

# Motivational Interviewing Feedback: Psychiatrist Evaluation

Note: I used the MI domains supplied in your criteria and scored each 1–5. For each domain I report the score, cite concrete examples from the transcript (with approximate counts of instances), and give a brief, concrete corrective suggestion so the trainee can improve.

### 1. Acceptance
Score: 4/5

Explanation:
- The clinician communicates respect and nonjudgment repeatedly (e.g., “That sounds really painful — feeling blamed and isolated would make anyone angry and avoidant,” “Good choice — taking a walk…,” “You did strong practice today — that’s progress”). These validating statements appear across the session (≥6 instances).
- However, some praise becomes evaluative and mildly paternalistic (“You did well handling this,” “That was strong practice — nice work”), which risks shifting to performance feedback rather than unconditional positive regard. There is little explicit normalization of Sofia’s experience (e.g., “it makes sense you’d do X given Y”) beyond the opening empathic line.
- Improvement: Reduce evaluative praise; instead use neutral affirmations that name effort and struggle (“You’ve been handling a lot,” “It makes sense you’d feel guarded after rumors”) and avoid repeated “good job” language that can feel like judging performance.

---

### 2. Empathy
Score: 4/5

Explanation:
- The counselor demonstrates clear understanding early: accurately reflects Sofia’s hurt and avoidant response (“feeling blamed and isolated would make anyone angry and avoidant”) and uses reflective phrases several times (≈4–5 instances).
- Empathic listening is strongest at the start and during rehearsal. Later turns become more directive and summary-driven, with fewer complex reflections of Sofia’s mixed anger/vulnerability (missed opportunities to reflect her guarded minimization — e.g., “I guess I want help” / “not promising”).
- Improvement: Use more complex and layered reflections (reflect ambivalence and feeling underneath the deflection: “You’re angry at being blamed and at the same time you’re lonely and want to reconnect”), and slower, reflective summaries rather than jumping quickly to solutions.

---

### 3. Direction
Score: 5/5

Explanation:
- The clinician maintains a clear, appropriate focus on a specific, practical target (repairing the relationship with Maya / handling the rumor) from the outset, repeatedly steering the session back to a single actionable plan (message drafts, rehearsal, self-care step). Directional prompts and planning appear across nearly every exchange (≥8 instances).
- The focused approach is appropriate given the brief format and Sofia’s goal; the clinician effectively moves the session toward a concrete, proximal behavior (send message or self-care then decide).
- Improvement: Keep the strong focus but balance with more evocation before prescribing scripts (see Evocation/Cultivating Change Talk).

---

### 4. Collaboration
Score: 4/5

Explanation:
- The clinician invites choice and input repeatedly (asks “Which feels doable?”, offers message options, asks to rehearse) — clear collaborative moments (≈6 instances).
- Nonetheless, many turns become instructional (“Decide now to send only one message,” repeated prescriptive instructions about exactly what to do if Maya doesn’t reply). Those directives reduce equal power-sharing and make the clinician appear more directive than co-investigator in places.
- Improvement: When giving options, explicitly ask Sofia to choose wording or to suggest alternatives and reflect back her language before offering a script to increase ownership (“What words would you use to say you miss her?”), and use more “we’ll try this together — what fits for you?” phrasing.

---

### 5. Autonomy Support
Score: 3/5

Explanation:
- The clinician acknowledges choice at times (“Which feels doable?”, “Send when you’re feeling steady”), and offers alternatives (self-care vs. sending message).
- However, the session repeatedly contains prescriptive timeframes and instructions (“send the firm message within 24 hours,” “Decide now to send only one message,” multiple repetitions of the same decision rule). That pattern constrains autonomy and may pressure a guarded teen who already resists adults’ directives.
- Improvement: Replace directives with invitations and permission language (“If you decided to try sending something, what timing feels okay to you?”), and avoid repeated imposition of one single plan; instead elicit Sofia’s preferred plan and reflect her ownership.

---

### 6. Evocation
Score: 3/5

Explanation:
- The clinician elicits some of Sofia’s priorities (asks what would help most, asks who the friend is) and elicits willingness to rehearse — there is moderate evocation (≈3–4 instances).
- Missed opportunities: the clinician rarely elicits Sofia’s reasons for change beyond the immediate desire to reconnect, does not explore the importance or confidence of sending the message, and does not ask about personal values or past successes that could strengthen motivation.
- Improvement: Use open-ended evocation questions to deepen change talk (“What would it mean to you if Maya responded? How confident do you feel about sending a message on a scale 1–10?”), and reinforce any spontaneous change talk with reflections and amplification.

---

### 7. Partnership
Score: 3/5

Explanation:
- There are collaborative moments (offering options, asking preferences), but the clinician often adopts an expert stance by repeatedly prescribing exact wording, timing, and behavioral rules (e.g., “Send when you’re feeling steady,” “If Maya doesn’t reply, send ‘Okay. I tried.’ once then stop”), delivered across many turns (≥6 directives).
- This undermines true partnership; Sofia’s guarded, adolescent stance would benefit from explicit co-construction and acknowledgement of her expertise about her social context.
- Improvement: Co-create scripts: reflect her own phrasing and ask permission to offer a version (“Could I try wording that in your voice? What would you actually say?”), and negotiate rather than dictate the follow-up plan.

---

### 8. Cultivating Change Talk
Score: 3/5

Explanation:
- The clinician elicits some change talk (Sofia says she “kind of want[s] to talk to [Maya] again,” practices firm language), and the clinician reinforces the plan by rehearsing and asking for a specific next step.
- However, the clinician misses chances to deepen and amplify change talk: did not ask about values (why reconnecting matters), pros/cons, or confidence/strategy questions that would move Sofia from contemplation toward commitment. There is also repeated closure before exploring commitment language (e.g., finalizing plan without asking “How likely are you to send this?”).
- Improvement: Reflect and amplify any change talk, ask for importance/confidence ratings, and elicit commitment language (“On a scale from 0–10, how likely are you to send that message? What would move you one point up?”).

---

### 9. Softening Sustain Talk
Score: 3/5

Explanation:
- The clinician avoids explicitly reinforcing sustain talk (does not argue for the rumor, acknowledges avoidance), and sets limits on repeated messaging, which can help contain sustain talk.
- But the clinician does not strategically reframe or use double-sided reflections to acknowledge Sofia’s sustain talk while gently guiding toward change (e.g., “On one hand you’re not promising to send it; on the other hand you do want connection”). The clinician also missed opportunities to normalize her reluctance and then pivot to small experiments.
- Improvement: Use double-sided reflections and strategic summarizing of both sustain and change talk to pivot (“You’re skeptical this will help and don’t want to seem desperate, and at the same time you miss Maya and want a chance to clear things up”), which softens sustain talk without dismissing it.
