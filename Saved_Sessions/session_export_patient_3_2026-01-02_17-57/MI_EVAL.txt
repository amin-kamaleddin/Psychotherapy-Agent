# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

---

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
- [x] Used respectful, validating statements (e.g., “Thanks — that makes sense,” “You’ve earned this effort”)
- [x] Avoided labeling/shaming
- [ ] Provided extended unconditional normalization beyond brief reassurances

**Comments:** Consistently respectful and encouraging tone; statements communicated positive regard. Felt somewhat formulaic and quickly moved to advice rather than extended unconditional normalization.

---

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
- [x] Offered brief reflections/validations
- [ ] Used deep emotional reflections or amplification
- [ ] Explored meaning of feelings (e.g., what “feeling gross” means in daily life)

**Comments:** Some understanding shown via brief reflections and validations, but limited use of deeper reflective listening or exploration of the patient’s affective statements.

---

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 5
- [x] Maintained clear focus on target behavior (starting/maintaining brief exercise)
- [x] Set specific behavioral goals (Tue 6:30 AM walk; Thu 1:00 PM yoga)
- [x] Scheduled follow-up (check back Fri, Jan 16)

**Comments:** Strong, consistent direction — concrete planning, scheduling, and repeated refocusing on the behavior were clear strengths.

---

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
- [x] Invited patient input (asked for times, obstacle, reward, confidence)
- [ ] Co-created solutions primarily from patient-generated ideas
- [ ] Balanced clinician-led and patient-led problem-solving

**Comments:** Patient choices were solicited, but clinician frequently supplied multiple prescriptive strategies; collaboration felt uneven and tilted toward clinician-driven solutions.

---

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
- [x] Acknowledged choice and asked for patient commitment
- [ ] Consistently emphasized patient control without directive prompts
- [ ] Avoided any pressuring language

**Comments:** Some autonomy-supporting language and invitations (confidence ruler, asking for commitment), but occasional directive prompts (e.g., “If you’re ready, say ‘I commit’”) and a phrasing slip reduced full autonomy support.

---

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
- [x] Elicited concrete planning and barriers from patient
- [ ] Drew out deeper reasons, values, or personal meaning
- [ ] Used open-ended questions to amplify intrinsic motivation

**Comments:** Elicited practical change talk (times, obstacles, confidence) but missed opportunities to deepen and amplify the patient’s underlying values and motivators.

---

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
- [x] Asked about patient preference for follow-up timing
- [ ] Framed patient as expert on her life consistently
- [ ] Shared decision-making led by patient

**Comments:** Some partnership elements (scheduling, asking preference), but interaction often positioned clinician as primary problem-solver rather than equal collaborator.

---

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
- [x] Elicited and captured change talk and a verbal commitment (“I commit”)
- [ ] Reflected and amplified the patient’s change talk to strengthen commitment
- [ ] Explored the personal meaning/benefit of change in greater depth

**Comments:** Patient produced repeated change talk and a commitment; clinician secured specifics but underused reflection/amplification to deepen motivation.

---

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
- [x] Redirected focus to change and offered pragmatic strategies to address barriers
- [x] Normalized nerves/obstacles (e.g., “Expect nerves; be compassionate”)
- [ ] Maintained consistently affirming language (notwithstanding a phrasing slip)

**Comments:** Generally avoided reinforcing barriers and used normalization. A notable slip (“restart without kindness to yourself”) occurred but was corrected; more consistent affirming phrasing would strengthen this domain.
