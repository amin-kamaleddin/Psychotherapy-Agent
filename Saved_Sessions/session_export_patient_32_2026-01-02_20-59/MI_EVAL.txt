# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

---

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Clinician consistently communicated respect and nonjudgment (e.g., "I’m here to listen," "Thank you for sharing — that sounds really painful and scary," "That makes sense — getting your license back is a clear, urgent goal"). Kept patient engaged with multiple affirmations. Not a 5 because warmth occasionally felt formulaic and safety framing ("A BAC of 2.8 is dangerously high; I’m concerned for your safety.") reduced purely unconditional positive regard; limited explicit normalization of ambivalence.

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Accurate reflections and summaries matched patient concerns (e.g., "You said you made six months and relapse felt tied to loneliness and the breakup"; safety check). Good listening overall. Missed opportunities for deeper emotional exploration of loneliness/shame/fear of being an outcast — more complex reflections would better validate emotional weight.

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 5
**Comments:** Clear, consistent focus on target behavior (safety/license recovery). Session moved logically: safety check → target selection → concrete plan → rehearsal → contingency planning. Clinician repeatedly prompted concrete steps (boundary, $60 cab fund, cousin standby, script, role‑play) and emphasized immediate safety actions.

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Collaborative stance with choices offered, patient-selected script, and role‑play. Fostered joint problem‑solving. Not a 5 because clinician occasionally shifted to a prescriptive/list-style mode (repeated directives and summaries) rather than negotiating follow‑up or eliciting patient summary of the plan.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 5
**Comments:** Strong autonomy support throughout: offered options, permissive language, and framed actions as patient choices ("Which feels most important to you..."; "If you’d like, I can help draft..."). Safety directives were presented as contingencies, preserving choice.

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Some evocation via offering options and asking which goal to focus on, but limited systematic elicitation of Derrick’s own reasons, importance, or confidence (little probing of "why" the license matters or what motivates change). Recommend targeted questions to draw out intrinsic motivation.

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Generally worked in partnership (invited patient choices, role‑play, asked what support would help). Partnership weakened at times by clinician-led checklists/repeated instructions rather than co-creating follow-up plans or asking the patient to restate commitments.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Clinician noted concrete commitments (six‑week boundary, $60, cousin standby) but did not sufficiently evoke or amplify spontaneous change talk (reasons, importance, confidence). Needs more reflective reinforcement of any change language and explicit scaling/confidence queries.

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Clinician avoided confrontation and validated ambivalence, which reduced sustain talk. Reflections and acceptance helped; however, more targeted reframing and strategic reflections addressing excuses/relapse rationalizations could further soften sustain talk and convert it to change talk.
