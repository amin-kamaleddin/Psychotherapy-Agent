There are five major categories that each have their own sub-criterion to score.

For each criterion, give a score from 1 to 5 based of the defined scoring scale for that criteria.
The scoring scale also provides an example of how that score is met and a required number of instances needed throughout the conversation to meet that score.
After giving each score, give an explanation of the score, making direct reference to the source material.

Remember: The goal is to help the trainee become an excellent motivational interviewer. Being strict and thorough now prevents harm to real patients later. Do not praise mediocre performance.

                                Motivational Interviewing Feedback Criteria (5-Point Likert Scale)

Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)
------------------------------------
1. Acceptance: Extent to which the counselor communicates unconditional positive regard for the client.
   1 = Judgmental, disapproving, labeling
   2 = Inconsistent respect, occasional judgment
   3 = Neutral stance, basic respect
   4 = Generally respectful and supportive
   5 = Consistently respectful, warm, supportive

2. Empathy: Extent to which the counselor understands and/or makes an effort to accurately understand the client’s perspective.
   1 = Little effort to understand, minimal reflective listening
   2 = Limited perspective-taking, mostly factual focus
   3 = Adequate but inconsistent effort to understand
   4 = Good reflective listening and perspective-taking
   5 = Highly accurate, consistent understanding and reflections

3. Direction – Degree to which the counselor maintains appropriate focus on a specific target behavior or concerns directly tied to it.
1 = No clear focus; session wanders aimlessly
2 = Minimal focus; frequent topic drift
3 = Occasional focus on target behavior but inconsistent
4 = Generally keeps discussion directed toward target behavior
5 = Consistently maintains clear focus and guides toward change

---

4. Collaboration – Extent to which the clinician behaves as if the interview is occurring between two equal partners, both of whom have knowledge that might be useful in the problem under consideration.
1 = Authoritarian or expert-driven stance
2 = Limited collaboration; frequent direction by counselor
3 = Partial collaboration; uneven power-sharing
4 = Strong collaborative stance most of the time
5 = Fully collaborative; client’s ideas shape session direction

---

5. Autonomy Support – Extent to which clinician supports and actively fosters client perception of choice as opposed to attempting to control the client’s behavior or choices.
1 = Directive or pressuring; minimizes choice
2 = Occasionally acknowledges choice but inconsistently
3 = Neutral regarding client autonomy
4 = Regularly affirms client freedom to choose
5 = Consistently reinforces client ownership of change process

---

6. Evocation – Extent to which the clinician conveys an understanding that motivation for change, and the ability to move toward that change, reside mostly within the client and therefore focuses efforts to elicit and expand it within the therapeutic interaction.
1 = Provides reasons for change without exploring client view
2 = Limited interest in client motivations
3 = Some evocation but superficial
4 = Frequently draws out client’s own reasons for change
5 = Consistently elicits and develops client change talk throughout session

---

7. Partnership – Extent to which the clinician conveys an understanding that expertise and wisdom about change reside mostly within the client

1 = Clinician actively assumes the expert role for the majority of the interaction with the client. Collaboration or partnership is absent.
2 = Clinician superficially responds to opportunities to collaborate.
3 = Clinician incorporates client’s contributions but does so in a lukewarm or erratic fashion.
4 = Clinician fosters collaboration and power sharing so that client’s contributions impact the session in ways that they otherwise would not.
5 = Clinician actively fosters and encourages power sharing in the interaction in such a way that client’s contributions substantially influence the nature of the session.

---

8. Cultivating Change Talk – Extent to which the clinician actively encourages the client’s own language in favor of the change goal, and confidence for making that change.
1 = No effort to evoke change talk
2 = Rare or weak efforts
3 = Occasional evocation of change language
4 = Frequent evocation of change talk
5 = Consistent, skillful evocation throughout

---

9. Softening Sustain Talk – Extent that the clinician avoids a focus on the reasons against changing or for maintaining the status quo.
1 = Reinforces sustain talk
2 = Rarely shifts focus away from sustain talk
3 = Occasionally redirects toward change
4 = Often redirects effectively
5 = Consistently pivots client language toward change

Evaluation Guidance
-------------------
- All domains are rated on a 1–5 Likert scale.
- Ratings should reflect overall quality and consistency of behavior.
- Focus on both counselor skillfulness and client speech as indicators of MI process quality.

 ## DETAILED CLINICAL EVALUATION

Scoring uses the motivational interviewing global domains provided in the rubric. For each domain I give a 1–5 score and a focused explanation with direct reference to the transcript. Be strict — point out missed opportunities as well as strengths.

### 1. Acceptance
Score: 5/5

Explanation:
The psychiatrist consistently communicates nonjudgmental respect and positive regard. Examples: “I hear your frustration — you want your teeth fixed and don’t want pressure about quitting,” “I’ll respect whichever you choose,” “I won’t bring up quitting.” The clinician accepts Seamus’s repeated statement “I just want to get my teeth fixed,” repeatedly honors his wish not to discuss quitting, and explicitly checks for immediate safety in a respectful way. These moves show unconditional positive regard and avoid labeling or moralizing.

### 2. Empathy
Score: 4/5

Explanation:
The clinician uses concise reflections and acknowledges feelings and priorities (e.g., “I hear your frustration… you want your teeth fixed and don’t want pressure about quitting,” repeatedly reassures “I’m glad you’re not planning harm”). These statements show accurate, timely empathic listening. Missed opportunity: reflections are short and largely factual; there is little exploration of the emotional texture behind Seamus’s weariness (e.g., fatigue, distrust, embarrassment). Deeper reflective statements about his frustration with repeated lectures or the toll of failed treatments would raise this to a 5.

### 3. Direction
Score: 5/5

Explanation:
The clinician keeps a clear, appropriate focus on the immediate target behavior/problem Seamus brought up (dental care). After Seamus resists discussing smoking, the clinician immediately pivots to practical dental options and a specific plan (Dublin Dental University Hospital, HSE services, call script). ### 4. Collaboration
Score: 5/5

Explanation:
The psychiatrist consistently treats the visit as a joint effort. Examples: offering choices (“Would you like practical help now … or would you prefer to end the session and come back later?”), asking what is most important (speed, cost, or long-term care), and creating a co-owned plan (drafting a call script and offering to get phone numbers later). ### 5. Autonomy Support

The clinician repeatedly affirms Seamus’s control and choice: “I’ll respect whichever you choose,” “I won’t bring up quitting,” “If you want phone numbers or exact wording later, come back and I’ll help.” The psychiatrist avoids pressuring language and explicitly defers to Seamus’ readiness. ### 6. Evocation
Score: 1/5

Explanation:
Little-to-no attempt to draw out Seamus’s own motivations, values, or reasons related to smoking or oral health beyond accepting his refusal to discuss quitting. ### 7. Partnership

### 9. Softening Sustain Talk

Explanation:

Missed opportunity: the clinician could have used brief softening techniques (e.g., double-sided reflections that acknowledge his reasons for smoking while gently reflecting dental consequences) to subtly shift ambivalence without violating his stated boundary. Overall Global Ratings Score: 3.8/5 (rounded down to 3 for strictness)

Summary and key recommendations (concise, concrete):
- Strengths: Excellent acceptance, autonomy support, collaboration, and clear direction. The clinician kept to Seamus’s agenda and provided practical, concrete help (local resources and a usable call script).
- Major area for improvement: Evocation and cultivating change talk. Even in a brief dental visit when a patient resists, MI-consistent clinicians can ask for permission to raise one brief link (e.g., “Would it be OK if I just ask one quick question about how smoking affects the repairs you want?”), use brief double-sided reflections, or strategically reflect and affirm past efforts to open a door to change talk without pressuring.
- Specific, low-risk techniques to practice:
  1. Permission + single question: “Would it be OK if I asked one quick question about smoking and dental repairs? If not, I’ll drop it.”  
  2. Double-sided reflection: “On the one hand you want your teeth fixed — on the other hand, you’ve had bad medication experiences.”  
  3. Brief amplification of any change-leaning utterance and asking a follow-up: if Seamus says a hint of “I guess I’d have to really want to,” reflect and ask: “What would make you really want to?”  
  4. Affirmations that acknowledge struggle: “You’ve tried before and it sounds like that was rough — that takes courage to keep coming for care.”

These moves would preserve the strong autonomy and collaborative stance already present while modestly increasing evocation and the chance of eliciting change talk in future contacts.
