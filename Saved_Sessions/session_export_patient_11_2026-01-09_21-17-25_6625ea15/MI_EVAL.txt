# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

---

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 5
**Comments:** Clinician consistently communicated respect and nonjudgmental support, using validating language and normalizing setbacks (e.g., recommending deloads and acknowledging time/injury limits). Tone and wording were affirming throughout the session.

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** There were some brief validations and invitations to share, but limited reflective listening and deeper perspective-taking; the clinician missed opportunities to mirror ambivalence or elaborate the patient's hopes and worries. More complex reflections would have strengthened rapport and understanding.

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 5
**Comments:** The session maintained a clear focus on increasing activity and building functional strength with concrete, stepwise guidance (timing, exercises, progression rules, recovery markers). Frequent summaries and actionable targets kept momentum toward the behavior change.

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist solicited preferences and offered choices (timing, cues, fallbacks) positioning the patient as a partner, but often supplied detailed plans rather than co-designing solutions. Collaboration was strong but somewhat clinician-led.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 5
**Comments:** Clinician repeatedly affirmed choice and supported patient control over pace and decisions, offering multiple options and clear permission to regress or delay progress. Coercive language was avoided and patient autonomy was reinforced.

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The clinician provided useful practical tools but asked few evocative questions to draw out intrinsic motivations, values, or deeper reasons for change. Motivation was supported instrumentally rather than being elicited from the patient.

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** There were collaborative moves (asking about injuries, timing, and setting targets), yet much of the interaction remained clinician-driven with prescriptive recommendations. Greater elicitation of the patient's ideas before offering plans would improve power-sharing.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The patient offered strong change talk, but the clinician did not consistently evoke or amplify it with questions about importance, reasons, or confidence. Change language was supported indirectly through plans rather than actively deepened.

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The clinician avoided reinforcing sustain talk and offered concrete strategies to manage fatigue and setbacks (e.g., dropping rounds, deload weeks, micro-sessions). A missed opportunity was using double-sided reflections to explicitly link the patient's caution with her desire for progress.
