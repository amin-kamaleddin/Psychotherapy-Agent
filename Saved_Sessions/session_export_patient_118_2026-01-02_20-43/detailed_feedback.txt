                                Motivational Interviewing Feedback Criteria (5-Point Likert Scale)

Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)
------------------------------------
1. Acceptance: Extent to which the counselor communicates unconditional positive regard for the client.
   1 = Judgmental, disapproving, labeling
   2 = Inconsistent respect, occasional judgment
   3 = Neutral stance, basic respect
   4 = Generally respectful and supportive
   5 = Consistently respectful, warm, supportive

2. Empathy: Extent to which the counselor understands and/or makes an effort to accurately understand the client’s perspective.
   1 = Little effort to understand, minimal reflective listening
   2 = Limited perspective-taking, mostly factual focus
   3 = Adequate but inconsistent effort to understand
   4 = Good reflective listening and perspective-taking
   5 = Highly accurate, consistent understanding and reflections

3. Direction – Degree to which the counselor maintains appropriate focus on a specific target behavior or concerns directly tied to it.
1 = No clear focus; session wanders aimlessly
2 = Minimal focus; frequent topic drift
3 = Occasional focus on target behavior but inconsistent
4 = Generally keeps discussion directed toward target behavior
5 = Consistently maintains clear focus and guides toward change

---

4. Collaboration – Extent to which the clinician behaves as if the interview is occurring between two equal partners, both of whom have knowledge that might be useful in the problem under consideration.
1 = Authoritarian or expert-driven stance
2 = Limited collaboration; frequent direction by counselor
3 = Partial collaboration; uneven power-sharing
4 = Strong collaborative stance most of the time
5 = Fully collaborative; client’s ideas shape session direction

---

5. Autonomy Support – Extent to which clinician supports and actively fosters client perception of choice as opposed to attempting to control the client’s behavior or choices.
1 = Directive or pressuring; minimizes choice
2 = Occasionally acknowledges choice but inconsistently
3 = Neutral regarding client autonomy
4 = Regularly affirms client freedom to choose
5 = Consistently reinforces client ownership of change process

---

6. Evocation – Extent to which the clinician conveys an understanding that motivation for change, and the ability to move toward that change, reside mostly within the client and therefore focuses efforts to elicit and expand it within the therapeutic interaction.
1 = Provides reasons for change without exploring client view
2 = Limited interest in client motivations
3 = Some evocation but superficial
4 = Frequently draws out client’s own reasons for change
5 = Consistently elicits and develops client change talk throughout session

---

7. Partnership – Extent to which the clinician conveys an understanding that expertise and wisdom about change reside mostly within the client

1 = Clinician actively assumes the expert role for the majority of the interaction with the client. Collaboration or partnership is absent.
2 = Clinician superficially responds to opportunities to collaborate.
3 = Clinician incorporates client’s contributions but does so in a lukewarm or erratic fashion.
4 = Clinician fosters collaboration and power sharing so that client’s contributions impact the session in ways that they otherwise would not.
5 = Clinician actively fosters and encourages power sharing in the interaction in such a way that client’s contributions substantially influence the nature of the session.

---

8. Cultivating Change Talk – Extent to which the clinician actively encourages the client’s own language in favor of the change goal, and confidence for making that change.
1 = No effort to evoke change talk
2 = Rare or weak efforts
3 = Occasional evocation of change language
4 = Frequent evocation of change talk
5 = Consistent, skillful evocation throughout

---

9. Softening Sustain Talk – Extent that the clinician avoids a focus on the reasons against changing or for maintaining the status quo.
1 = Reinforces sustain talk
2 = Rarely shifts focus away from sustain talk
3 = Occasionally redirects toward change
4 = Often redirects effectively
5 = Consistently pivots client language toward change

Evaluation Guidance
-------------------
- All domains are rated on a 1–5 Likert scale.
- Ratings should reflect overall quality and consistency of behavior.
- Focus on both counselor skillfulness and client speech as indicators of MI process quality.

# Motivational Interviewing Feedback: Psychiatrist Evaluation (Seamus O’Connor transcript)

Scoring note: I rate five global MI domains (Acceptance, Empathy, Direction, Collaboration, Autonomy Support). For each I give a 1–5 score and a concrete explanation with direct references to the transcript. Be strict — identify missed opportunities and concrete examples.

### 1. Acceptance
Score: 4/5

Explanation:
- The clinician consistently communicates respect and avoids judgment. Examples: multiple reflective/open statements such as “I hear you — you want the dental work finished and you’re tired of smoking lectures,” and explicit permission-giving (“Yes — you can go,” “If you change your mind and want to rehearse the call, reach out”). These show regard for Seamus’s boundary about smoking.
- This is generally warm and supportive, but somewhat formulaic and repetitive (the same “I hear you” phrasing is used many times). There is no deeper normalization of his frustration (e.g., “It makes sense you’d be fed up after repeated lectures”) that would strengthen unconditional positive regard to a 5. No explicit statements that validate the difficulty of past medication side effects beyond brief acknowledgement.

---

### 2. Empathy
Score: 3/5

Explanation:
- The clinician demonstrates basic reflective listening: early paraphrase (“I hear you — you want the dental work finished and you’re tired of smoking lectures”) and later recaps of the plan. These accurately capture Seamus’s surface stance (wanting treatment, tired of lectures).
- However, empathy is uneven and superficial. The clinician does not explore the emotional tone behind Seamus’s resistance (irritation, fatigue, fear of side effects). For example, when Seamus says “I did the pills… it was a waste and it caused more side effects,” the clinician does not reflect the frustration/fear in depth or ask an open question to probe meaning (“That sounds rough — what was most difficult about those side effects?”). Repeated identical reflections (“I hear you…”) become rote rather than deepening understanding. This yields an “adequate but inconsistent” level of empathic engagement.

---

### 3. Direction
Score: 3/5

Explanation:
- The clinician keeps the session practically focused: concrete behavioral tasks are set (call the dentist today; ask about procedures, timeline, healing, and whether smoking affects outcomes; set an alarm for 1 minute of 4‑4 breathing). That is useful and keeps the visit goal-oriented.
- At the same time, there is an inconsistent focus on the broader change target (smoking). Given the clinical concern (smoking-related oral health), there were missed opportunities to gently link dental outcomes to motivation for smoking change or to elicit change talk about smoking-related concerns. The clinician largely accepted the patient’s “I don’t want to get into this” stance and therefore did not strategically guide toward exploring ambivalence. Direction toward smoking cessation was therefore only indirect (ask the dentist about whether smoking affects healing) rather than an explicit, collaborative exploration. That fits “occasional focus” rather than consistent guided movement toward behavior change.

---

### 4. Collaboration
Score: 4/5

Explanation:
- The clinician behaves collaboratively: asks the patient to choose which of two brief steps to start with (“Which feels most helpful to start with?”), offers options (“If you want to rehearse the call, say so next time”), and frames reminders as suggestions rather than prescriptions. This respects Seamus as partner and decision-maker.
- The clinician’s repeated recaps and ending statements (“session closed,” “Yes — you can go”) sometimes read as procedural rather than jointly negotiated; still, the overall stance is collaborative rather than expert-driven. A 5 would require more explicit co-creation of meaning (inviting Seamus to shape how the dental and smoking concerns fit his values) and more elicitation of his agenda beyond the single practical choice.

### 5. Autonomy Support
Score: 5/5

Explanation:

Overall Global Rating: 3.8/5 (rounded down to 3 for strictness)

Summary — key strengths and recommended improvements:
- Strengths: Respectful, autonomy-supportive approach; practical, concrete action steps tied to the patient’s immediate goal (dental work); collaborative wording and permission-giving.
- Main limitations (missed opportunities):
  1. Empathy could be deeper and more varied — reflect and probe underlying feelings about repeated treatment failure and side effects (e.g., reflect frustration, fear, discouragement).
  2. Evocation of change talk was minimal — the clinician did not elicit Seamus’s reasons, desires, or confidence about smoking beyond accepting his boundary. Simple open questions and amplifying reflections could have generated more change language (e.g., “You said the pills caused problems — what did you learn from that experience?” or “What would have to be different for you to consider change?”).
  3. Sustain talk was not actively softened — while the clinician appropriately respected the patient’s wish not to discuss smoking, there were missed opportunities to use double-sided reflections or to briefly acknowledge both sides of ambivalence to gently tilt toward change (e.g., “You want your teeth fixed and you’ve also had tough experiences with quitting — that makes sense”).
  4. Reflections and empathic statements were repetitive and formulaic (“I hear you…” used many times). Varying reflective complexity (simple, complex, amplified) would increase rapport and deepen exploration.

Concrete coaching tips:
- After a concise initial affirmation, add one empathic probe when the patient mentions medication side effects (“That sounds rough — what about the side effects made you stop?”). Use a complex reflection to validate emotion.
- Use one brief double-sided reflection if the patient expresses ambivalence (e.g., “On one hand you want the dental work done; on the other hand you’re tired of being lectured — both are true”). That acknowledges sustain talk while softening it.
- When offering advice/steps, ask a scaling question (if acceptable) to gauge willingness: “On a 0–10 scale, how willing are you to call the dentist today?” Then reflect either reason for the number or what would move it up.
- Vary reflections from simple to paraphrase to amplified to avoid sounding scripted.

Overall: The clinician maintains a respectful, autonomy-supportive stance and sets useful practical steps, but to be a stronger MI practitioner they should deepen empathic reflections, more systematically evoke and reinforce any change talk, and use strategic reflections to softly redirect sustain talk toward consideration of change.
