                                Motivational Interviewing Feedback Criteria (5-Point Likert Scale)

Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)
------------------------------------
1. Acceptance: Extent to which the counselor communicates unconditional positive regard for the client.
   1 = Judgmental, disapproving, labeling
   2 = Inconsistent respect, occasional judgment
   3 = Neutral stance, basic respect
   4 = Generally respectful and supportive
   5 = Consistently respectful, warm, supportive

2. Empathy: Extent to which the counselor understands and/or makes an effort to accurately understand the client’s perspective.
   1 = Little effort to understand, minimal reflective listening
   2 = Limited perspective-taking, mostly factual focus
   3 = Adequate but inconsistent effort to understand
   4 = Good reflective listening and perspective-taking
   5 = Highly accurate, consistent understanding and reflections

3. Direction – Degree to which the counselor maintains appropriate focus on a specific target behavior or concerns directly tied to it.
1 = No clear focus; session wanders aimlessly
2 = Minimal focus; frequent topic drift
3 = Occasional focus on target behavior but inconsistent
4 = Generally keeps discussion directed toward target behavior
5 = Consistently maintains clear focus and guides toward change

---

4. Collaboration – Extent to which the clinician behaves as if the interview is occurring between two equal partners, both of whom have knowledge that might be useful in the problem under consideration.
1 = Authoritarian or expert-driven stance
2 = Limited collaboration; frequent direction by counselor
3 = Partial collaboration; uneven power-sharing
4 = Strong collaborative stance most of the time
5 = Fully collaborative; client’s ideas shape session direction

---

5. Autonomy Support – Extent to which clinician supports and actively fosters client perception of choice as opposed to attempting to control the client’s behavior or choices.
1 = Directive or pressuring; minimizes choice
2 = Occasionally acknowledges choice but inconsistently
3 = Neutral regarding client autonomy
4 = Regularly affirms client freedom to choose
5 = Consistently reinforces client ownership of change process

---

6. Evocation – Extent to which the clinician conveys an understanding that motivation for change, and the ability to move toward that change, reside mostly within the client and therefore focuses efforts to elicit and expand it within the therapeutic interaction.
1 = Provides reasons for change without exploring client view
2 = Limited interest in client motivations
3 = Some evocation but superficial
4 = Frequently draws out client’s own reasons for change
5 = Consistently elicits and develops client change talk throughout session

---

7. Partnership – Extent to which the clinician conveys an understanding that expertise and wisdom about change reside mostly within the client

1 = Clinician actively assumes the expert role for the majority of the interaction with the client. Collaboration or partnership is absent.
2 = Clinician superficially responds to opportunities to collaborate.
3 = Clinician incorporates client’s contributions but does so in a lukewarm or erratic fashion.
4 = Clinician fosters collaboration and power sharing so that client’s contributions impact the session in ways that they otherwise would not.
5 = Clinician actively fosters and encourages power sharing in the interaction in such a way that client’s contributions substantially influence the nature of the session.

---

8. Cultivating Change Talk – Extent to which the clinician actively encourages the client’s own language in favor of the change goal, and confidence for making that change.
1 = No effort to evoke change talk
2 = Rare or weak efforts
3 = Occasional evocation of change language
4 = Frequent evocation of change talk
5 = Consistent, skillful evocation throughout

---

9. Softening Sustain Talk – Extent that the clinician avoids a focus on the reasons against changing or for maintaining the status quo.
1 = Reinforces sustain talk
2 = Rarely shifts focus away from sustain talk
3 = Occasionally redirects toward change
4 = Often redirects effectively
5 = Consistently pivots client language toward change

Evaluation Guidance
-------------------
- All domains are rated on a 1–5 Likert scale.
- Ratings should reflect overall quality and consistency of behavior.
- Focus on both counselor skillfulness and client speech as indicators of MI process quality.

Motivational Interviewing Feedback: Psychiatrist Evaluation (Sofia Ramirez session)

Scoring instructions followed: five major MI-related categories rated 1–5. Each rating includes specific evidence from the transcript and clear, actionable feedback.

1) Acceptance
Score: 4/5

Explanation:
- The clinician consistently used a respectful, nonjudgmental tone and repeatedly affirmed the patient’s choices and limits (examples: “That sounds like a good first step — thanks for trying it.”; “If you miss some, don’t beat yourself up.”). The psychiatrist checked safety directly and normalized partial effort (“missing some is okay”), which supports unconditional positive regard.
- The clinician avoided labeling or moralizing; they acknowledged Sofia’s reasons for use (stress, fitting in) and validated realistic limits (“I’m not saying I’ll cut back yet, but I’ll see if the log shows” — clinician responded supportively).
- Why not a 5: acceptance felt somewhat formulaic and repetitive rather than individualized. Many of the affirmations were brief or procedural (repeated reminders about the plan) rather than deep expressions of warmth or explicit normalization of ambivalence. A top score would include more individualized affirmations (e.g., naming specific strengths or resilience the patient has shown) and a few more empathic validations of Sofia’s context (family expectations, peer pressure) beyond procedural reinforcement.

2) Empathy
Score: 3/5

Explanation:
- The psychiatrist showed some empathic statements (e.g., opening: “You’re juggling a lot — that makes sense.”; repeated acknowledgements of the patient’s constraints and concerns about privacy). Those lines indicate perspective-taking.
- However, most of the interaction after the opening moved into advice and instructions rather than sustained reflective listening. The clinician did not use complex or amplified reflections of Sofia’s ambivalence (e.g., reflecting both the comfort/use-for-belonging side and her doubts about effectiveness and cost). There were few paraphrases that deepened emotional content (no reflections of frustration, fear, or values).
- Missed opportunities: The psychiatrist could have reflected statements like “I’m not promising I’ll cut back” into a double-sided reflection (“On one hand it helps you chill and fit in; on the other hand you’re worried about money and trouble”) to show deeper understanding and evoke further exploration. 3) Direction (maintaining focus on the target behavior)
Score: 5/5

Explanation:
- The clinician maintained a clear and consistent focus on Sofia’s marijuana use and pragmatic, small-step harm-reduction strategies throughout the entire interaction. The agenda was set early (“Quick plan: 1) Check safety… 2) clarify whether weed helps or causes problems… 3) If you want to cut back… tracking use, set a small goal…”) and every subsequent turn returned to the same concrete plan (9:00 pm reminder, locked shorthand, aim for 4–5 entries, delay/walk/breathing).

4) Evocation (Cultivating change talk and Softening sustain talk)
Score: 2/5

Explanation:
- Weaknesses: The psychiatrist did not actively evoke or amplify Sofia’s change talk. The transcript shows that Sofia volunteered tentative change talk (“I’ll try to log it this week… If it looks bad, maybe I’ll try one less session next week.”; “Okay. I guess I’ll think about it.”), but the clinician rarely asked open-ended questions to explore the meaning, importance, or confidence about change (no questions like “What makes you think it might be worth cutting back?” or “How would that help you long term?”).

That neutral-to-advice response did not reduce sustain talk nor amplify Sofia’s own reasons for change.
- Limited success: The clinician did elicit a concrete commitment to try logging (behavioral step), which is a form of change talk. But given the numerous unaddressed statements of sustain talk and few elicitation attempts, the overall evocation work was minimal.

5) Partnership & Autonomy Support
Score: 3/5

Explanation:
- Strengths: The clinician used collaborative language (“we’ll review what patterns you notice next time,” “Bring your log and we’ll review together”), asked for a commitment (“Can you commit to logging this week?”), and repeatedly emphasized choice and safety (“If you feel overwhelmed or unsafe at any point, contact a trusted adult…”; “I’m not saying I’ll cut back yet, but I’ll see if the log shows up” — clinician validated this stance). Overall summary and key recommendations
Overall composite (average of five scores): 3.4/5 (rounded down to 3 for conservative feedback)

Major strengths
- Clear focus and direction: the clinician stayed on-target and created a simple, doable next step (tracking) appropriate for a brief session.
- Safety check and practical harm-reduction: direct assessment of self-harm and provision of immediate coping options and privacy-preserving methods were appropriate and clinically responsible.
- Consistent supportive tone and normalization of imperfect adherence.

Primary areas for improvement
1. Evoke and amplify change talk: ask open questions to draw out Sofia’s reasons and discrepancy (e.g., “What do you notice about how marijuana affects your school or goals?”; “How would your life look different if you cut back a bit?”). When Sofia offers any change talk, reflect and amplify it immediately.
2. Reflect sustain talk and use double-sided reflections: instead of moving quickly to solutions, reflect Sofia’s ambivalence (“It helps you relax and fit in, and yet you worry about the money and whether it really helps”) and then gently invite exploration of the downside.
3. Increase depth of reflective listening: use complex reflections and summaries to demonstrate understanding of feelings and values (family expectations, anxiety) rather than mainly repeating instructions.

This session was clinically safe, organized, and supportive, and it produced a concrete small behavioral step (tracking). To progress from competent to excellent MI, the clinician needs to shift more time and skill into evocation and reflective listening — intentionally drawing out and strengthening Sofia’s own motivations and resolving ambivalence rather than primarily providing repeated advice and instructions.
