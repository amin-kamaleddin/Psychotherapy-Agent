                                Motivational Interviewing Feedback Criteria (5-Point Likert Scale)

Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)
------------------------------------
1. Acceptance: Extent to which the counselor communicates unconditional positive regard for the client.
   1 = Judgmental, disapproving, labeling
   2 = Inconsistent respect, occasional judgment
   3 = Neutral stance, basic respect
   4 = Generally respectful and supportive
   5 = Consistently respectful, warm, supportive

2. Empathy: Extent to which the counselor understands and/or makes an effort to accurately understand the client’s perspective.
   1 = Little effort to understand, minimal reflective listening
   2 = Limited perspective-taking, mostly factual focus
   3 = Adequate but inconsistent effort to understand
   4 = Good reflective listening and perspective-taking
   5 = Highly accurate, consistent understanding and reflections

3. Direction – Degree to which the counselor maintains appropriate focus on a specific target behavior or concerns directly tied to it.
1 = No clear focus; session wanders aimlessly
2 = Minimal focus; frequent topic drift
3 = Occasional focus on target behavior but inconsistent
4 = Generally keeps discussion directed toward target behavior
5 = Consistently maintains clear focus and guides toward change

---

4. Collaboration – Extent to which the clinician behaves as if the interview is occurring between two equal partners, both of whom have knowledge that might be useful in the problem under consideration.
1 = Authoritarian or expert-driven stance
2 = Limited collaboration; frequent direction by counselor
3 = Partial collaboration; uneven power-sharing
4 = Strong collaborative stance most of the time
5 = Fully collaborative; client’s ideas shape session direction

---

5. Autonomy Support – Extent to which clinician supports and actively fosters client perception of choice as opposed to attempting to control the client’s behavior or choices.
1 = Directive or pressuring; minimizes choice
2 = Occasionally acknowledges choice but inconsistently
3 = Neutral regarding client autonomy
4 = Regularly affirms client freedom to choose
5 = Consistently reinforces client ownership of change process

---

6. Evocation – Extent to which the clinician conveys an understanding that motivation for change, and the ability to move toward that change, reside mostly within the client and therefore focuses efforts to elicit and expand it within the therapeutic interaction.
1 = Provides reasons for change without exploring client view
2 = Limited interest in client motivations
3 = Some evocation but superficial
4 = Frequently draws out client’s own reasons for change
5 = Consistently elicits and develops client change talk throughout session

---

7. Partnership – Extent to which the clinician conveys an understanding that expertise and wisdom about change reside mostly within the client

1 = Clinician actively assumes the expert role for the majority of the interaction with the client. Collaboration or partnership is absent.
2 = Clinician superficially responds to opportunities to collaborate.
3 = Clinician incorporates client’s contributions but does so in a lukewarm or erratic fashion.
4 = Clinician fosters collaboration and power sharing so that client’s contributions impact the session in ways that they otherwise would not.
5 = Clinician actively fosters and encourages power sharing in the interaction in such a way that client’s contributions substantially influence the nature of the session.

---

8. Cultivating Change Talk – Extent to which the clinician actively encourages the client’s own language in favor of the change goal, and confidence for making that change.
1 = No effort to evoke change talk
2 = Rare or weak efforts
3 = Occasional evocation of change language
4 = Frequent evocation of change talk
5 = Consistent, skillful evocation throughout

---

9. Softening Sustain Talk – Extent that the clinician avoids a focus on the reasons against changing or for maintaining the status quo.
1 = Reinforces sustain talk
2 = Rarely shifts focus away from sustain talk
3 = Occasionally redirects toward change
4 = Often redirects effectively
5 = Consistently pivots client language toward change

Evaluation Guidance
-------------------
- All domains are rated on a 1–5 Likert scale.
- Ratings should reflect overall quality and consistency of behavior.
- Focus on both counselor skillfulness and client speech as indicators of MI process quality.

Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)

1. Acceptance
Score: 5/5

Explanation:
The psychiatrist consistently communicated nonjudgmental, respectful tone and positive regard. Examples: opening with “I’m here to listen and support you,” multiple affirmations of the patient’s honesty (“Thanks for being straight,” “I appreciate you giving this a go for her”), and repeated statements that the clinician will not force change (“I’m not here to force you,” “No pressure to quit”). There is no labeling, blaming, or shaming despite the patient’s repeated hostility. These behaviors meet the highest standard for acceptance.

2. Empathy
Score: 4/5

Explanation:
The clinician demonstrated accurate, on-target reflections of Richard’s feelings and perspective: “I hear anger, resistance, and that you don’t want to lose your wife,” and “You’re right to be skeptical.” The psychiatrist acknowledged his routine and what smoking provides (“You want to keep your routine and your wife”) and validated the difficulty of change. Where this could be improved: reflections tended to be concise and pragmatic rather than deeper explorations of underlying emotions (e.g., the clinician did not explore the patient’s anger, identity tied to smoking, or fear of loss beyond naming it). Overall solid reflective listening but not consistently probing emotional nuance.

3. Direction
Score: 5/5

Explanation:
The session maintained a clear and appropriate focus on the target behavior (smoking) and a concrete next step. The clinician guided the interaction toward a specific, measurable experiment (the “pause” ritual), asked 0–10 importance and confidence scales, and set a plan for tracking outcomes. The repeated closings reinforced the same objective (notes, irritability ratings, rituals). The clinician kept the session goal-directed without becoming coercive.

4. Collaboration
Score: 5/5

Explanation:
The psychiatrist operated as a collaborative partner rather than an expert director. Examples: offering choices (“Would you tell me what you like about smoking… Or, if you prefer, rate how important keeping your marriage is?”), asking permission to explore (“Would it be okay if we briefly explore small changes…?”), and co-designing the experiment from a menu of options (gum, walk, pause ritual). The clinician invited Richard’s input about which strategy he would try and agreed to review his notes together—clear partnership.

5. Autonomy Support
Score: 5/5

Explanation:
The clinician repeatedly emphasized Richard’s control over decisions: “I’m not here to force you,” “No pressure to quit — this is information gathering,” and multiple reminders that he can choose whether to call if distressed. The plan was presented as an experiment the patient could accept or decline; backup options (gum) were framed as his choice. Autonomy was consistently reinforced.

6. Evocation
Score: 4/5

Explanation:
The psychiatrist elicited Richard’s own reasons and values (asked about what he likes about smoking, the importance of marriage rated 8/10, confidence rating 3/10) and used those to shape an experiment that addresses both values (preserving routine) and harms. These questions drew out change-relevant material (concern about losing his wife). That said, the clinician could have probed more to deepen intrinsic motives (e.g., exploring what “an 8 for marriage” really means for him, evoking desired future outcomes beyond marital loss). Overall, frequent but not maximally deep evocation.

7. Partnership
Score: 4/5

Explanation:
The clinician fostered power-sharing—inviting choice, offering options, and asking permission to explore. The session generally treated Richard as the source of expertise on his life. Minor limitation: the clinician proposed a fairly specific strategy (the “pause” ritual with lighter in pocket), which risked subtly steering the plan toward preserving smoking ritual rather than fully eliciting Richard’s own creative alternatives. Partnership is strong but not maximal because there was some clinician-driven structuring of the plan.

8. Cultivating Change Talk
Score: 3/5

Explanation:
The psychiatrist created opportunities for change talk (importance/confidence questions, asking about likes/worries, and offering a harm-reduction experiment) and Richard produced some change talk (e.g., “I don’t want to lose her,” rating marriage an 8). Score: 4/5

Explanation:

However, the clinician sometimes phrased the alternative in ways that preserved the smoking ritual (lighter in pocket, same chair), which may have unintentionally validated sustain talk about the importance of the ritual rather than deliberately pivoting to values-consistent change talk. Overall, the clinician often redirected sustain talk effectively but could have used more double-sided reflections and strategic summaries to shift the balance further.

Overall global rating (average of domains): 4.1/5 (rounded down to 4 for strictness)

Key strengths
- Consistent respectful, nonjudgmental stance and strong autonomy support.
- Clear, focused direction with a concrete, low-risk experiment that the patient accepted.
- Good collaboration and evocation of relational motivation.

Key areas for improvement (actionable)
- Amplify and reflect change talk when it appears. For example, when Richard says “I don’t want to lose her” or rates marriage an 8, the clinician should reflect and ask follow-up questions to develop personal motivations (“What would it mean to you if things stayed the way they are? - Use more complex and summary reflections that draw out discrepant statements and highlight change language (double-sided reflections: “On one hand you like the routine; on the other you’re worried about losing your wife…”).

- Probe confidence and past successes: ask about times he managed stress without smoking and how he did it, to build self-efficacy.

Overall impression
This was a competent MI session that maintained safety, respect, and focus and succeeded in negotiating a concrete, patient-agreed experiment. To move toward higher fidelity MI and greater likelihood of internalized motivation, the clinician should more actively elicit, reflect, amplify, and summarize change talk and use strategic reflections to soften sustain talk further.
