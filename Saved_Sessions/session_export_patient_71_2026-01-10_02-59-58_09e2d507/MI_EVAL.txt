# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

---

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 5
**Comments:** The clinician consistently conveyed respect and nonjudgmental support through validating statements and normalizing difficulty, demonstrating steady unconditional positive regard.

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Reflections showed good perspective-taking and understanding of ambivalence, but they were often pragmatic rather than exploring deeper emotional nuance like fear or frustration.

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 5
**Comments:** The session maintained a clear, consistent focus on the target behavior with actionable details (start date/time, alarms, backups) and minimal topic drift.

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Interaction felt like joint problem-solving with co‑constructed plans and solicitations of preference, though occasional clinician-led solutions reduced the sense of fully equal partnership.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 5
**Comments:** The clinician repeatedly supported choice by offering options, asking Laura to select what felt doable, and avoiding coercion, which affirmed client ownership of the plan.

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 5
**Comments:** The clinician effectively drew motivation from Laura using open prompts and scaling, amplifying her intrinsic reasons (groundedness, values) to build commitment.

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist positioned the work as collaborative through questions and choices, but occasional offers to step in with corrective actions slipped toward an expert role.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 5
**Comments:** The clinician actively elicited and reinforced change talk with scaling, reflections, and requests for specific plans, converting intent into concrete commitments.

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The clinician avoided reinforcing sustain talk and reframed resistance into manageable contingencies, though sometimes moved quickly to problem-solving rather than using double-sided reflections.
