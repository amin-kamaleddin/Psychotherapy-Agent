                                Motivational Interviewing Feedback Criteria (5-Point Likert Scale)

Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)
------------------------------------
1. Acceptance: Extent to which the counselor communicates unconditional positive regard for the client.
   1 = Judgmental, disapproving, labeling
   2 = Inconsistent respect, occasional judgment
   3 = Neutral stance, basic respect
   4 = Generally respectful and supportive
   5 = Consistently respectful, warm, supportive

2. Empathy: Extent to which the counselor understands and/or makes an effort to accurately understand the client’s perspective.
   1 = Little effort to understand, minimal reflective listening
   2 = Limited perspective-taking, mostly factual focus
   3 = Adequate but inconsistent effort to understand
   4 = Good reflective listening and perspective-taking
   5 = Highly accurate, consistent understanding and reflections

3. Direction – Degree to which the counselor maintains appropriate focus on a specific target behavior or concerns directly tied to it.
1 = No clear focus; session wanders aimlessly
2 = Minimal focus; frequent topic drift
3 = Occasional focus on target behavior but inconsistent
4 = Generally keeps discussion directed toward target behavior
5 = Consistently maintains clear focus and guides toward change

---

4. Collaboration – Extent to which the clinician behaves as if the interview is occurring between two equal partners, both of whom have knowledge that might be useful in the problem under consideration.
1 = Authoritarian or expert-driven stance
2 = Limited collaboration; frequent direction by counselor
3 = Partial collaboration; uneven power-sharing
4 = Strong collaborative stance most of the time
5 = Fully collaborative; client’s ideas shape session direction

---

5. Autonomy Support – Extent to which clinician supports and actively fosters client perception of choice as opposed to attempting to control the client’s behavior or choices.
1 = Directive or pressuring; minimizes choice
2 = Occasionally acknowledges choice but inconsistently
3 = Neutral regarding client autonomy
4 = Regularly affirms client freedom to choose
5 = Consistently reinforces client ownership of change process

---

6. Evocation – Extent to which the clinician conveys an understanding that motivation for change, and the ability to move toward that change, reside mostly within the client and therefore focuses efforts to elicit and expand it within the therapeutic interaction.
1 = Provides reasons for change without exploring client view
2 = Limited interest in client motivations
3 = Some evocation but superficial
4 = Frequently draws out client’s own reasons for change
5 = Consistently elicits and develops client change talk throughout session

---

7. Partnership – Extent to which the clinician conveys an understanding that expertise and wisdom about change reside mostly within the client

1 = Clinician actively assumes the expert role for the majority of the interaction with the client. Collaboration or partnership is absent.
2 = Clinician superficially responds to opportunities to collaborate.
3 = Clinician incorporates client’s contributions but does so in a lukewarm or erratic fashion.
4 = Clinician fosters collaboration and power sharing so that client’s contributions impact the session in ways that they otherwise would not.
5 = Clinician actively fosters and encourages power sharing in the interaction in such a way that client’s contributions substantially influence the nature of the session.

---

8. Cultivating Change Talk – Extent to which the clinician actively encourages the client’s own language in favor of the change goal, and confidence for making that change.
1 = No effort to evoke change talk
2 = Rare or weak efforts
3 = Occasional evocation of change language
4 = Frequent evocation of change talk
5 = Consistent, skillful evocation throughout

---

9. Softening Sustain Talk – Extent that the clinician avoids a focus on the reasons against changing or for maintaining the status quo.
1 = Reinforces sustain talk
2 = Rarely shifts focus away from sustain talk
3 = Occasionally redirects toward change
4 = Often redirects effectively
5 = Consistently pivots client language toward change

Evaluation Guidance
-------------------
- All domains are rated on a 1–5 Likert scale.
- Ratings should reflect overall quality and consistency of behavior.
- Focus on both counselor skillfulness and client speech as indicators of MI process quality.

# Motivational Interviewing Feedback: Psychiatrist Evaluation (Kevin Ramirez — alcohol-related injury)

Summary judgment (brief): Overall this was a solid, competent MI-style session that stayed focused on a specific, practical harm-reduction plan the patient owned. The psychiatrist demonstrated consistent respect, supported autonomy, and co‑created a realistic experiment (texting a friend, shortcut + reminder). Key missed opportunities were deeper evocation and amplification of change talk, explicit elicitation of commitment/confidence, and more active softening of sustain talk/ambivalence. With targeted adjustments the clinician could move from competent to highly skilled MI.

Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)

1. Acceptance
Score: 5/5

Explanation:
The clinician consistently conveyed unconditional positive regard and nonjudgmental warmth. Examples: “I hear you — you feel embarrassed… and drinking helps you ‘relax and have fun with the guys,’” and repeatedly normalized the plan as “a simple plan you own, not a rule.” No blaming, lecturing, or minimizing of Kevin’s perspective occurred. This tone is sustained throughout the interaction and clearly allowed Kevin to speak freely about both the embarrassment and the reasons he values drinking.

2. Empathy
Score: 4/5

Explanation:
Good reflective listening and paraphrase of both content and affect — e.g., “you feel embarrassed about slipping at the pub,” and capturing the value of socializing (“keeps the vibe and feels doable”). Missed opportunity: reflections were mostly summary/paraphrase rather than deeper, affect‑focused reflections that might have probed the emotional meaning of the embarrassment or the stress‑coping function of alcohol (e.g., “It sounds like drinking helps you unwind after a rough day — tell me more about that” or a reflection that captures the shame/frustration underlying “stupid”). A few more complex or feeling-laden reflections would push this to a 5.

3. Direction
Score: 5/5

Explanation:
The clinician maintained a clear, appropriate behavioral focus (reducing risk of repeat injury while preserving social life). Every question moved the discussion toward a concrete, achievable harm-reduction strategy (options → choice → implementation details → contingency plans). There was no drifting into unrelated topics; the flow logically progressed from ideas (pace, taxi, mate) to a specific experiment (keyboard shortcut, reminder, text to Mike).

The clinician adopts a collaborative stance: frequent invitations (“Which of those feels most doable?”; “Who would be the easiest friend to ask?”), reinforces Kevin’s ownership (“treat this like a simple plan you own, not a rule”), and co-constructs the plan. Slight shortcoming: the clinician occasionally narrows the options toward a specific solution quite quickly (favoring the “text Mike” plan rather than exploring other values or longer-term goals). Missed opportunity: deeper exploration of intrinsic motivators (e.g., what getting home safely would mean for Kevin’s relationships, self-image, or long‑term goals) would strengthen evocation and anchor change in personal values.

Overall Global Rating: 4.0/5 (competent to strong MI with clear areas for improvement)

Explanation of overall score:
The clinician demonstrates strong MI-consistent behaviors: respect, focus, autonomy support, and collaborative planning that led to a concrete, feasible experiment. Shortcomings that kept the score from being higher were a relative lack of deeper evocation of motive and values, limited amplification and reinforcement of change talk into commitment/confidence language, and missed opportunities for strategic softening of sustain talk. Concrete, actionable feedback for improvement
- Elicit and amplify commitment and confidence: after Kevin says “I’ll try it once or twice,” ask a follow-up like “On a scale from 0–10, how likely are you to actually send that text tonight?” and then explore what would move that number up. Ask about past successes (when he did follow through with a plan) to bolster confidence.
- Use more evocative questions to connect the plan to values and discrepancy: e.g., “What would it mean to you if you didn’t have to explain a limp to your mum again?” or “How does avoiding another injury fit with how you see your life in the next year?” This strengthens intrinsic motivation.
- Amplify change talk with selective reflection: when Kevin lists reasons the experiment would be worthwhile (no limping, same fun, better next day), reflect and amplify (“Avoiding another trip to A&E and still having fun — that sounds important to you”), then follow with a question to elicit a commitment.
- Soften sustain talk actively: use double-sided reflections and affirm the legitimacy of the positive aspects of drinking while nudging toward the change side (“You enjoy how it helps you relax and connect, and you also don’t want to be limping — how might you keep the first while reducing the second?”).
- Elicit an implementation intention plus commitment: after co-creating the shortcut/reminder plan, ask Kevin to state specifically what he will do tonight (e.g., “So tonight, you’ll set the 1‑minute reminder and send ‘babysitting duty 😉’ — can you tell me exactly when you’ll do each?”). A brief summary by the patient increases follow-through.

Specific brief examples the trainee can try next time
- After a preparatory plan is named, ask: “What makes you say that number (confidence) — what in your life suggests you can do that?” — then reflect and amplify.
- Use a values question: “If avoiding another injury meant you could keep doing what you enjoy without embarrassment, how important would that be to you?” Follow with: “What would be the first sign for you that this experiment is working?”
- Use a double-sided reflection when sustain talk appears: “On one hand you don’t want to give up nights out; on the other hand you don’t want to be limping again — what part of that would you like to try first?”

Final note:
The clinician did much of what MI intends — eliciting the patient's own ideas, supporting autonomy, and collaboratively designing a low‑cost experiment. Strengthening evocation, amplifying change talk toward commitment, and more actively softening sustain talk would increase the likelihood that Kevin follows through and that the behavior change generalizes beyond a one-time test.
