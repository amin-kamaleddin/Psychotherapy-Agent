# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

---

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 5
**Comments:** Consistently nonjudgmental and accepting (opening “confidential, nonjudgmental space,” repeated assurances about nonjudgmental check-ins, explicit appreciations: “I appreciate your effort,” “I appreciate your honesty and willingness to try this”). Multiple explicit acceptance/affirmation moments across the session.

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Used accurate reflections and perspective-taking (“That sounds like a lot to juggle,” “It’s understandable mornings are hectic…”). Reflections focused on logistics and validation; missed some opportunities to probe deeper emotional content or values (e.g., more exploration of worry about complications).

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 5
**Comments:** Clear, focused agenda on medication adherence throughout. Set a specific, time-bound plan (one-week meds-by-coffee + gentle alarm), used a confidence ruler, and arranged a concrete brief text follow-up and safety plan.

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Engaged the patient in planning (asked “If you’re willing, we can brainstorm”; offered choices and solicited preferred check-in method). Interaction was collaborative though could further elicit patient-generated ideas to increase ownership.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Supported autonomy by offering options (alarm, leaving meds out, sticky note), asking for willingness and check-in preference, and respecting the patient’s limits (“keep it short — a quick call or message”). Some advice given without explicit permission at times (room to prefacing info).

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Elicited some change talk via questions (“How much does worry about complications influence your desire to change?”) and confidence ruler, but did not consistently follow up to deepen or amplify reasons, desires, or intentions.

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Maintained a team-oriented stance (“we can brainstorm,” “I’ll send a brief check-in”), summarized next steps and planned joint follow-up. Partnership was evident though could use more co-authored reflections to build commitment.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Patient offered change statements (“I want to stay healthy,” “I know I should take my diabetes more seriously”) after clinician prompts, but clinician missed repeated targeted reflections/questions to strengthen and elaborate those statements into stronger commitment language.

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Did not confront sustain talk; normalized ambivalence and redirected to small, client-preferred strategies (pairing meds with coffee, gentle alarm, leaving meds out). Fewer double-sided reflections explicitly juxtaposing both sides of ambivalence — could further leverage to resolve sustain talk.
