# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

---

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 5
**Comments:** Clinician used a respectful, nonjudgmental tone and normalized ambivalence throughout, validating the patient’s perspective without shaming. Statements like “That’s a tough spot” and “You’re not promising, and that makes sense” demonstrated strong unconditional positive regard.

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 5
**Comments:** Accurate, consistent perspective-taking and frequent complex reflections mirrored the patient’s language and emotional nuance. Reflections captured competing pulls and invited elaboration, showing high empathic attunement.

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 5
**Comments:** The clinician maintained clear focus on reducing risky drinking while preserving social life and guided the conversation from values to a concrete plan. Each question and reflection tied back to the target behavior and advanced toward implementable steps.

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 5
**Comments:** The session was clearly co‑creative, with the clinician soliciting the patient’s ideas and jointly constructing the plan (choosing Jake, text wording, bringing soda). Language like “Let’s plan one small action” reinforced equal partnership.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 5
**Comments:** The clinician consistently reinforced voluntariness and choice, asking which option felt most doable and respecting reluctance (e.g., “You’re not promising, and that makes sense”). Interventions supported the patient’s ownership of the plan rather than imposing directives.

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The clinician drew out internal reasons and values (protecting grades, reputation, staying in control) and elicited feasible steps, but did not more explicitly probe importance/confidence or fully consolidate intrinsic motivation. Strong evocation that could be strengthened by targeted scaling or commitment elicitation.

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 5
**Comments:** Power was shared throughout, with the clinician eliciting options, reflecting them, and asking the patient to choose a specific step. Co‑construction of implementation details (friend, reminder, wording) demonstrated high partnership.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The clinician effectively elicited and amplified change talk leading to concrete plans (alternate with soda, skip before tests, ask Jake), but stopped short of fully consolidating commitment or assessing confidence. Good elicitation that would benefit from explicit commitment or scaling questions.

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Sustain talk was acknowledged nonconfrontationally and redirected toward planning using double‑sided reflections, which reduced resistance. A missed opportunity was a briefer exploration of the costs of continuing to tip ambivalence more decisively toward change.
