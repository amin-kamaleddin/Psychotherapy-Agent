                                Motivational Interviewing Feedback Criteria (5-Point Likert Scale)

Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)
------------------------------------
1. Acceptance: Extent to which the counselor communicates unconditional positive regard for the client.
   1 = Judgmental, disapproving, labeling
   2 = Inconsistent respect, occasional judgment
   3 = Neutral stance, basic respect
   4 = Generally respectful and supportive
   5 = Consistently respectful, warm, supportive

2. Empathy: Extent to which the counselor understands and/or makes an effort to accurately understand the client’s perspective.
   1 = Little effort to understand, minimal reflective listening
   2 = Limited perspective-taking, mostly factual focus
   3 = Adequate but inconsistent effort to understand
   4 = Good reflective listening and perspective-taking
   5 = Highly accurate, consistent understanding and reflections

3. Direction – Degree to which the counselor maintains appropriate focus on a specific target behavior or concerns directly tied to it.
1 = No clear focus; session wanders aimlessly
2 = Minimal focus; frequent topic drift
3 = Occasional focus on target behavior but inconsistent
4 = Generally keeps discussion directed toward target behavior
5 = Consistently maintains clear focus and guides toward change

---

4. Collaboration – Extent to which the clinician behaves as if the interview is occurring between two equal partners, both of whom have knowledge that might be useful in the problem under consideration.
1 = Authoritarian or expert-driven stance
2 = Limited collaboration; frequent direction by counselor
3 = Partial collaboration; uneven power-sharing
4 = Strong collaborative stance most of the time
5 = Fully collaborative; client’s ideas shape session direction

---

5. Autonomy Support – Extent to which clinician supports and actively fosters client perception of choice as opposed to attempting to control the client’s behavior or choices.
1 = Directive or pressuring; minimizes choice
2 = Occasionally acknowledges choice but inconsistently
3 = Neutral regarding client autonomy
4 = Regularly affirms client freedom to choose
5 = Consistently reinforces client ownership of change process

---

6. Evocation – Extent to which the clinician conveys an understanding that motivation for change, and the ability to move toward that change, reside mostly within the client and therefore focuses efforts to elicit and expand it within the therapeutic interaction.
1 = Provides reasons for change without exploring client view
2 = Limited interest in client motivations
3 = Some evocation but superficial
4 = Frequently draws out client’s own reasons for change
5 = Consistently elicits and develops client change talk throughout session

---

7. Partnership – Extent to which the clinician conveys an understanding that expertise and wisdom about change reside mostly within the client

1 = Clinician actively assumes the expert role for the majority of the interaction with the client. Collaboration or partnership is absent.
2 = Clinician superficially responds to opportunities to collaborate.
3 = Clinician incorporates client’s contributions but does so in a lukewarm or erratic fashion.
4 = Clinician fosters collaboration and power sharing so that client’s contributions impact the session in ways that they otherwise would not.
5 = Clinician actively fosters and encourages power sharing in the interaction in such a way that client’s contributions substantially influence the nature of the session.

---

8. Cultivating Change Talk – Extent to which the clinician actively encourages the client’s own language in favor of the change goal, and confidence for making that change.
1 = No effort to evoke change talk
2 = Rare or weak efforts
3 = Occasional evocation of change language
4 = Frequent evocation of change talk
5 = Consistent, skillful evocation throughout

---

9. Softening Sustain Talk – Extent that the clinician avoids a focus on the reasons against changing or for maintaining the status quo.
1 = Reinforces sustain talk
2 = Rarely shifts focus away from sustain talk
3 = Occasionally redirects toward change
4 = Often redirects effectively
5 = Consistently pivots client language toward change

Evaluation Guidance
-------------------
- All domains are rated on a 1–5 Likert scale.
- Ratings should reflect overall quality and consistency of behavior.
- Focus on both counselor skillfulness and client speech as indicators of MI process quality.

# Motivational Interviewing Feedback: Psychiatrist Evaluation (Jamal Okoro — punctuality/attendance)

Summary judgment (strict): Overall solid MI-consistent approach with clear focus and frequent collaboration; missed opportunities to deepen intrinsic motivation and to more actively pivot sustain talk toward change. Overall quality: 4.0/5 (rounded for feedback purposes).

Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)

1. Acceptance
Score: 4/5

Explanation:
- The clinician consistently communicates respect and avoids judgment. Examples: “Let’s figure this out together — you know your mornings best”; “Laying your clothes out is a clear, practical step — you know your mornings best”; “That frustration with your mom nagging makes total sense.” These statements appeared repeatedly (in most clinician turns) and convey positive regard.
- Why not a 5: language is sometimes formulaic and repetitive (“I hear you…” appears multiple times) rather than offering individualized affirmations that explicitly acknowledge Jamal’s strengths or attempts (e.g., “You’ve already tried things and stuck with X,” or “You showed responsibility by planning a specific step”). The clinician misses a few chances to praise specific effort or resilience that would deepen acceptance.

2. Empathy
Score: 4/5

Explanation:
- Good reflective listening and perspective-taking. Clinician accurately names ambivalence and feelings: “being on time matters, but so does feeling not rushed or nagged”; “That frustration with your mom nagging makes total sense.” These reflections occur across many turns and match Jamal’s terse, frustrated tone.
- Why not a 5: reflections mostly mirror surface content and do not fully probe or reflect underlying emotion/values (e.g., curiosity about why punctuality matters “for me” beyond avoiding nagging, or the emotional toll of repeated conflict). More complex, affective reflections (heightening underlying motivation or eliciting deeper meaning) were limited.

3. Direction
Score: 5/5

Explanation:
- The clinician consistently keeps the session focused on the target behavior (getting to school on time) and moves toward concrete, doable steps. Examples: “What ideas do you have… even one small change…?”; offering specific options (backpack by door, earlier alarm, text); asking “what would be different for you at home or at school?”; scaling confidence and asking what would make sending the text easier.

4. Collaboration
Score: 5/5

The clinician offers to draft the text only after Jamal asks for help and then defers to Jamal’s preferred wording.

5. Autonomy Support
Score: 5/5

Explanation:
- The clinician consistently foregrounds Jamal’s choice and control: asks which small change he’d be willing to try, offers options rather than directives, asks “which small change would you be willing to try?” and “Which do you want: the short message…?”. The clinician avoids pressure or paternalism and affirms Jamal’s right to choose how to proceed.

6. Evocation
Score: 4/5

Explanation:
- The clinician frequently elicits ideas and reasons for change (e.g., “What ideas do you have…?”, “What would be different…?”, asking about confidence and barriers). These prompts successfully produced concrete plans and some change talk.
- Why not a 5: evocation focused mostly on strategies and logistics rather than deeper values or intrinsic motivations — Jamal’s repeated “For me” statements and the persistent theme “mostly so people will get off my back” were noted but not further evoked into intrinsic reasons (e.g., how being on time would matter for Jamal’s own goals, self-image, or future plans). More deliberate elicitation of personal meaning/values would raise this to a 5.

7. Partnership
Score: 4/5

Score: 3/5

Brief recommendations to improve toward excellence
- Deepen evocation of intrinsic motivation: when Jamal says “For me,” follow with an open, curiosity-driven probe (“What about that matters to you?” or “What would being on time let you do or feel that you care about?”).
- Amplify and elaborate any emergent change talk (DESIRE/REASON/NEED/ABILITY): reflect and ask for specifics (“It sounds like not feeling rushed is important — what would a morning where you weren’t rushed look like?”).
- Use double-sided reflections and summaries to soften sustain talk and highlight discrepancy (“You want to get to school on time and also don’t want this to feel like doing it for others — how could we make this plan feel like it’s for you?”).
- Offer more specific affirmations of Jamal’s competence/effort (e.g., “You noticed a thing that helps and you committed to two concrete steps — that shows you’re taking responsibility”), which would strengthen acceptance and support internalization.
- When Jamal expresses barriers (fear mom will lecture), offer brief problem-solving with autonomy (“If she does lecture, how might you handle it so it doesn’t derail your plan?”) to protect the plan without taking over.

Concluding overall score (strict average of domains above): 4.0/5

Final note: The clinician used many high-quality MI skills — collaboration, autonomy support, and clear direction are strengths. The main growth area is intentionally shifting from logistical planning to deeper elicitation and consolidation of Jamal’s own reasons and ownership (moving change talk from “to stop nagging” toward personally meaningful goals). Addressing this would reduce sustain talk and strengthen durable behavior change.
