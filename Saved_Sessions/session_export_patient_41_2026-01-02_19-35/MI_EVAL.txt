# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

---

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Clinician communicates respect and nonjudgmental support (e.g., “That sounds really frustrating — thank you for sharing,” and normalization: “Small steps matter — I believe you can try this.”). Multiple safety checks present. Slightly formulaic and could include more individualized affirmations of patient strengths.

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Accurate reflective statements capture the patient’s experience (“It sounds like avoidance is keeping you stuck,”; “feeling nervous and fearing you’ll mess up is really common”), demonstrating understanding. Could deepen emotional nuance with more complex reflections of shame/fear.

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 5
**Comments:** Strong, consistent focus on a specific behavioral experiment (10‑minute timer, one flashcard/one sentence). Plan is clearly specified, rehearsed, and summarized multiple times, keeping the session targeted on change.

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Clinician invites choices (alarm vs. buddy vs. blocker) and offers to be the check‑in, showing willingness to work together. Interaction becomes clinician-driven in plan construction (if‑then script) with less co‑creation of wording than ideal.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Choices are offered and safety/agency are reiterated (“If you ever feel unsafe…”; “Would 10 minutes feel doable?”). Slightly prescriptive follow‑up schedule and scripted prompts reduce perceived client ownership of the plan.

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Some elicitation of barriers and confidence (patient reports obstacles and gives a “four or five”), but clinician emphasizes solutions rather than systematically eliciting the patient’s own reasons, values, or past successes to build motivation.

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Partnership language and preference-seeking are present, but clinician predominantly assumes planner role (designing exact sequence and nudges). Greater mutual co‑construction of the plan would strengthen partnership.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Change talk is minimal and not actively amplified (patient says “I’ll try” and rates confidence). Clinician moves quickly to behavioral prescriptions instead of eliciting or reinforcing desire, reasons, need, ability, or commitment language.

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Sustain talk and ambivalence are acknowledged (“I’m not sure it’ll work,” “I usually ignore them”), and clinician avoids confrontation. Missed opportunities for double‑sided reflections or explicit summaries of costs/benefits to reduce sustain talk.
