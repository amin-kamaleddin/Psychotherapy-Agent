# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

---

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The clinician opens supportively, uses validating language, and maintains respect without blame, demonstrating consistent positive regard. Acceptance is somewhat procedural and brief with few personalized affirmations or explicit normalization to deepen unconditional positive regard.

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** There are moments of perspective-taking and listening, but most responses are pragmatic and directive rather than sustained reflective listening. The clinician does not consistently deepen understanding of Jason’s feelings or complex reflections about peer pressure and consequences.

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 5
**Comments:** The session maintains a clear, consistent focus on the underage drinking incident and the student-conduct meeting, with each intervention tied to that target. The clinician keeps the conversation on-topic and progresses toward concrete behavioral and administrative goals.

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The clinician works practically with Jason through role-plays, feedback, and permission-seeking, creating a functional teamwork dynamic. However, the clinician often leads and supplies scripted content, so collaboration is strong but not fully equal in shaping strategy.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist regularly offers choices and frames actions as Jason’s decisions, inviting role-play and checking preferences. Some guidance is prescriptive (exact wording and strong advice), which mildly reduces a full sense of client ownership.

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** The clinician does almost no evocation of Jason’s internal motivations, values, or reasons for change and does not ask open questions to draw out concerns or goals. The session provides tools but fails to explore intrinsic motivation or elicit change talk.

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** There are collaborative elements and offers of options, but the clinician largely assumes an expert role by providing ready-made statements and directives. Client expertise about motivations is not sufficiently invited or used to co-create alternatives.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** The clinician does not actively elicit Jason’s reasons for change, values, or goals and focuses on factual logistics rather than exploratory questions. Change language is clinician-framed rather than evoked from Jason, showing weak effort to cultivate change talk.

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The clinician appropriately avoids confrontation and does not reinforce Jason’s minimizing or externalizing defenses, which prevents escalation. However, strategic MI techniques to soften sustain talk (e.g., double-sided reflections or eliciting pros/cons) are rarely used, limiting redirection toward ambivalence resolution.
