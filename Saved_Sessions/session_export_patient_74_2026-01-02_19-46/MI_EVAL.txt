# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

---

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:**
- [x] Maintained respectful, nonjudgmental tone across the session.
- [x] Offered multiple affirmations and praise for small wins (“Great job delaying today,” “You’re doing the right thing…”).
- [x] Normalized ambivalence (“Ambivalence is normal”).
- [ ] Used few individualized, specific affirmations linking behavior to strengths.
- Notes: Supportive but somewhat formulaic/repetitive; multiple abrupt session-ending statements reduced warmth.

---

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:**
- [x] Demonstrated brief empathic moves and reflections (“I hear your ambivalence,” “That ambivalence makes sense”).
- [x] Acknowledged mixed feelings about role-modeling and enjoyment of smoking.
- [ ] Reflections were shallow; missed opportunities for affect-focused mirrors (e.g., deeper reflection of “I worry I’m a bad role model” or what “relaxing” means).
- Notes: Would benefit from more complex, affect-focused reflections to deepen rapport.

---

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 5
**Comments:**
- [x] Kept a clear, consistent focus on the target behavior (smoking/harm reduction).
- [x] Repeatedly offered concrete, achievable strategies (1‑hour delay, post‑meal walks, 10‑minute urge‑surfing).
- [x] Routinely provided resource/referral information (1‑800‑QUIT‑NOW, health dept, insurers, NRT).
- Notes: Strong, sustained direction toward behavior change.

---

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:**
- [x] Invited a small experiment (“Would you try a small experiment: delay first cigarette one hour?”).
- [x] Asked about the patient’s experience of the experiment (“How did your first delayed cigarette go today?”).
- [ ] Interaction often became clinician-driven and prescriptive; limited co‑creation of options.
- Notes: Some collaborative moments, but clinician frequently defaults to expert/advice-giving.

---

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:**
- [x] Occasionally acknowledged choice (offered an “experiment,” phrased quitline as “if helpful”).
- [ ] Frequently used prescriptive language and corrective statements (e.g., asserting NRT benefits) that could undermine perceived autonomy.
- Notes: More explicit autonomy-supportive phrasing and asking permission before advice would strengthen this domain.

---

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:**
- [x] Elicited prior attempts and concrete behavior change (asked what patient tried before; asked about delayed cigarette).
- [ ] Limited effort to evoke deeper reasons, values, importance, or goals related to change beyond the surface (child role-modeling not deeply explored).
- Notes: More open, evocative questions would amplify intrinsic motivation.

---

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:**
- [x] Demonstrated moments of partnership (inviting the experiment, checking results).
- [ ] Predominantly in expert/teacher role with repeated directives and session-closure statements.
- Notes: Share ideas briefly then ask patient preference to increase genuine partnership.

---

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:**
- [x] Reinforced early change behavior and progress (praised delays; acknowledged use of distractions/walks).
- [ ] Did not consistently elicit or amplify desire, ability, reasons, need, or commitment language; limited escalation toward commitment.
- Notes: Use reflections and targeted evocative questions to draw out and strengthen change talk.

---

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:**
- [x] Avoided overtly arguing; attempted to reframe NRT beliefs and address cost concerns with information.
- [ ] Responses were sometimes corrective/didactic (“NRT isn’t swapping one dangerous habit for another”), which can feel like counter‑arguing and risk strengthening resistance.
- Notes: Use double-sided reflections and validate sustain reasons before offering information to gently soften sustain talk.
