# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

---

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 5
**Comments:** Consistently communicates unconditional positive regard—validates concerns, normalizes side‑effect worries, affirms small steps, and avoids judgment throughout the session.

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Demonstrates strong reflective listening and perspective‑taking, accurately summarizing conflicts, but misses some opportunities to explore deeper emotions and affect‑level meaning.

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 5
**Comments:** Maintains clear focus on cholesterol/medication hesitancy and moves the session from exploration to concrete planning with specific goals, safety monitoring, and follow‑up.

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 5
**Comments:** Interaction reflects an equal partnership—clinician solicits patient input on goals, scheduling, barriers, and co‑creates practical supports and messaging for the doctor.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 5
**Comments:** Consistently reinforces choice and control by offering options, framing the plan as reversible, asking preferences, and avoiding pressure to start medication.

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Elicits some reasons for change (family, preferring lifestyle) and a plan, but does not deeply draw out intrinsic motivations or amplify personal reasons for change.

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 5
**Comments:** Consistently frames the work as collaborative—asks permission, offers options, invites edits, and respects the patient's expertise about his life and priorities.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Effectively elicits and reinforces change talk and secures a concrete commitment with a confidence rating, but could further probe why change matters to deepen motivation and amplify change language.

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Handles sustain talk well by acknowledging worries and offering alternatives and monitoring, though could use more advanced techniques (e.g., double‑sided reflections) to shift emphasis toward change.
