# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

---

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Clinician maintained a respectful, nonjudgmental tone (“I’m glad you came in,” “That makes sense”) and validated Jason’s priorities (avoiding a conduct note/parent notification). Acceptance sometimes shifted quickly into managerial problem‑solving rather than deeper normalization of Jason’s feelings.

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Frequent accurate paraphrases and reflections demonstrated understanding of Jason’s perspective (e.g., reflecting that the incident felt “blown out of proportion” and concerns about parents/conduct record). Reflections were mostly content‑focused rather than probing deeper emotions like shame or anxiety.

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The session was clearly task‑focused (deciding focus, rehearsing scripts, planning responses to parent/record threats) and the clinician repeatedly steered agenda toward rehearsal. Direction was narrowly procedural and did not also guide toward eliciting longer‑term goals or consequences.

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Clinician invited choice (what to focus on, whether to rehearse) and worked with Jason in role‑play. At times the clinician supplied wording and solutions rather than co‑creating phrasing from Jason, introducing an expert‑driven element.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Options were offered and Jason’s preferences were solicited (script vs. rehearsal; how to respond to parent/record threats). Autonomy could be strengthened by eliciting Jason’s own language before providing prescriptive scripts.

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Some evocative prompts were used (choice of focus, confidence 0–10), but the encounter was primarily clinician‑led with advice and scripts. Limited elicitation of Jason’s underlying motivations, values, or reasons for change.

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The clinician adopted a cooperative stance (role‑play together, iterative refinement of script) and treated Jason as a collaborator. Partnership was solid but leaned toward clinician leadership in crafting responses rather than fully co‑creating language.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Little change talk was elicited or amplified; Jason’s statements were mostly procedural (“I’ll use that script,” “I’ll offer education/community service”). The clinician missed opportunities to probe desirability, importance, or commitment to avoid future incidents.

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Clinician generally rolled with minimizations (didn’t argue) and avoided escalation, but did not consistently redirect sustain talk toward change using double‑sided reflections or discrepant summaries. Some subtle redirections occurred (suggesting educational alternative), but more strategic softening was possible.
