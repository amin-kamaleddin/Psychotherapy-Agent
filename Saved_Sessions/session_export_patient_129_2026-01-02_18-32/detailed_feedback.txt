                                Motivational Interviewing Feedback Criteria (5-Point Likert Scale)

Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)
------------------------------------
1. Acceptance: Extent to which the counselor communicates unconditional positive regard for the client.
   1 = Judgmental, disapproving, labeling
   2 = Inconsistent respect, occasional judgment
   3 = Neutral stance, basic respect
   4 = Generally respectful and supportive
   5 = Consistently respectful, warm, supportive

2. Empathy: Extent to which the counselor understands and/or makes an effort to accurately understand the client’s perspective.
   1 = Little effort to understand, minimal reflective listening
   2 = Limited perspective-taking, mostly factual focus
   3 = Adequate but inconsistent effort to understand
   4 = Good reflective listening and perspective-taking
   5 = Highly accurate, consistent understanding and reflections

3. Direction – Degree to which the counselor maintains appropriate focus on a specific target behavior or concerns directly tied to it.
1 = No clear focus; session wanders aimlessly
2 = Minimal focus; frequent topic drift
3 = Occasional focus on target behavior but inconsistent
4 = Generally keeps discussion directed toward target behavior
5 = Consistently maintains clear focus and guides toward change

---

4. Collaboration – Extent to which the clinician behaves as if the interview is occurring between two equal partners, both of whom have knowledge that might be useful in the problem under consideration.
1 = Authoritarian or expert-driven stance
2 = Limited collaboration; frequent direction by counselor
3 = Partial collaboration; uneven power-sharing
4 = Strong collaborative stance most of the time
5 = Fully collaborative; client’s ideas shape session direction

---

5. Autonomy Support – Extent to which clinician supports and actively fosters client perception of choice as opposed to attempting to control the client’s behavior or choices.
1 = Directive or pressuring; minimizes choice
2 = Occasionally acknowledges choice but inconsistently
3 = Neutral regarding client autonomy
4 = Regularly affirms client freedom to choose
5 = Consistently reinforces client ownership of change process

---

6. Evocation – Extent to which the clinician conveys an understanding that motivation for change, and the ability to move toward that change, reside mostly within the client and therefore focuses efforts to elicit and expand it within the therapeutic interaction.
1 = Provides reasons for change without exploring client view
2 = Limited interest in client motivations
3 = Some evocation but superficial
4 = Frequently draws out client’s own reasons for change
5 = Consistently elicits and develops client change talk throughout session

---

7. Partnership – Extent to which the clinician conveys an understanding that expertise and wisdom about change reside mostly within the client

1 = Clinician actively assumes the expert role for the majority of the interaction with the client. Collaboration or partnership is absent.
2 = Clinician superficially responds to opportunities to collaborate.
3 = Clinician incorporates client’s contributions but does so in a lukewarm or erratic fashion.
4 = Clinician fosters collaboration and power sharing so that client’s contributions impact the session in ways that they otherwise would not.
5 = Clinician actively fosters and encourages power sharing in the interaction in such a way that client’s contributions substantially influence the nature of the session.

---

8. Cultivating Change Talk – Extent to which the clinician actively encourages the client’s own language in favor of the change goal, and confidence for making that change.
1 = No effort to evoke change talk
2 = Rare or weak efforts
3 = Occasional evocation of change language
4 = Frequent evocation of change talk
5 = Consistent, skillful evocation throughout

---

9. Softening Sustain Talk – Extent that the clinician avoids a focus on the reasons against changing or for maintaining the status quo.
1 = Reinforces sustain talk
2 = Rarely shifts focus away from sustain talk
3 = Occasionally redirects toward change
4 = Often redirects effectively
5 = Consistently pivots client language toward change

Evaluation Guidance
-------------------
- All domains are rated on a 1–5 Likert scale.
- Ratings should reflect overall quality and consistency of behavior.
- Focus on both counselor skillfulness and client speech as indicators of MI process quality.

# Motivational Interviewing Feedback: Psychiatrist Evaluation (Tyler Johnson Case)

### Summary statement
The clinician did well at quickly establishing safety-focused, practical harm-reduction steps (securing pills, no use in house, no driving) and used collaborative scripting to defuse immediate family conflict. Those actions likely reduced near-term risk for Tyler. However, the clinician missed multiple concrete opportunities to elicit Tyler’s own motivations, explore ambivalence, and deepen change talk. Overall the interaction is competent for brief crisis-focused harm reduction but falls short of a full MI-consistent evocation and exploration of motivation.

## Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)

1. Acceptance
Score: 5/5

Explanation:
The psychiatrist consistently maintained a respectful, nonjudgmental tone and validated Tyler’s perspective. Examples: “Thanks for being straight with me — that makes this useful,” “I hear you — you want the drama to stop,” and “I respect that you’re not ready to quit.” The clinician avoided moralizing or blaming and affirmed Tyler’s stated priorities (calming his mom). These behaviors demonstrate clear unconditional positive regard.

2. Empathy
Score: 4/5

Explanation:
There are several accurate, concise reflections capturing Tyler’s stance and feelings (e.g., “I hear you — you want the drama to stop,” “That sounded calm — nice job”). The clinician recognized Tyler’s resistance and prioritized what he wanted (short-term calm). However, empathy could be deeper and more exploratory — for example reflecting Tyler’s guardedness, shame, or peer-related motivations (“sounds like your friends are important to you” or “that reaction from your mom felt over the top and maybe embarrassing”) were not probed. Several good empathic moments, but not consistently probing emotional nuance.

3. Direction
Score: 5/5

Explanation:
The clinician maintained clear focus on immediate, behaviorally specific targets relevant to safety: what was in the stash, current use pattern, and concrete risk-reduction steps (secure pills, no in-home use, no driving). The clinician repeatedly brought the conversation back to these limits and practiced a script Tyler could use with his mom. Session direction matched Tyler’s stated goal (calming the mom) and prioritized risk reduction.

4. Collaboration
Score: 5/5

Explanation:
The clinician engaged Tyler as a partner: asked what Tyler wanted to focus on (“calming things with your mom… or talk about risks?”), offered two script options and let Tyler choose, asked permission to practice, and co-developed specific language. This aligns well with a collaborative MI stance — Tyler’s choices shaped the content and plan.

5. Autonomy Support
Score: 5/5

Explanation:
The clinician repeatedly affirmed Tyler’s control and did not pressure for long-term change (“You don’t have to promise long-term change now,” “I respect that you’re not ready to quit”). Recommendations were framed as options and mitigations rather than demands. Autonomy was clearly supported.

6. Evocation
Score: 3/5

Explanation:
There was some limited elicitation (opening question, asking frequency of use, asking which focus Tyler preferred), but the clinician mostly offered solutions and safety advice rather than drawing out Tyler’s own reasons for change or exploring his values. For example, after Tyler said he wanted the drama to stop, the clinician moved directly to scripted language and behavioral steps rather than asking evocative questions such as “What do you like about hanging out with this crowd?” or “What worries you, if anything, about using?” The clinician therefore evoked some relevant information but did not develop intrinsic motivation.

Overall Global Rating (average of domains): 4.0/5

Final appraisal (concise):
Strengths — clear nonjudgmental acceptance, strong safety-focused direction, good collaboration and autonomy support, practical harm-reduction outcomes (Tyler agreed to secure pills and not drive/use in the house). These are appropriate priorities for a brief, safety-oriented visit.

Major areas for improvement — the clinician underutilized core MI evocation skills and missed repeated opportunities to elicit and amplify Tyler’s own reasons for change (or even subtle ambivalence). This limits the session’s potential to move Tyler beyond immediate compliance toward intrinsic motivation or commitment to safer choices. The clinician should more often use open evocative questions, reflective listening that explores absolute and ambivalent statements, and strategic summaries to link Tyler’s values/concerns to behavior.

Concrete recommendations (phrases and techniques to use next time)
- After Tyler says he wants the drama to stop, follow with an evocative question: “What about the drama bothers you the most?” or “How would things be different if that stopped?” — to elicit reasons for change.
- Use scaling questions to elicit importance/confidence: “On a scale of 0–10, how important is avoiding driving after using?” then follow with “Why a 4 and not a 0?” to elicit change talk.
- Use double-sided reflections to acknowledge sustain talk and contrast it with any concern: “You don’t want to quit and you want things to calm down — on the other hand you’re willing to secure pills so things don’t blow up.”
- Elicit commitment language before closing when possible: “What specifically will you do when your mom starts to escalate? Would you be willing to say that script now and name one small step you’ll take if she gets loud?”
- Briefly explore peer influences with curiosity, not judgment: “Tell me about this ‘cool crowd’ — what do you like about hanging out with them?”

Be strict in future feedback: while harm reduction was well handled, insufficient evocation risks missing chances to support durable behavior change.
