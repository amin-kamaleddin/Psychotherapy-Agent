# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

---

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Clinician uses a respectful, nonjudgmental tone, normalizes difficulty, and affirms the patient's request while addressing safety concerns. Points were deducted because responses often shift to transactional information (medication lists, thresholds) that reduce a warmer, personalized acceptance.

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** There is some empathic attunement—inviting the patient's concerns and acknowledging difficulty—but reflections are limited and frequently followed by information-giving. The clinician seldom deepens or mirrors the patient's emotional language to explore feelings more fully.

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 5
**Comments:** The clinician maintains a clear, focused agenda on smoking cessation with concrete, actionable steps (quit date, meds, monitoring, prescriber message). Each exchange advances the plan and keeps the session goal-oriented.

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 5
**Comments:** The interaction is strongly collaborative: the psychiatrist co-constructs the prescriber message, repeatedly checks the patient's preferences, and invites input at each step. Decisions and wording are clearly negotiated with the patient.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The clinician supports autonomy by offering choices, clarifying limits to prescribing, and asking which items to include in the message. Deductions reflect moments of firm recommendations and concrete thresholds provided without explicitly asking how involved the patient wants to be.

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Some needs and requests are elicited, but the clinician relies heavily on information-giving and planning rather than drawing out the patient's own reasons, values, or confidence. Few evocative open questions or scaling prompts are used to deepen internal motivation.

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The clinician generally adopts a collaborative stance—seeking medical details and incorporating patient preferences—while offering help with messaging and planning. Points were lost because the clinician often leads with expert recommendations instead of fully sharing decision-making power.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The clinician elicits and supports the patient's concrete request for medication and helps build a quit plan, which reinforces commitment. However, there are missed opportunities to ask open questions that would elicit and amplify additional change talk about importance, confidence, and past successes.

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Sustain talk about stress and social triggers is acknowledged and practical strategies are offered, reducing barriers. Still, MI-specific techniques (e.g., double-sided reflections) are rarely used, with the clinician quickly moving to solutions rather than gently reframing ambivalence.
